Diffie-Hellman & the Discrete Log Problem

RSA's security rests on factoring being hard. This lesson builds a second public-key scheme — older than RSA, actually — resting on a different hard problem: not factoring a number, but inverting exponentiation inside modular arithmetic. Diffie-Hellman doesn't encrypt anything by itself; it solves a narrower, equally fundamental problem: how can two people agree on a shared secret over a channel someone else is listening to?

The discrete logarithm problem

Fix a prime p and a base g. Given an exponent a, computing A = ga mod p is fast — square-and-multiply again, exactly as in every modular exponentiation this course has done. The discrete logarithm problem (DLP) is the reverse direction: given g, p, and A, find a. No known classical algorithm solves this in time polynomial in the number of digits of p — the best known classical attacks (index calculus and its refinements) are sub-exponential, in the same complexity family as GNFS for factoring, but they are a genuinely different algorithm attacking a genuinely different problem.

A different hardness assumption, not a restatement of factoring Factoring and the discrete log problem are believed hard for structurally different reasons, and there's no known way to convert a factoring oracle into a discrete-log solver or vice versa in general. That's precisely why Diffie-Hellman is worth learning as its own system rather than as "RSA again" — if one hardness assumption ever turned out to be wrong, the other might still stand.

Generators and primitive roots

Not every base g is equally useful. The powers g1, g2, g3, ... mod p cycle through some subset of {1, ..., p−1}; the size of that subset is g's order, exactly the same notion of order used for order-finding in lesson 3. When g's order is the largest possible, p − 1, g is called a primitive root (or generator) mod p — its powers hit every nonzero value mod p exactly once before repeating, which is what gives Diffie-Hellman the largest possible space of secrets to draw from.

Worked check: is g=5 a primitive root mod p=23? Its order must divide p−1=22, so the candidates are 1, 2, 11, 22. Direct computation gives 51=5, 52=25 mod 23=2, 511 mod 23=22, and only at 522 mod 23 does the result first equal 1 — so the order is 22, confirming 5 is a primitive root mod 23. That's exactly the (p, g) pair the protocol below uses.

The protocol

Alice and Bob want a shared secret, over a channel an eavesdropper (call her Eve) can read every message on but not alter (that limitation matters — see the callout below).

  1. Alice and Bob publicly agree on a prime p and a primitive root g. (These aren't secret — Eve knows them too.)
  2. Alice picks a secret random a, computes A = ga mod p, and sends A to Bob.
  3. Bob picks a secret random b, computes B = gb mod p, and sends B to Alice.
  4. Alice computes Ba mod p. Bob computes Ab mod p. Both equal gab mod p — the shared secret.

Why do Alice's and Bob's computations agree? Ba mod p = (gb)a mod p = gba mod p, and Ab mod p = (ga)b mod p = gab mod p — and ba = ab, ordinary integer multiplication commutes, so the two paths land on the identical value. Neither a nor b, the actual secrets, is ever transmitted — only A and B, the public exponentials, cross the wire.

Worked example: p = 23, g = 5 Alice picks a = 6, computes A = 56 mod 23 = 8, sends 8. Bob picks b = 15, computes B = 515 mod 23 = 19, sends 19. 
Alice computes: B^a mod p = 19^6 mod 23  = 2
Bob computes:   A^b mod p = 8^15 mod 23  = 2
Both land on 2 — the shared secret — without either ever seeing the other's private exponent. Eve, watching the whole exchange, sees p=23, g=5, A=8, B=19, and needs to solve the discrete log problem (recover a from A, or b from B) to catch up. For p=23 that's a five-minute brute-force search; for a real 2048-bit prime, it's exactly as infeasible as factoring a 2048-bit RSA modulus.
What Diffie-Hellman alone doesn't give you Nothing in the protocol above authenticates who Alice and Bob actually are — an active attacker who can intercept and replace messages (not just read them) can run the whole protocol separately with each party, ending up with two different shared secrets and relaying traffic between them undetected: a man-in-the-middle attack. Real deployments (like TLS) combine Diffie-Hellman with digital signatures for authentication — the subject of the still-locked lesson 5.

Diffie-Hellman and quantum computers

The discrete log problem, like factoring, has period-finding structure a quantum computer can exploit: an appropriately generalized version of the order-finding subroutine from lesson 3 — still built from phase estimation and the QFT — solves the discrete log problem in polynomial time too. So a quantum computer capable of running Shor's factoring algorithm threatens Diffie-Hellman on the same timeline it threatens RSA, via a related but distinct application of the same underlying technique, not by "breaking encryption in general." Lesson 7 covers what's believed to survive that threat.
