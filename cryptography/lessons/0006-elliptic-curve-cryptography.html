Elliptic Curve Cryptography: Same Ideas, Smaller Keys

Diffie-Hellman and DSA both ran inside one structure: the nonzero numbers mod a prime, multiplied together (DSA in a prime-order subgroup of them). Neither protocol cares what those numbers are, only that the discrete log problem in them is hard. This lesson keeps the protocols and swaps in the points of an elliptic curve. Underneath, every step is still mod-p arithmetic and modular inverses; what changes is which attacks exist — and that shrinks a 3072-bit key to 256 bits.

What Diffie-Hellman actually needs

A group is a monoid in which every element has an inverse: a set G with an associative operation ∘, an identity e (g ∘ e = e ∘ g = g), and for every g some g⁻¹ with g ∘ g⁻¹ = g⁻¹ ∘ g = e. It is abelian if ∘ commutes, and a finite group is cyclic if repeating ∘ on one element, a generator, reaches everything. You know one already: {1, …, 22} under multiplication mod 23, identity 1, generator 5 — Diffie-Hellman’s primitive root.

Diffie-Hellman used exactly three facts about that group: ga is fast to compute (square-and-multiply), (ga)b = (gb)a, and recovering a from ga is hard. (Strictly, Eve must get gab from ga and gb; no known method does that without a discrete log.) Any cyclic group with those three properties runs the protocol unchanged. Curve groups are written additively, so gk becomes kP:

Elliptic curves over Fp

For a prime p > 3, an elliptic curve over Fp (the integers mod p) in short Weierstrass form is the set of solutions of

y² ≡ x³ + ax + b  (mod p),   4a³ + 27b² ≢ 0  (mod p)

together with one extra point, O (Corbellini, part 2). The second condition says x³ + ax + b has no repeated root, so the curve has no cusps or self-crossings and tangents are well defined (Boneh & Shoup, §15.2). These are cubics, not ellipses; the name comes from elliptic integrals.

18 points plus O: a group of 19 elements. Hasse’s theorem keeps the count within 2√p of p + 1, and Schoof’s algorithm counts a cryptographic-size curve in polynomial time (Boneh & Shoup, §15.2).

The point at infinity O has no (x, y) coordinates and is in no list. It is a formal extra element, the identity: P + O = P for every P, and it is where a point and its mirror image add up to.

Adding points: chord, tangent, and a modular inverse

Over the reals the group law is a picture: the line through P and Q meets the curve in one more point; reflect it across the x-axis to get P + Q. Doubling uses the tangent at P; mirror images give a vertical line and sum to O (Corbellini, part 1, draws this and links an interactive tool). Over F17 there is no curve to draw, only scattered points, so the picture’s algebra becomes the definition, all mod p:

chord   (x₁ ≠ x₂):      λ = (y₂ − y₁) · (x₂ − x₁)⁻¹
tangent (P = Q, y₁ ≠ 0): λ = (3x₁² + a) · (2y₁)⁻¹
x₃ = λ² − x₁ − x₂
y₃ = λ(x₁ − x₃) − y₁
P + (−P) = O,  where −(x, y) = (x, −y)

Every “division” in λ is a modular inverse: curves run on the same number theory as RSA.

Worked example: 2P for P = (5, 1) 
numerator    3·5² + 2 = 77 ≡ 9        (77 − 68)
denominator  2·1 = 2,  2⁻¹ ≡ 9         (2·9 = 18 ≡ 1)
λ = 9 · 9 = 81 ≡ 13                    (81 − 68)
x₃ = 13² − 5 − 5 = 159 ≡ 6             (159 − 153)
y₃ = 13·(5 − 6) − 1 = −14 ≡ 3          (−14 + 17)
2P = (6, 3)
Check: 3² = 9 and 6³ + 12 + 2 = 230 ≡ 9. ✓ And −P = (5, 16), since −1 ≡ 16: the line through (5, 1) and (5, 16) is vertical, so P + (−P) = O, the curve’s a · a⁻¹ = 1.
What point addition is not Not coordinate-wise: P + P that way gives (10, 2), which is off the curve (2² = 4, but 1022 ≡ 2). Not the unreflected third point: that is (6, 14) = −2P. Associativity holds, but its proof is a long computation taken on trust, as Boneh & Shoup do (“it can be shown”).

Scalar multiplication: square-and-multiply, written additively

Adding P to itself k times gives kP. Lesson 1’s square-and-multiply, written additively, does it in at most 2 log₂ k point operations instead of k − 1: scan k’s bits left to right, double at every bit, add P on each 1-bit.

10 = 1010₂, start R = O
bit 1: R = 2·O + P = P   = (5, 1)
bit 0: R = 2·P     = 2P  = (6, 3)    λ = 13
bit 1: R = 2·2P    = 4P  = (3, 1)    λ = 7
       R = 4P + P  = 5P  = (9, 16)   λ = 0
bit 0: R = 2·5P    = 10P = (7, 11)   λ = 5

(4P: λ = (3·6² + 2)·6⁻¹ = 110·3 ≡ 8·3 = 24 ≡ 7. 5P: (3, 1) and (5, 1) share a y, so the chord is flat, λ = 0. 10P: λ = (3·9² + 2)·(2·16)⁻¹ = 245·15⁻¹ ≡ 7·8 = 56 ≡ 5.) For a 256-bit k: at most about 512 point operations.

Keep adding and you reach 19P = O, so P has order 19. It had to: an element’s order divides the group size (Lagrange, Corbellini part 2), 19 is prime, and P ≠ O — so every one of the 18 points is a generator. Real systems want the same shape: a base point G of large prime order n.

The elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem

The elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP): given the curve, P, and Q = kP, find k. Computing kP is fast; undoing it has no known shortcut, on a well-chosen curve, beyond the generic √n algorithms described below. Step through the multiples: they land all over the grid. The one visible pattern is the mirror, since (19 − k)P = −kP; beyond that, where kP sits gives no hint of k.

Grey: the 18 affine points. Green: multiples visited so far, labelled by k. Dashed: the mirror line y = 8.5 — every (x, y) has a partner (x, 17 − y), and the partner of kP is (19 − k)P.

ECDH: Diffie-Hellman, point by point

Public: the curve and P. Alice picks a = 3 and sends A = 3P = (10, 6). Bob picks b = 10 and sends B = 10P = (7, 11). Both then compute (ab)P, since a(bP) = b(aP): scalars commute as exponents did. With 19P = O, 30P = 11P = (13, 10).

Worked example: Alice computes 3B = 2B + B, with B = (7, 11)

      ≡ 13·5⁻¹ ≡ 13·7 = 91 ≡ 6
    x = 6² − 7 − 7 = 22 ≡ 5

Bob’s 10·A lands there too (your practice task). Eve, seeing P, A and B, needs (ab)P, and the only known route is an ECDLP: trivial with 19 points, hopeless with 2256. The man-in-the-middle weakness carries over, as does its fix: sign the exchange.

Deployed ECDH uses vetted curves over primes of about 256 bits, such as Curve25519 over 2255 − 19 (RFC 7748, X25519). ECDSA is lesson 5’s DSA with gk mod p replaced by the point kG and r taken from its x-coordinate mod n (Corbellini, part 3) — nonce reuse included.

Why 256 bits matches 3072 — and why Shor does not care

Key size is set by the best known attack. Factoring and discrete logs mod p face sub-exponential attacks (GNFS, index calculus), so moduli run to thousands of bits. No such attack is known on a well-chosen curve (Roetteler et al., §1): the ECDLP is not the mod-p problem in disguise. The best known attacks are generic — baby-step giant-step, Pollard’s rho — at about √n steps for prime order n (Corbellini, part 4; Boneh & Shoup §15.3). Shoup proved that no classical black-box algorithm, one that only calls the group operation, does much better than √n (Boneh & Shoup §16.3). Beating it means exploiting a curve’s internals, which nobody has managed on a well-chosen curve. Believed hard, not proven hard, like factoring.

A 256-bit order thus costs √(2256) = 2128 steps. NIST’s SP 800-57 Part 1 Rev. 5, Table 2:

security 128 bitsRSA modulus 3072mod-p DH 3072ECC order 256–383

As of 2026, Rev. 5 (2020) is still final and the December 2025 Rev. 6 draft keeps this row (as its Table 4). It holds only for well-chosen curves: an order with only small prime factors splits into easy small logs, a curve with exactly p points falls in polynomial time, and others reduce to an easier finite-field log — hence a few vetted curves (Boneh & Shoup §15.3).

What smaller keys do not buy you Shor’s discrete-log algorithm generalises to curves: the same period-finding via phase estimation, with point addition as the group operation (circuits: Quantum Computing’s Shor lesson). Roetteler et al. (2017) count at most 9n + 2⌈log₂ n⌉ + 10 logical qubits: 2,330 for P-256, against 6,146 for RSA-3072. The figures keep falling. By 2026 a 256-bit curve was down to about 1,200 logical qubits: 1,193 in Chevignard et al. (Eurocrypt 2026), and under 1,200 with under 90 million Toffoli gates in Babbush et al. (Google Quantum AI with co-authors, March 2026; backed by a zero-knowledge proof rather than published circuits). Chevignard et al. set theirs against Gidney’s 2025 estimate of 2,043 for RSA-3072. All of these counts are polynomial in n. ECC is a different classical assumption, not a quantum-safe one; as of September 2026 about 70% of browser traffic reaching Cloudflare already pairs X25519 with lattice-based ML-KEM (Cloudflare). Practice before next lesson

By hand, on y² = x³ + 2x + 2 over F17:

  1. Compute 3P = 2P + P with the chord rule, using 2P = (6, 3) and P = (5, 1). You should get λ = (1 − 3)·(5 − 6)⁻¹ = 2 and 3P = (10, 6) — Alice’s public point.
  2. Play Bob: compute 10·A for A = (10, 6) by double-and-add (10 = 1010₂). You should pass through 2A = (16, 13) with λ = 11, 4A = (0, 11) with λ = 10, 5A = (3, 16) with λ = 8, and finish at 10A = (13, 10) with λ = 11 — the same shared point Alice found. In the widget: 6P, 12P, 15P, 11P.
