Post-Quantum Cryptography: Lattices and the Next Hardness Assumption

Lesson 4 ended on a promise: Shor's algorithm threatens Diffie-Hellman on the same timeline as RSA, and a later lesson would say what survives. This is that lesson. Lesson 6 added elliptic curves to the casualty list. This lesson sorts every scheme in the course into broken, weakened or believed safe. Then it builds the hardness assumption behind NIST's new standards, Learning With Errors, and runs it by hand mod 17. At that size you can see exactly where the security comes from: a little deliberate noise.

What Shor breaks, and what Grover only weakens

Shor's quantum core, phase estimation, finds a hidden period of a function on an abelian (commutative) group. Lesson 3's order-finding is one such period. The discrete log is another, in two variables. Take lesson 4's p = 23, g = 5 and A = 8 = 56. The function f(x, y) = 5x · 8y mod 23 is unchanged when (x, y) shifts by (6, −1), because 56 · 8−1 ≡ 8 · 3 ≡ 1. For instance, f(6, 0) = 8 = f(0, 1). The secret a = 6 is the hidden shift. The elliptic-curve version lives in the curve's group. In Regev's words, "almost all known quantum algorithms which run super-polynomially faster than classical algorithms solve special cases of the HSP [hidden subgroup problem] on Abelian groups" (Regev). So RSA, Diffie-Hellman, DSA, ECDH and ECDSA all fall. NIST's IR 8105 (Table 1) marks RSA, ECDSA, ECDH and DSA "No longer secure," and its text extends that to every scheme resting on factoring or the discrete log.

Symmetric ciphers and hashes have no such structure. The best known quantum attack on them is generic search, and for that Grover's √N speedup is provably optimal. IR 8105 says AES needs "Larger key sizes" and SHA-2/SHA-3 need "Larger output," and that "doubling the key size will be sufficient." (Lesson 8 works out the hash numbers.) Post-quantum cryptography replaces the public-key half. In IR 8105's words, it means systems "secure against both quantum and classical computers" that "can interoperate with existing communications protocols and networks." It is ordinary software resting on problems with no known quantum shortcut. It is not quantum key distribution.

Harvest now, decrypt later An adversary can record today's (EC)DH handshakes and the traffic they protect. Once a large quantum computer exists, they run Shor on the recorded public values and recover the session keys, years after the sessions ended. NIST calls this "harvest now, decrypt later." Any secret that must outlive the wait for that machine is already exposed, so key exchange has to migrate first.

Lattices: one grid, many bases

A lattice is the set of all integer combinations of some linearly independent basis vectors: {x1b1 + ⋯ + xnbn : xi ∈ ℤ}. It is a grid that repeats forever, and a group under addition. The same lattice has infinitely many bases, and they are not equally useful.

Worked example: two bases, one lattice 
good: b₁ = (3, 1)    b₂ = (1, 3)
bad:  c₁ = (13, 7)   c₂ = (10, 6)
det:  9 − 1 = 8 = 78 − 70

c₁ = 4b₁ + b₂    b₁ = c₁ − c₂
c₂ = 3b₁ + b₂    b₂ = −3c₁ + 4c₂
Each basis builds the other with integer coefficients, so the two reach exactly the same points. The equal determinants (the area of one grid cell) are a quick sanity check.

The Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) asks: given a basis, find a shortest nonzero lattice vector. Here the answer is (2, −2) = b1 − b2 = 4c1 − 5c2, of length √8 ≈ 2.83, shorter than either good basis vector (√10). You can check this by hand. The point x·b1 + y·b2 = (3x + y, x + 3y) has squared length 8(x + y)2 + 2(x − y)2, which is at least 8 for every nonzero (x, y) and equals 8 only at x = −y = ±1. From the bad basis you would have to stumble on 4 and −5. In 2D that is a puzzle. In the hundreds of dimensions cryptography uses, lattice reduction (LLL) only gets within an exponential factor of the shortest vector. At the polynomial factors cryptography relies on, the best known algorithms, classical or quantum, take time exponential in the dimension (Regev's LWE survey, §2).

Learning With Errors: linear algebra plus a little noise

Learning With Errors (LWE) works like this. Fix a modulus q and a secret vector s. You are given random vectors ai together with bi = ⟨ai, s⟩ + ei (mod q), where each error ei is small. Recover s. The rest of the lesson uses this toy instance:

q = 17     secret s = (3, 5)
i  aᵢ       ⟨aᵢ,s⟩ mod 17    eᵢ  bᵢ
1  (2, 7)   6+35=41 ≡ 7      +1   8
2  (11, 4)  33+20=53 ≡ 2     −1   1
3  (5, 13)  15+65=80 ≡ 12     0  12
4  (9, 1)   27+5=32 ≡ 15     +1  16

public:  the aᵢ, b = (8, 1, 12, 16)
private: s (and e)

Without the ei, Gaussian elimination would solve this from any two rows. With them, it fails:

rows 1, 4 without noise:
  2x + 7y ≡ 7, 9x + y ≡ 15
  → (3, 5) ✓
rows 1, 4 with noise:
  2x + 7y ≡ 8, 9x + y ≡ 16
  → (7, 4) ✗
check (7, 4) on rows 2 and 3:
  11·7 + 4·4 ≡ 8,  but b₂ = 1
  5·7 + 13·4 ≡ 2,  but b₃ = 12

Errors of ±1 in the inputs produced an answer that misses the other rows by 7, nearly the most anything mod 17 can miss by (8). Elimination combines rows, and that combines and multiplies their errors too: "thereby amplifying the error to unmanageable levels, leaving essentially no information in the resulting equations" (survey, §1). The noise is not an implementation nuisance; it is the hardness. Two coordinates are still easy, though. Try all 172 = 289 candidates and keep the one whose errors all lie within ±1, and only (3, 5) survives. Real LWE has hundreds of coordinates, so the list has qn entries. LWE is also a lattice problem in disguise. The noiseless vectors A·s, plus multiples of q, form a lattice, and b lies a short distance e off it. Recovering s means finding the nearest lattice point: "a random bounded distance decoding problem on lattices."

Regev's encryption: hiding one bit in the noise

Regev turned LWE into a public-key cryptosystem in exactly lesson 2's sense (Regev 2005). The private key is s, and the public key is the samples (ai, bi). Each bit β is encrypted on its own, using ⌊q/2⌋ = 8:

Encrypt β: random subset r of rows
  u = Σ rᵢ·aᵢ         (mod q)
  v = Σ rᵢ·bᵢ + β·8   (mod q)
Decrypt (u, v):
  d = v − ⟨u, s⟩      (mod q)
  0 if d nearer 0 than 8, else 1

Since each bi = ⟨ai, s⟩ + ei, subtracting ⟨u, s⟩ cancels everything except d = Σ riei + β·8. A 0 lands near 0 and a 1 lands near 8, each blurred by the summed noise. Without s, an eavesdropper sees a subset-sum of rows that, under the LWE assumption, look uniformly random, so (u, v) reveals nothing about β (survey, §4).

Worked example: encrypt β = 1 with r = (1, 0, 1, 1) 
u = a₁ + a₃ + a₄
  = (2+5+9, 7+13+1)
  = (16, 21) ≡ (16, 4)
v = b₁ + b₃ + b₄ + 8
  = 8+12+16+8 = 44 ≡ 10
⟨u,s⟩ = 16·3 + 4·5 = 68 ≡ 0
d = 10 − 0 = 10
  2 from 8, 7 from 0  →  1 ✓
The 2 is the carried noise e1 + e3 + e4 = 1 + 0 + 1. Encrypting β = 0 with the same rows gives v = 36 ≡ 2 and d = 2, which decrypts to 0.

Correctness and security pull against each other. Here any noise sum from −3 to +3 decrypts correctly. A sum of +4 puts an encrypted 0 at d = 4, exactly halfway, where "else 1" gets it wrong. In general, errors occur "only if the sum of the error terms … is greater than q/4" (survey, §4). Now multiply e by 4 to make s harder to learn. Then b = (11, 15, 12, 2), and the same encryption of 0 gives v = 11 + 12 + 2 = 25 ≡ 8, so d = 8 and it decrypts as 1. Real parameters make q large enough that the summed noise essentially never reaches q/4, while each ei still buries s.

Why LWE is believed hard, stated honestly

  
Lesson 3 called factoring "believed hard, not proven hard", and LWE gets the same label. Three things back it.

  
A worst-case to average-case reduction. Key generation produces random LWE instances. Regev proved that solving those efficiently would solve approximate GapSVP and SIVP (shortest-vector-type problems) on every lattice, including the hardest ones. As his abstract puts it, "Our reduction, however, is quantum. Hence, an efficient solution to the learning problem implies a quantum algorithm for GapSVP and SIVP" (Regev). Breaking a random key means beating worst-case lattice problems, not finding an easy instance.

  
No hidden period to find. A lattice is an abelian group, but its period is not hidden: you are handed a basis. The survey's rule of thumb is that “‘algebraic’ questions regarding lattices are easy,” while “Geometric questions are typically hard.” Short is geometric. The closest known quantum route goes through the hidden subgroup problem on the non-abelian dihedral group. A suitable algorithm for it would solve a version of SVP (Regev), but the best known one, Kuperberg's, is subexponential, not polynomial (Kuperberg). For where quantum speedups stop in general, see Quantum Computing lesson 8.

  
Cryptanalysis so far. Peikert's survey credits lattice cryptography with "apparent resistance to quantum attacks (in contrast with most number-theoretic cryptography)." Apparent is the honest word.

  

    What this lesson is not claiming
    It does not claim lattice crypto is NP-hard. GapSVP is NP-hard only for small approximation factors, and at cryptography's polynomial factors it "is believed not to be NP-hard, but is nevertheless believed to be hard" (survey, §2). It does not claim the reduction proves LWE hard: it ties LWE to worst-case problems that are themselves only assumed hard. And it does not claim that surviving a competition makes an assumption safe. SIKE, an isogeny scheme that had just reached NIST's fourth round, fell in 2022 to a classical attack that broke its level-1 parameters "in about ten minutes on a single core" (Castryck & Decru).
  

  
So the field hedges. In March 2025 NIST chose HQC, a code-based scheme, as a backup KEM because it "is based on different math than ML-KEM, which could be important if a weakness were discovered in ML-KEM" (NIST). Deployments also run old and new side by side. RFC 10024 (August 2026) defines X25519MLKEM768 for TLS 1.3, which pairs an ECDH share with an ML-KEM share. The pair provides "security as long as at least one of the component algorithms remains secure."


  
The standards, as of 2026

  
NIST finalized three standards in August 2024:

  
    
    
  • ML-KEM (FIPS 203, from Kyber) is key encapsulation, replacing (EC)DH. In the standard's words, "The security of ML-KEM is related to the computational difficulty of the Module Learning with Errors problem."
    • 
    
  • ML-DSA (FIPS 204, from Dilithium) gives module-lattice signatures, replacing RSA and ECDSA signatures.
    • 
    
  • SLH-DSA (FIPS 205, from SPHINCS+) gives signatures built only from a hash function, a backup on different math. Lesson 8 shows how.
    • 
  

  
"Module" means LWE whose entries are small polynomials instead of single numbers: the same noisy equations, packed into blocks for efficiency. The plain scheme above is "quite inefficient" (survey, §4), while an ML-KEM-768 public key is 1,184 bytes (RFC 10024 §4.1). NIST's project page (updated August 2026) says Falcon (future FN-DSA, a lattice signature) and HQC were "selected for ongoing standardization; that process is underway." Neither is final yet.

  

    The sentence this course was aiming at
    ML-KEM rests on Module-LWE: recovering a secret from noisy linear equations mod q. Regev's reduction ties that problem to approximate shortest-vector problems on lattices, and it has no hidden abelian period for Shor to find. It is not factoring, and it is not a discrete log.

Practice before next lesson

  
By hand, encrypt β = 1 with r = (1, 1, 0, 1), then decrypt. You should get u = (5, 12), v = 16, ⟨u, s⟩ = 75 ≡ 7 and d = 9, which decrypts to 1. Redo it with the ×4 public key b = (11, 15, 12, 2). This time v = 36 ≡ 2 and d = 2 − 7 ≡ 12, still a 1. Then explain why this subset survives ×4 noise when r = (1, 0, 1, 1) does not. (Its noise sum is 4 · (1 − 1 + 1) = 4, not 8. That is only just enough: encrypting β = 0 with the same rows lands on d = 4, the halfway tie, and decrypts wrongly.)


  
Primary resource

  
See Oded Regev, "The Learning with Errors Problem" (PDF), a short survey by LWE's inventor. It opens with a mod-17 system of approximate equations and explains why Gaussian elimination amplifies the error. §2 states the worst-case hardness results for GapSVP and SIVP. §4 gives the exact one-bit scheme you ran above, with the q/4 bound and the security sketch.
