# Mission: Emerging AI Research

## Why
Complements a parallel course on AI Engineering — where that course is the practical/systems side of working with agents, this one is the research/theory side: understanding what's actually being tried beyond next-token-prediction LLMs, well enough to read a real current paper and form a genuine, defensible opinion, not repeat a headline or a hot take.

## Success looks like
- Explain what a "world model" is and how JEPA-style self-supervised architectures differ from autoregressive LLMs, precisely — not just "it's different"
- Read one real, current paper on a non-LLM architecture end to end and summarize its actual claim and evidence, distinct from the abstract's marketing framing
- Explain at least two other live research directions (e.g. mechanistic interpretability, the scaling-laws debate) accurately enough to state what's established vs. genuinely open
- Apply a consistent rigor-over-rhetoric standard to new AI claims — distinguishing real architectural/theoretical advances from hype, the same discipline used for Gödel-misuse in Logic and superposition-misuse in Quantum Mechanics

## Constraints
- Strong CS/ML-adjacent background; comfortable with linear algebra and probability (already covered in the user's Category Theory / Probability courses)
- Self-study with an AI teacher; assumes rough familiarity with how current transformer-based LLMs work (pairs naturally with the AI Engineering course, but doesn't require finishing it first)

## Out of scope
- Training or reproducing any of these architectures hands-on — this is a reading/understanding course, not a research-lab course
- A full deep-learning-fundamentals prerequisite curriculum — assumes the reader already knows roughly how transformers work or can pick that up quickly with the teacher's help
- Picking a winner among competing research directions — presenting the live, unresolved debate accurately is the goal, same standard as Quantum Mechanics' interpretation debate
