Beyond Next-Token Prediction: What a "World Model" Actually Means

You already have a rough sense of how a large language model is trained: feed it enormous amounts of text, and at every position ask it to predict the next token, scored against how well that prediction matched what actually came next. That single objective — cheap to compute, trivially scalable, no human labeling required — is the engine behind essentially every frontier LLM. This lesson asks a sharper question than "is that enough?": what would it even mean for a model to have more than that — a "world model" — and is that phrase doing real work or just borrowing gravitas the way "quantum" gets borrowed outside physics?

What next-token prediction actually optimizes

Precisely: an autoregressive LLM is trained to minimize cross-entropy loss between its predicted probability distribution over the next token and the actual next token, at every position in a training corpus, conditioned only on the tokens that came before. Nothing in that objective directly rewards the model for representing "the state of the world" — no notion of objects persisting, actions having consequences, or physical dynamics unfolding over time is written into the loss function anywhere. Whatever structure like that ends up inside the model, if any, has to emerge as a side effect of getting very good at predicting text — it isn't the thing being optimized for.

That gap — between "optimizes token prediction" and "represents the world" — is the entire subject of this lesson.

A precise definition of "world model"

The modern technical use of the term comes from reinforcement learning, most influentially from David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber's 2018 paper, which trains an agent's internal model to do two specific things: (1) compress raw observations into a compact latent representation, and (2) predict how that latent representation changes over time, typically conditioned on an action the agent takes. That gives a much sharper bar than "the model knows stuff about the world":

World model (working definition) A learned internal representation of an environment's state, together with a learned model of how that state changes — ideally in response to actions — good enough to predict future states and support planning, without needing to re-observe the real environment for every step.

Notice what this definition requires that plain next-token prediction doesn't provide for free: a notion of state distinct from the raw input stream, and a notion of dynamics — how state transitions — rather than just "what token is statistically likely to follow this text." A model can be extremely good at the second (next-token prediction) while it remains a genuinely open question whether, and to what extent, it has built the first (state + dynamics) as a byproduct.

Does an LLM build a world model? The honest answer is: sometimes, partially, and it's contested

This is the section to read slowly, because the popular discourse tends to collapse into one of two overconfident answers ("obviously yes, look what it can do" or "obviously no, it's just autocomplete"), and neither survives contact with the actual evidence.

Evidence for implicit structure: Kenneth Li and colleagues trained a small GPT-style model on nothing but sequences of legal moves in the board game Othello — the model was never told the rules or shown a board. Trained purely to predict the next move, the network turned out to contain an internal, decodable representation of the actual board state, and intervening on that internal representation causally changed the model's predicted moves in the expected way. That's a genuine, controlled result: next-token prediction, on a narrow synthetic task, produced something that behaves like a state representation — a real, small-scale existence proof that the gap between "predicts tokens" and "represents state" can be bridged by training alone, at least sometimes.

Evidence for limits: Lukas Berglund and colleagues showed what they called the "reversal curse": a model trained on "A is B" (e.g., a specific person described one way) reliably fails to answer the reverse question "who is B?", even though a model with a genuine, queryable internal representation of the underlying fact ought to answer both directions equally well. That's evidence the internal representations LLMs build are, at least in many cases, tied tightly to the specific direction and phrasing they were trained on — more like a huge web of directional associations than a clean, queryable model of "who is who" that you could interrogate from any angle.

The skeptical position, stated fairly: Emily Bender and colleagues' widely-cited "stochastic parrots" paper predates both of the above results, but makes the structural point that still has to be answered by anyone claiming an LLM "understands" or "models" anything: a system trained purely to match the statistical distribution of surface text form can produce fluent, contextually appropriate output without that output being backed by any grounded representation of meaning or reference at all. The paper isn't a technical proof that LLMs lack world models — it's a warning against inferring understanding from fluency alone, which is exactly the inferential move both boosters and casual critics keep making in different directions.

Hold these together, not apart Othello-GPT shows narrow, task-specific state representations can emerge from pure next-token prediction. The reversal curse shows those representations are often shallower and more direction-dependent than "a world model" implies. Stochastic Parrots explains why fluent output is not, by itself, evidence of either. None of these three results contradicts the others — they narrow the honest answer down to "partially, unevenly, and it depends enormously on what you're testing," which is a much more useful thing to have learned than either slogan.

Before you say an LLM "has a world model"

Same discipline as Logic lesson 11's Gödel-misuse check and Quantum Mechanics' superposition-misuse check: whenever "world model" gets applied to a specific system, ask precisely which claim is being made before accepting or rejecting it.

"LLMs are just stochastic parrots, they can't model anything" — Overclaim in the other direction. Othello-GPT is a real, controlled counterexample to the strong version of this claim on at least one narrow task. The accurate claim is narrower: fluent output alone isn't evidence of a world model, not that no world-model-like structure can ever emerge from next-token prediction.
"GPT-4 clearly has a world model, look how well it reasons about physics questions" — Overclaim. Good performance on physics-flavored text questions is evidence about output quality on that distribution of questions, not direct evidence of a persistent internal state-plus-dynamics representation in the technical sense above. The reversal curse is exactly the kind of result that should make you suspicious of inferring a clean internal model from surface competence.
"Whether LLMs have world models is settled" — Overclaim regardless of which side it's asserting. As of this course, it's an active empirical research question studied case by case (which capability, which model, which probing method) — not a single yes/no fact. Treat any confident one-line answer, in either direction, as a sign the claim hasn't been checked against a specific result.
