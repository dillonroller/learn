JEPA & Self-Supervised Representation Learning

Lesson 1 defined a world model as a state representation plus a model of how that state changes — and left open whether next-token prediction reliably builds one. The Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) is a direct, deliberate attempt to build one by architectural design instead of hoping it emerges. This lesson explains exactly what JEPA does differently from a generative model, and states plainly what's demonstrated versus what's still a bet.

Self-supervised learning, briefly

Self-supervised learning (SSL) trains on unlabeled data by manufacturing a supervisory signal from the data itself — no human annotation required. Next-token prediction is one instance of SSL: the "label" for each position is just the next token, which the corpus already provides for free. Masked-image modeling is another: hide patches of an image and train the model to reconstruct them from the visible ones. What all of these share is a pretext task — a training objective chosen not because anyone cares about it directly, but because doing well at it forces the model to learn useful internal representations along the way.

Two ways to build a pretext task: reconstruct, or predict in embedding space

Both next-token prediction and masked-image reconstruction share a structural choice: the target the model is scored against lives in input space — actual pixels, actual tokens. JEPA makes a different structural choice. Its pretext task is: given a context (part of an input), predict the representation of a target (another part of the same input) — not the target's raw pixels or tokens, but the embedding a separate encoder produces from them.

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) An architecture that learns by predicting the latent embedding of a target from the latent embedding of a context, rather than by reconstructing the target's raw input values. "Joint-embedding" because both context and target pass through encoders into the same representation space; "predictive" because a separate predictor network is trained to map context embeddings to target embeddings.

Yann LeCun's original argument for this choice, in his 2022 position paper, is about where model capacity gets spent. A pixel-reconstruction model is forced to predict every last unpredictable detail of the target — the exact texture of grass, the exact position of leaves on a tree — because the loss penalizes any pixel-level mismatch, predictable or not. A model predicting in embedding space can let the target encoder discard that irrelevant, unpredictable detail up front, so the predictor only has to get the parts that actually are predictable — structure, layout, semantics — right. The claim is capacity efficiency: less of the model's effort goes toward modeling noise it was never going to predict anyway.

The architecture, piece by piece

A JEPA system has three trained pieces working together on a single input (an image, a video clip, and so on):

  • Context encoder — encodes the visible/context portion of the input into an embedding.
  • Target encoder — encodes the target portion (the part being predicted) into an embedding. This is usually not trained directly by backpropagation; instead it's updated as a slowly-moving exponential moving average (EMA) of the context encoder's weights, and gradients are stopped from flowing into it.
  • Predictor — a smaller network trained to map the context embedding to a prediction of the target embedding. The loss is a distance (e.g. squared error) between the predictor's output and the target encoder's actual output — entirely in embedding space, never touching raw pixels.
Why the stop-gradient and EMA target encoder matter Predicting in embedding space creates a failure mode reconstruction-based methods don't have to worry about as much: representation collapse, where the easiest way to drive the prediction loss to zero is for every encoder to output the same constant vector regardless of input — a trivially "perfect," and completely useless, solution. Making the target encoder a stop-gradient EMA copy of the context encoder (rather than trained jointly by the same loss) is one of the standard tricks — shared with earlier non-contrastive SSL work like BYOL — that keeps this collapse from happening in practice, though avoiding collapse remains an active area of architectural and regularization work rather than a single fully solved problem.

From position paper to working system: I-JEPA and V-JEPA

The 2022 position paper laid out the JEPA idea as a proposal. Mahmoud Assran and colleagues then built and benchmarked a concrete instance for images, I-JEPA: given one context block of an image, predict the embeddings of several separate target blocks in the same image, using a Vision Transformer for all three pieces. Trained this way, I-JEPA's representations performed competitively with — and in some settings better than — prior pixel-reconstruction and augmentation-heavy contrastive methods on standard benchmarks like linear-probe ImageNet classification, while being reported as substantially cheaper to train. Meta's follow-on V-JEPA line extends the same embedding-prediction idea to video, and the 2025 V-JEPA 2 work goes further still, using the learned representation to support robot action planning — a step toward the "predict state, then plan with it" picture from lesson 1's definition, now demonstrated on a real robotic arm rather than only as a proposal.

The bet this architecture is making

Strip away the engineering and JEPA is a specific, falsifiable research bet: that architectures which predict abstract representations of the world — built through self-supervised learning, organized hierarchically, and eventually used for planning rather than only for one-step prediction — are a necessary ingredient for reaching more sample-efficient, common-sense, animal-like intelligence, and that pixel/token-level generative prediction alone is not sufficient to get there. LeCun's position paper is explicit that this includes a specific critique of pure autoregressive generation for long-horizon tasks: because each generated token can carry some error, and errors compound across a long generated sequence, autoregressive generation is argued to be an unreliable way to plan or reason over long horizons without some additional mechanism.

What's demonstrated vs. what's still a bet Demonstrated: JEPA-style embedding prediction is a working, benchmarkable technique — I-JEPA and V-JEPA report real, reproducible results against real baselines, and V-JEPA 2 demonstrates the representation transferring to a genuine robotic planning task. Still a bet: that this specific architectural family is necessary for reaching more general, sample-efficient intelligence, and that scaled-up autoregressive LLMs specifically cannot get there through some other route (larger context, tool use, chain-of-thought, hybrid architectures, and so on). That second claim is the position paper's central thesis — a serious, well-argued research hypothesis from a highly credentialed researcher, not yet an empirically settled result the way backpropagation or attention are settled techniques.

Before repeating "next-token prediction can't reason" or "JEPA solves world models"

Same discipline as lesson 1's misuse check, applied to architecture claims specifically.

"LLMs just predict the next token, so they fundamentally can't reason" — Overclaim as a one-line dismissal. It skips over real, specific, testable phenomena — chain-of-thought prompting, in-context learning, tool use — that any serious version of this critique has to engage with directly rather than wave away. The defensible version of this critique is LeCun's actual, narrower argument about error compounding over long autoregressive horizons specifically, not a blanket "can't reason" claim.
"JEPA has solved the world model problem" — Overclaim. I-JEPA and V-JEPA are real, working systems with real benchmark results — that's demonstrated. Whether embedding-space self-supervised prediction is the architecture that gets AI research to robust, general world models is the open bet stated above, not a closed question.
"JEPA and LLMs are direct competitors, pick one" — Overclaim in a different direction. Nothing about JEPA rules out combining representation-prediction ideas with large transformer-based language systems, and active research explores exactly that kind of hybrid; treating the two as a forced binary is a simplification the source material doesn't require.
