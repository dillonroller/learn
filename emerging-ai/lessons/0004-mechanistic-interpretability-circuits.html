Mechanistic Interpretability: Peeking Inside the Black Box

Lesson 1 left you with a narrow existence proof: Othello-GPT carried a decodable board state, and editing it changed the moves. The next-token loss fixes what a network must output, never which algorithm produces it; mechanistic interpretability asks about the algorithm. This lesson gives you the field’s vocabulary, four results with the scale each holds at, and an evidence ladder for placing any headline of the form “researchers found the X feature in model Z.” Lesson 3’s split between the checkable and the interpretive does most of the work.

What the field is trying to do

Mechanistic interpretability (working definition) Reverse-engineering a trained network’s computation into human-understandable features (what is represented) and circuits (how representations are combined), then testing that those parts actually carry the computation rather than merely correlate with it.

The 29-author review Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability (Sharkey et al., 2025) gives the motive as science plus “greater assurance over AI system behavior.” Chris Olah and colleagues’ Zoom In (Distill, 2020) stated the program as three claims they call “deliberately speculative”:

Features, circuits & universality (the three speculative claims)
  1. Features are the fundamental unit of a network, and they correspond to directions: linear combinations of neurons in a layer.
  2. Circuits: features are connected by weights into subgraphs that can be rigorously studied.
  3. Universality: analogous features and circuits form across models and tasks.
These are working hypotheses, not results. Note that claim 1 makes the unit a direction, not a neuron.

In a transformer, the residual stream after layer n “is the sum of the embedding and the outputs of all layers up to layer n” (Nanda’s glossary). In Elhage et al.’s Mathematical Framework (2021), heads and MLPs “communicate with each other by reading and writing to different subspaces of the residual stream.” Because writes are additive, you can ask what one component contributed; the methods below exploit that linearity.

What it has actually found, and at what scale

Induction heads complete [A][B] … [A] → [B]: attend to the token that followed an earlier copy of the current token, then raise its logit. Catherine Olsson, Nelson Elhage, Neel Nanda and colleagues (2022) found they form in a narrow early-training window, “visible as a bump in the training loss,” exactly when in-context learning jumps. Their self-grading is the model to copy: “strong, causal evidence” for small attention-only models; for large models with MLPs (up to 13B parameters) the claim that induction heads do most in-context learning is rated “Medium, Correlational,” because “we do not have ablations for our full-scale models.” Whether such a jump counts as “emergence” is lesson 5’s question.

  • IOI in GPT-2 small. Kevin Wang et al. (2022) explained how “When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to” gets “Mary”: 26 heads in 7 classes, of the model’s 144, found by path patching and knockouts and scored on faithfulness, completeness and minimality. It improves on a naive circuit “but fails to pass the most challenging tests,” and backup heads take over when the main ones are ablated.
  • Modular addition. Nanda et al. (2023) fully reverse-engineered a one-layer transformer trained on addition mod 113: “discrete Fourier transforms and trigonometric identities” turn addition into rotation, confirmed by ablations in Fourier space. Complete, but a toy model on one task.
  • Othello-GPT made linear. Li et al. (2022) found a nonlinear board representation; Nanda, Andrew Lee and Martin Wattenberg (2023) showed it is linear in a “my colour” vs “opponent’s colour” basis and steerable “with simple vector arithmetic.” What a probe finds depends on the basis you ask about.

Polysemanticity and superposition: why neurons are the wrong unit

Zoom In’s InceptionV1 has a neuron that “responds to cat faces, fronts of cars, and cat legs”: it is polysemantic. Toy Models of Superposition (Elhage et al., 2022) explains why: models “represent more features than they have dimensions.” A d-dimensional space has d orthogonal directions but exponentially many almost-orthogonal ones. When features are sparse, “superposition allows compression … at the cost of ‘interference’ that requires nonlinear filtering,” and each neuron ends up carrying slices of several features.

Name collision: not quantum superposition This is a linear-algebra packing claim about real-valued activations, unrelated to quantum superposition. It passes that lesson’s misuse check because it has its own precise, checkable definition; it just shares a word. Its authors add that it is “very unclear what to generalize to real networks.”

Sparse autoencoders (dictionary learning) try to pull features back out: a much wider autoencoder reconstructs a layer’s activations from a sparse set of learned directions (L2 reconstruction plus an L1 penalty), and each direction is a candidate feature. Towards Monosemanticity (Bricken et al., 2023) expanded a one-layer model’s 512-neuron MLP into up to 131,072 features. Scaling Monosemanticity (Templeton et al., 2024) trained 1M, 4M and 34M-feature SAEs on the residual stream at Claude 3 Sonnet’s middle layer; clamping its Golden Gate Bridge feature to 10× its maximum made the model “self-identify as the Golden Gate Bridge.” Keep the layers apart: the label is a human reading of top-activating examples (interpretive); the steering result is checkable and causal.

Probing vs causal intervention: the evidence ladder

Each method licenses a different strength of claim. Put a headline on the highest rung its method reaches, then add the model scale.

  1. Rung 1 · DecodableProbe: a classifier predicts X from activations. Licenses: the information is present in a form this probe can read. Not: the model uses it. Belinkov’s review: probing “may indicate correlations … but it does not tell us whether this property is involved in predictions.”
  2. Rung 2 · Causally implicatedActivation patching (splice one component’s activation from a clean run into a corrupted run and measure how much of the correct-answer logit difference returns), ablation (zero or mean-replace it), steering (add or clamp a direction). Licenses: this component affects this behaviour on these inputs. Not: it is the only path or the whole story.
  3. Rung 3 · Mechanism explainedA circuit that passes faithfulness, completeness and minimality tests, or an algorithm read off the weights and confirmed by targeted ablation. Licenses: how this behaviour is computed in this model. Not: that it transfers to other models or scales. Modular addition reaches this rung; IOI comes close but, by its authors’ own account, fails the hardest completeness tests.

Othello-GPT reached rung 2 because Li et al. edited the representation and the moves changed. Rung 2 is method-sensitive: Zhang and Nanda showed that patching metrics and corruption choices “could lead to disparate interpretability results” (see Heimersheim and Nanda’s guide), and IOI’s backup heads mean an ablation can understate a component’s role. And locating is not editing: ROME (Meng et al.) used causal tracing to choose where to edit facts, but Hase et al. found localization results “do not provide any insight into which model MLP layer would be best to edit.”

What interpretability still can’t tell you

A flagship frontier-scale study, Lindsey et al.’s On the Biology of a Large Language Model (2025), traces Claude 3.5 Haiku answering “the capital of the state containing Dallas” through an internal Texas step; swapping in California features makes it answer Sacramento. That is rung 2 on a production model. The limits are just as specific, and most come from the papers’ own limitations sections:

  • The graphs give “satisfying insight for about a quarter of the prompts we’ve tried,” “only capture a small fraction of the mechanisms,” and run on a “replacement model” that “incompletely and imperfectly captures the original.”
  • Claude 3 Sonnet can list every London borough, yet the 34M SAE had features for only “about 60% of the boroughs” (Templeton et al.).
  • Across Pythia model sizes, SAEs on randomly initialized transformers often score about as well on auto-interpretability and reconstruction metrics as SAEs on trained ones, so those metrics alone cannot certify that real features were found (Heap et al., 2025).
  • Google DeepMind’s team found plain linear probes beat SAE probes on out-of-distribution harmful-intent detection and announced they were “deprioritising fundamental SAE research” (March 2025; see also Kantamneni et al.).
  • The streetlight worry: Nanda calls the charge that IOI is “an incredibly cherry-picked, toy problem” “a fairly legitimate criticism” (glossary).

As of 2026, public framing runs ahead of these numbers: MIT Technology Review listed mechanistic interpretability among its 10 Breakthrough Technologies of 2026, while noting “the field is split on how far you can go with these techniques.”

Hold these together, not apart IOI, modular addition, Golden Gate steering and the Texas→California swap are real causal results; a quarter of prompts, 60% of boroughs and the random-network baselines are equally real. Together: checkable mechanisms for selected behaviours, even in production models, and no evidence yet that they cover most of what a frontier model does. A growing set of narrow existence proofs, not a readable model.

Established: specific circuits in small models; superposition in toy models; SAE features that steer a production model. Unproven bet: that this scales to a usable account of frontier models; universality; that SAEs find the model’s own features, not dictionary artefacts. Marketing: below.

Before repeating an interpretability headline

Same discipline as lesson 3’s four questions. Q3 (checked outside the original team?) and Q4 (does the headline survive next to the limitations?) do most of the work: Heap et al. and DeepMind’s probe comparison are outside checks, and the “quarter of the prompts” line sits in the headline paper itself. Then name the method, the rung and the scale. Lesson 6’s claim audit generalizes this.

“Researchers can now read the AI’s mind” — Overclaim. The authors’ own figures: a quarter of prompts, a small fraction of mechanisms, an imperfect replacement model, about 60% of a list the model knows. A microscope with a narrow field of view, not a transcript.
“They found a deception feature, so the model is deceptive (or understands)” — Overclaim. The label is the interpretive layer, and Templeton et al. warn “there’s a difference … between knowing about lies, being capable of lying, and actually lying in the real world.” A mechanistic story answers functional “easy problem” questions only.
“Mech interp only works on toy models, so it’s useless” — Overclaim the other way. IOI is natural text in a real pretrained model; Golden Gate steering and the Texas→California swap are interventions on production models. The fair criticism is coverage and selection, not reality.
