Scaling Laws: What's Actually Established vs. Debated

“Just scale it up” is the stock answer to every open question this course has raised. It shows up as the wrong quiz answer in lesson 1 (“more scale will resolve it automatically”), and it is the thesis LeCun’s bet argues against. Scaling laws are the evidence behind that answer. They are real and unusually clean, but they describe one quantity: the next-token cross-entropy from lesson 1, measured on held-out text. The win for this lesson is to sort any scaling claim as established, revised or contested, or not in the laws at all, and to do the two-line power-law arithmetic that keeps you honest. Along the way, lesson 3’s four questions get applied to a famous paper.

What a scaling law says, exactly

Jared Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI trained Transformer language models across a wide range of sizes, datasets and compute budgets, with some trends spanning “more than seven orders of magnitude”. Test loss followed three power laws, each holding when the other two resources are not the bottleneck (Kaplan et al. 2020, eqs. 1.1–1.3):

L(N) ≈ (Nc/N)0.076  N = non-embedding parameters
L(D) ≈ (Dc/D)0.095  D = training tokens
L(C) ≈ (Cc/C)0.050  C = training compute
Scaling law (working definition) An empirical fit of the form L(X) = (Xc/X)α, sometimes plus a constant. It relates pretraining loss to parameters N, data D or compute C, for a fixed recipe and data distribution, and is valid within the range where it was measured. On log-log axes it is a straight line with slope −α.

Three consequences follow. The y-axis is loss (nats per token), not a capability score. Small exponents mean steep diminishing returns: ten times the parameters multiplies loss by 10−0.076 ≈ 0.84, only 16% lower; halving it would take 21/0.076 ≈ 9,100× the parameters, or 220 ≈ 106× the compute. The regularity is empirical, not derived. Joel Hestness and colleagues saw power laws in translation, language modelling, image processing and speech, with exponents “yet to be explained by theoretical work” (Hestness et al. 2017); Tom Henighan and colleagues found power law plus a constant for images, video, multimodal models and mathematics (Henighan et al. 2020). The laws are established because they replicate across domains and labs, not because anyone has proved they must hold.

The floor: irreducible loss

A pure power law falls toward zero, but real loss cannot. Kaplan’s own Section 6.3, “Contradictions and a Conjecture”, says: “Our trends must eventually level off, though, since natural language has non-zero entropy.” Henighan et al. split cross-entropy into S(True) + DKL(True‖Model). The first term is the Shannon entropy of the source, and no model can remove it. Only the KL term shrinks with scale. The Chinchilla paper (Hoffmann et al. 2022, eq. 2, fitted in Appendix D.2) gives

L(N, D) = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ  E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28
Irreducible loss (working definition) The constant E in a power-law-plus-constant fit: the loss that an ideal model of the data distribution would still incur, which Hoffmann et al. say “should correspond to the entropy of natural text”. Scale shrinks only the reducible part, L − E.

Plug Chinchilla itself into the fit (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) and you get L ≈ 1.94, of which 1.69 is floor, so only about 0.25 nats per token is left to remove. Under this fit, no amount of scale halves a loss of 2.0. Two cautions apply. E is itself fitted (the replication below re-estimates it at ≈ 1.82), and loss values depend on tokenizer and dataset, so Kaplan’s numbers cannot be compared directly with Chinchilla’s. These are empirical fits within a range, not laws of nature.

Compute-optimal training: a confident prescription, revised twice

Training costs roughly 6ND FLOPs, so a fixed budget forces a trade between model size and tokens. Kaplan’s fit gave Nopt ∝ C0.73, which means “training very large models on a relatively modest amount of data and stopping significantly before convergence”. Per 10× of compute, that is a model 5.5× bigger but only 1.8× more data, as Hoffmann et al. summarise it. The field followed: GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B) and Gopher (280B) were all trained on about 300B tokens (Chinchilla, Table 1).

Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues at DeepMind trained over 400 models (70M to over 16B parameters, 5B to 500B tokens) and estimated the optimum three independent ways: a = 0.50, 0.49 and 0.46, against Kaplan’s 0.73 (Table 2). Parameters and tokens should grow at the same rate, about 3.2× each per 10× of compute; Table 3 pairs 1B parameters with 20.2B tokens, the source of the “≈ 20 tokens per parameter” rule. Then they tested it. Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4T tokens) used the same compute as Gopher (280B, 300B) and “uniformly and significantly outperforms” it and other larger models, reaching 67.5% on MMLU against Gopher’s 60.0%. That is a matched-compute comparison, lesson 3’s second question. They also diagnosed Kaplan’s error. Kaplan used “a fixed number of training tokens and learning rate schedule for all models”, which overstated the loss of shorter runs, and many of Kaplan’s runs were under 100M parameters. The prescription came from a choice of method, not from the power law itself.

The second revision came in 2024. Tamay Besiroglu and colleagues at Epoch AI reconstructed Chinchilla’s data from its plots and refit Approach 3 (Besiroglu et al. 2024). The published estimates were “inconsistent with their first two estimation methods”, fit the data poorly, and had confidence intervals so narrow they “would require over 600,000 experiments, while they likely only ran fewer than 500.” The published constants imply about 70 tokens per parameter; the refit implies about 20, matching Approaches 1–2 and Chinchilla’s actual run. That is lesson 3’s third question, independent reproduction, doing its job.

Hold these together, not apart Kaplan’s allocation was revised, Chinchilla’s constants were corrected, and the power-law structure survived both. What held is that loss is a smooth, predictable function of N and D for a given recipe. “The scaling laws were wrong” and “the scaling numbers are settled” both misread this history.

Offset vs exponent: shifting the curve vs changing its slope

On log-log axes, an improvement such as a new architecture, optimizer or data filter can change a scaling curve in two ways, and which one it is decides what a small-scale win is worth.

  • Offset. The line shifts but keeps its slope. This is a constant compute multiplier k: the method reaches any loss with 1/k of the compute, at every scale. With αC = 0.05, a 2× multiplier is worth 2−0.05 ≈ 0.966, about 3.4% lower loss. Small loss gaps can be large compute gaps.
  • Exponent. The slope changes, so the gap grows or shrinks with scale and the curves can cross. Something that looks worse at small scale can win at large scale, and the reverse can also happen.

The literature has both cases. Hestness et al. found that “model improvements only shift the error but do not appear to affect the power-law exponent”, which are offsets. Kaplan’s Figure 7 shows the other kind: LSTMs match Transformers on early-context tokens but plateau after fewer than 100 tokens, and Transformers “asymptotically outperform LSTMs due to improved use of long contexts”. So comparing architectures means comparing curves at several matched scales, not single points. Lesson 6 turns this into a check you can run on new claims.

Where it stops being settled

Everything above concerns average pretraining loss for a fixed recipe. Three live questions lie outside what the fits establish.

1. From loss to abilities: emergence vs the metric “mirage”

Jason Wei and colleagues call an ability emergent “if it is not present in smaller models but is present in larger models”, so it “cannot be predicted simply by extrapolating” (Wei et al. 2022). Rylan Schaeffer, Brando Miranda and Sanmi Koyejo answered that “nonlinear or discontinuous metrics produce apparent emergent abilities”, while continuous metrics show smooth change (Schaeffer et al. 2023). The mechanism fits in one line. If per-token accuracy p improves smoothly, exact match on a 10-token answer scores p10, so p = 0.5, 0.7, 0.9 gives 0.1%, 2.8%, 35%: flat, then a cliff.

That did not close the question. Zhengxiao Du and colleagues held data, tokenizer and architecture fixed and found some tasks jump once pretraining loss crosses a threshold, “regardless of the continuity of metrics” (Du et al. 2024). The Inverse Scaling Prize found 11 tasks where larger models do worse (McKenzie et al. 2023). And mechanistic work shows continuous change under apparent jumps: grokking “arises from the gradual amplification of structured mechanisms” (Nanda et al. 2023), and induction heads form at a visible bump in the training loss. A 2025 survey still calls it “an intense scientific debate” (Berti et al. 2025). The honest answer is: sometimes, partially, and it’s contested. A smooth fall in average loss does not say whether a particular structure or ability appears, which is why lesson 1’s “more scale will resolve it” was wrong.

2. How long the trends continue, and where compute should go

If current trends continue, training sets will match the stock of public human text “between 2026 and 2032” (Villalobos et al., revised 2024). As of October 2026 we are inside that window, and whether the limit binds depends on synthetic data, data efficiency and other modalities, none of which the fitted laws cover. Repeating data is nearly free for up to 4 epochs, after which “the value of adding compute eventually decays to zero” (Muennighoff et al. 2023). Chinchilla-optimal also minimises training compute only. With heavy inference demand it pays to train smaller models for longer, and quality keeps improving out to 10,000 tokens per parameter (Sardana et al. 2023). Test-time compute is a separate axis: FLOPs-matched, on problems where a smaller model already has some success, it can beat a 14× larger model (Snell et al. 2024). These add axes to optimise; they do not overturn the pretraining laws.

3. The Bitter Lesson vs the bet on new objectives

Rich Sutton argues that “general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin” (The Bitter Lesson, 2019). It is a historical induction from chess, Go, speech and vision, not a theorem. Against it stands LeCun’s bet that autoregressive prediction alone is insufficient. Sutton himself, in 2025: “It’s an interesting question whether large language models are a case of the bitter lesson. They are clearly a way of using massive computation… But they’re also a way of putting in lots of human knowledge” (Dwarkesh Podcast, Sept 2025). Both are positions, not results: a scaling law measures loss for one recipe and cannot decide whether a different objective is necessary.

Every scaling claim you meet should land in one of these three bins:

Established (in range)

  • Loss falls as a power law in N, D and C
  • Small exponents, steep diminishing returns
  • An entropy floor exists
  • Data must grow with parameters

Revised or contested

  • Kaplan’s allocation (revised)
  • Chinchilla’s exact constants (corrected)
  • Emergence vs mirage
  • Data limits, inference and test-time trade-offs

Not in the laws

  • Specific capabilities at a given scale
  • AGI dates or FLOP thresholds
  • “Hit a wall” as a refutation
  • Which objective is necessary

Before repeating “the scaling laws show…”

Same discipline as lesson 3’s misuse check, applied to scaling claims, which get misused in both directions.

“Scaling laws show AGI arrives once we pass 10X FLOPs” — Overclaim. The laws relate compute to average next-token loss on one distribution, within a measured range. Nothing in them maps loss to a named capability, and that mapping is exactly the contested part. Kaplan’s own Section 6.3 says the trends must break down.
“Scaling has hit a wall, so the scaling laws were wrong” — Overclaim in the other direction. The fits predicted diminishing returns all along (−16% loss per 10× parameters) and a floor. A refutation would be loss departing from the fitted curve under a matched recipe and data distribution. Stories about one lab’s unpublished runs fail lesson 3’s peer-review question before they get that far.
“Chinchilla proved the optimal ratio is 20 tokens per parameter” — Overclaim of precision. About 20 is a compute-optimal estimate for training loss on one data mix. The Approach 3 constants needed correcting, the optimum shifts with data quality (Besiroglu et al. cite work finding lower ratios for higher-quality data), and heavy inference makes smaller-and-longer the cheaper choice.

Primary resource

See Jordan Hoffmann et al., “Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models” (Chinchilla, 2022). It confirms the power-law structure across 400+ training runs and overturns a confident earlier prescription in the same paper, which makes it the clearest single source for telling the established parts of scaling from the revisable recipes. Its Table 2 puts the competing exponents side by side.
