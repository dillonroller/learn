Forming an Informed Opinion: Evaluating New Architecture Claims

Lesson 3 gave you four questions to ask before repeating a result. Lessons 4 and 5 gave you two yardsticks: the evidence ladder for mechanistic claims and offset versus exponent for scaling claims. This lesson combines them into a fixed audit for the field’s most common claim, “new architecture X beats Transformers.” The goal: given a headline and its paper, run five checks and write a calibrated verdict. It is the same rigor-over-rhetoric habit as Gödel-misuse in Logic and superposition-misuse in Quantum Mechanics. The worked example is Mamba; the drill uses KAN.

The audit: five checks, then a verdict

The architecture-claim audit
  1. Triage. Which of five claim types is this? Each needs different evidence.
  2. Matched comparison. Compute, data and size as in lesson 3, plus tuning effort.
  3. Regime. One scale or a curve? Which benchmark? Contaminated, or within seed noise?
  4. Base rate. How often do claims like this survive? Start there.
  5. Evidence trail. What did later, more independent work find?
Then write the verdict: a bucket, a confidence, and what would change your mind.

Steps 2, 3 and 5 extend lesson 3’s questions 2, 1 and 3. Question 4 (the headline set beside the limitations) becomes the verdict. Steps 1 and 4 are new.

Step 1: triage the claim type

A headline usually bundles several claims. Sort them first, because each type needs its own evidence:

  1. Efficiency at matched quality (“5× faster inference”): throughput or memory on stated hardware, at equal quality.
  2. Quality at matched compute and scale (“outperforms Transformers of the same size”): step 2’s matched baseline.
  3. Better scaling (“faster scaling laws”): a curve over several scales, never one point.
  4. Mechanism (“it works because…”): ablations that remove the named component and retrain. Claims about a trained model’s internals go on lesson 4’s ladder.
  5. Necessity or impossibility (“X is required for general intelligence”, “Transformers can’t Y”): a proof about a precisely defined task class, or evidence against every plausible alternative route.

Type 5 is asserted most and established least. JEPA’s thesis is type 5; I-JEPA’s benchmark numbers are type 2, and evidence for the second does not carry over to the first. A well-established type-5 result looks like Jelassi et al.’s copying proof below: narrow, formal and proved.

Step 2: a matched comparison also matches tuning effort

Lesson 3’s question 2 covers compute, data and size. The less visible confound is effort. When Gábor Melis, Chris Dyer and Phil Blunsom re-tuned recurrent language models with large-scale automatic hyperparameter search, properly regularised standard LSTMs outperformed the newer architectures that had claimed state-of-the-art results. Zachary Lipton and Jacob Steinhardt call this trend (ii) in their survey of troubling trends: “failure to identify the sources of empirical gains.”

Tuning-effort asymmetry (working definition) The new method gets a hyperparameter sweep and a modern recipe; the baseline gets defaults copied from an older paper. The measured gap then mixes architecture with tuning budget, the confound that changing one variable at a time prevents. Ask who tuned the baseline, with what budget, and whether its recipe is current.

Step 3: check the regime (scale, benchmark, noise)

Scale. A win at one scale is a point, not a curve. By lesson 5’s offset-versus-exponent distinction, an advantage at 125M parameters can shrink, vanish or reverse at 8B.

Benchmark contamination (working definition) Test items, or close paraphrases, leak into training data, so the score partly measures memorization rather than the ability the benchmark names. Oscar Sainz and colleagues argue this inflates results and can produce wrong published conclusions. The cleanest test is a matched fresh benchmark: Hugh Zhang and colleagues’ GSM1k mirrors GSM8k and showed accuracy drops of up to 8% for some model families, while frontier models showed minimal overfitting. Contamination is measured, not a universal excuse.

Noise. Xavier Bouthillier and colleagues show that variance from data sampling, initialization and hyperparameter choice markedly changes benchmark conclusions. A single-seed gap smaller than the seed-to-seed spread is not a finding.

Step 4: start from the base rate

Sharan Narang and colleagues at Google re-implemented about fifty Transformer modifications in one shared codebase and found that “most modifications do not meaningfully improve performance.” The ones that helped were mostly minor (SwiGLU, RMSNorm), added parameters or FLOPs, or came from the same codebase. Their own caveat is fixed hyperparameters, though a 25-configuration sweep still could not make the Universal Transformer match the baseline.

So your prior that a new architecture generally beats a well-tuned Transformer should be modest; skipping that step is base-rate neglect. Modest is not zero: SwiGLU and RMSNorm survived, and both are part of the “Transformer++” recipe that Mamba benchmarks against. Update proportionally; never snap to 0 or 1.

Step 5: follow the evidence trail

A paper is one data point; the trail is what came after. Rank follow-ups by independence, from least to most: same authors, new team with the same code, new team with new code, formal theory. Peer review is a different check. Mamba was rejected at ICLR 2024 and published at COLM 2024; KAN was accepted at ICLR 2025, with its abstract narrowed to “small-scale AI + Science tasks.” Neither decision substitutes for the controlled follow-ups.

The calibrated verdict

Verdict format Claim (scoped: which model, scale, tasks) · Bucket: established under these conditions / promising but unproven / overclaim · Confidence (a number you would bet at) · What would change my mind (a specific result).

The buckets are this course’s three columns (demonstrated, reasonable bet, marketing) turned into a verdict. Keep two answers apart, as in step 5 of Logic’s five-step process: is the evidence real under its stated setup, and does the general claim follow? A paper can get “yes” and “no.” Your confidence should be calibrated: 80% verdicts right about 80% of the time.

There are two ways to fail. Swallowing: appeal to authority (a famous lab tells you who is betting, not whether the bet pays) or hasty generalization from one benchmark. Dismissing: equally miscalibrated, so steelman the architecture’s case first. Lipton and Steinhardt’s other trends are useful red flags: speculation presented as explanation, mathiness, and suitcase words such as “reasoning” in a results section.

Worked example: Mamba, from abstract to trail

Albert Gu and Tri Dao’s Mamba paper (2023) claims “5× higher throughput than Transformers” and that “Mamba-3B outperforms Transformers of the same size and matches Transformers twice its size.”

  1. Triage. Type 1 (throughput, linear in sequence length) plus type 2 (quality at equal size), with a type-4 story: input-dependent “selection” enables content-based reasoning.
  2. Matched comparison. Mostly good: the downstream baselines are Pythia models trained on the same Pile data, tokenizer and 300B tokens. But ask lesson 3’s “outperforms against what?” In the language results, the strongest recipe, Transformer++, appears in the 125M–1.3B scaling curves, where the paper’s own word is “match.” “Outperforms” is against Pythia: 63.3 versus 59.1 average zero-shot accuracy at 2.8B, and 1.6 points above Pythia-6.9B.
  3. Regime. At most 2.8B parameters, short-context zero-shot tasks. The synthetic selective-copying and induction-heads wins are small models trained on those tasks, recalling one token; nothing yet on larger scale or on copying long spans from context in a pretrained model.
  4. Base rate. Modest. The abstract itself says earlier subquadratic models “have not performed as well as attention” on language.
  5. Trail. The table below.

Hold these together, not apart Mamba’s headline was accurate under its setup, and pure SSMs are provably worse at copying from context. Both are true; they measure different things (average zero-shot accuracy versus retrieval). And the MMLU gap measured at one training budget mostly closed at another, so one comparison does not settle it.
Verdict (on this lesson’s evidence, as of 2026) “Pure Mamba-class SSMs match same-size Transformers on standard language benchmarks”: established under these conditions (to 8B parameters and 3.5T tokens, most short-context tasks, with five-shot MMLU still slightly behind), about 80% confident, since the large-scale evidence is one study with the original authors on it. “SSMs can replace attention in general”: overclaim; they lag on copying and in-context retrieval, and a proof explains why. “Hybrids beat Transformers at equal size”: promising but unproven beyond one controlled 8B study and vendor reports. A matched frontier-scale comparison by a team without a stake would change my mind.

Before saying “X beats Transformers”, or “it’s all hype”

Same discipline as lesson 3’s misuse check, applied to architecture headlines in both directions.

“A top lab built it, so it’s the future” — Appeal to authority. LeCun’s JEPA thesis is a serious bet, not a result. KAN came from a strong MIT-led team and passed peer review, yet its general accuracy claim did not survive a matched-FLOPs comparison, and the published version scopes it to small-scale science tasks.
“New architectures are always hype; Transformers win” — The base-rate error run backwards: a modest prior is not a zero prior. SwiGLU survived Narang et al.’s test, and Mamba’s core result survived an 8B controlled study.
“Transformers or SSMs: which one wins?” — A false dilemma, and the concrete case behind lesson 2’s warning against “pick one”. The best 8B model in Waleffe et al. was a hybrid with attention in 4 of 56 layers. A verdict judges a claim under conditions; crowning an architecture is outside this course’s mission.

Check your understanding

1. Melis et al. re-tuned older LSTM language models with large-scale hyperparameter search. What did that reveal about earlier architecture comparisons?

2. Narang et al. found most of about fifty Transformer modifications did not meaningfully help in a shared setting. How should that shape your reading of a new architecture paper?

3. What did Waleffe et al.’s 8B controlled study add to Mamba’s original headline?

4. Which of these is a calibrated verdict, in this lesson’s sense?

Practice before next lesson

This is the last lesson, so the practice closes the loop. Write a five-line audit plus a verdict for JEPA’s claims from lesson 2, triaging I-JEPA’s benchmark numbers and LeCun’s necessity thesis separately, or for any architecture headline from the past month. One line per check, then the verdict with a confidence and what would change your mind. Building evaluations for your own systems is AI Engineering’s evaluation lesson.

Primary resource

Zachary C. Lipton and Jacob Steinhardt, “Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship” (ICML 2018: The Debates). A field insider’s account of the failure patterns this audit guards against (speculation posing as explanation, unidentified sources of gains with Melis et al. as the case, mathiness, misuse of language), which turns “is this hype?” into checkable red flags.
