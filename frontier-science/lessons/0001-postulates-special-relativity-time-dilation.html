The Postulates of Special Relativity & Time Dilation

Pop-science treatments of relativity tend to hand you "moving clocks run slow" as a bare assertion, usually with a rubber-sheet metaphor nowhere near it (that metaphor is for general relativity anyway, and a rough one even there). It isn't an assertion. It's a two-line consequence of two very specific, falsifiable postulates, and you can derive the exact factor by which clocks slow down using nothing more than the Pythagorean theorem. This lesson does that derivation in full, then checks the result against a real particle that only reaches the ground because of it.

Two postulates, no more

Special relativity (Einstein, 1905) rests on exactly two postulates:

  • The principle of relativity: the laws of physics are identical in every inertial (non-accelerating) reference frame. No experiment performed inside a closed, uniformly-moving lab can tell you your own speed — only your speed relative to something else.
  • Invariance of the speed of light: light travels at the same speed c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s in every inertial frame, regardless of the motion of the source or the observer.
The second postulate is the strange one For every other velocity you know, speeds add: throw a ball at 10 m/s from a train moving at 20 m/s and a platform observer clocks it at 30 m/s. Light refuses to do this. A flashlight beam from a spaceship moving at 0.9c is still measured at exactly c by both the ship and a "stationary" observer — not 1.9c. This was an experimental finding (most famously the 1887 Michelson–Morley experiment, which found no trace of Earth's motion through a hypothesized medium for light), not a convenient assumption. Einstein's move was to take it seriously as an axiom and follow the consequences wherever they led, rather than explain it away.

If ordinary velocity addition is wrong for light, something else classical physics assumed absolute — space or time — has to give. It turns out to be time (and, as lesson 2 shows, space and simultaneity too). The rest of this lesson derives exactly how much time gives, starting from nothing but these two postulates and geometry.

Deriving the Lorentz factor from a light clock

Build the simplest possible clock: two mirrors, a fixed distance L apart, with a single pulse of light bouncing between them. Each one-way trip (bottom mirror to top mirror) is a single "tick."

In the clock's own rest frame, the light travels straight up a distance L at speed c, so one tick takes:

Δt₀ = L / c        (the "proper time" per tick — measured in the clock's own rest frame)

Now watch the same clock fly past you horizontally at speed v, oriented so the mirrors are perpendicular to the direction of motion. By the time the light pulse reaches the top mirror, the whole clock — mirrors and all — has moved horizontally. So in your frame, the light didn't travel straight up; it traced the diagonal hypotenuse of a right triangle: vertical leg L (the mirror separation, unaffected by horizontal motion), horizontal leg vΔt (how far the clock moved during the tick, as you measure it), hypotenuse length cΔt — because the second postulate says light still moves at exactly c in your frame too, diagonal path or not.

Pythagorean theorem:      (cΔt)² = L² + (vΔt)²

Expand and collect Δt:    c²Δt² − v²Δt² = L²
                           Δt²(c² − v²) = L²
                           Δt² = L² / (c² − v²)
                           Δt² = (L²/c²) / (1 − v²/c²)

Substitute Δt₀ = L/c:     Δt² = Δt₀² / (1 − v²/c²)

                           Δt = Δt₀ / √(1 − v²/c²)

That's the whole derivation. No assumption went in beyond the two postulates and the Pythagorean theorem — the diagonal path is forced by postulate 1 (the clock really did move), and the "light still goes at speed c along that diagonal" is forced by postulate 2.

The Lorentz factor

Give the recurring quantity above a name — the Lorentz factor:

γ = 1 / √(1 − v²/c²) = 1 / √(1 − β²)        where β = v/c

so the light-clock result reads Δt = γΔt₀. A few properties worth internalizing directly from the formula, not from a chart:

  • γ ≥ 1 always — the square root of a number ≤ 1 is ≤ 1, so 1 divided by it is ≥ 1.
  • γ → 1 as v → 0: at everyday speeds, Δt ≈ Δt₀, recovering ordinary Newtonian time. This is why you've never noticed time dilation directly — γ for a car at 30 m/s differs from 1 by about 1 part in 10¹⁴.
  • γ → ∞ as v → c: the closer you push toward the speed of light, the more violently γ blows up. This asymptote is also why nothing with mass can reach c — it would take infinite energy, a fact lesson 3 makes precise.

Time dilation, stated precisely

Generalize the light-clock result to any clock at all (any periodic physical process — a heartbeat, a radioactive decay, an oscillating atom — ticks at the same rate as a light clock built to match it, by postulate 1: the laws of physics, including how fast clocks tick, can't depend on which kind of clock you built). Define proper time τ as the time elapsed on a clock, measured in that clock's own rest frame — equivalently, the time between two events that happen at the same place in that frame. An observer watching the clock move past at speed v measures a longer elapsed time t:

t = γτ        (γ ≥ 1, so t ≥ τ — the "moving" clock is judged to run slow)
This is symmetric, and that's the point Nothing in the derivation privileged either observer — by the first postulate, the clock's own frame is just as valid an inertial frame as yours, and from its point of view, you're the one moving, so it judges your clock to be running slow by the same γ. Both observers are right, in their own frame; there is no absolute fact about whose clock is "really" ticking slower. (The one case where this symmetry famously seems to break — the "twin paradox," where a traveling twin returns younger — is resolved by the traveling twin having to accelerate to turn around, which breaks the symmetry between the two frames. It's a real effect, not a paradox, but working through it properly needs the spacetime-diagram tools from lesson 2.)
Worked example: a cosmic-ray muon reaching the ground

Muons are created when cosmic rays strike the upper atmosphere, roughly 15 km up, typically moving at v = 0.9998c (β = 0.9998). A muon's proper lifetime — how long it lasts before decaying, measured in its own rest frame — is τ₀ = 2.2 μs. Does it survive the trip to sea level?

γ = 1/√(1 − 0.9998²) = 1/√(1 − 0.99960004) = 1/√0.00039996 ≈ 50.0

Non-relativistic (wrong) estimate — no time dilation:
  d = v·τ₀ = 0.9998 × (3×10⁸ m/s) × (2.2×10⁻⁶ s) ≈ 660 m  (0.66 km)
  → the muon would decay after well under 1 km. Almost none should reach the ground.

Relativistic (correct) estimate — dilated lifetime as measured from the ground:
  τ = γτ₀ = 50.0 × 2.2 μs ≈ 110 μs
  d = v·τ ≈ 0.9998 × (3×10⁸ m/s) × (110×10⁻⁶ s) ≈ 33,000 m  (33 km)
  → comfortably covers the 15 km fall. Muons should reach the ground in large numbers.

The second number is the one nature agrees with — cosmic-ray muons are detected at sea level in exactly the abundance this calculation predicts, and essentially not at all in the abundance the non-relativistic estimate predicts. This is one of the most direct, everyday confirmations of special relativity there is: the muon detector doesn't have to leave the ground.

Check your understanding

1. Which of these is one of the two postulates of special relativity?

2. In the light-clock derivation, why is the light's path a diagonal — not vertical — when viewed from the frame in which the clock is moving?

3. A clock ticks off exactly 1 second of proper time. According to the derivation, what does an observer watching it move past at speed v measure for that same tick?

4. The worked example's muon survives roughly 50× longer, as timed from the ground, than its proper lifetime. What's the precise way to describe why?

Practice before next lesson

By hand: compute γ for β = 0.9 and again for β = 0.999. Notice how much more steeply γ grows near β = 1 than near β = 0 — that non-linearity is the real reason particle accelerators need enormous amounts of energy to gain the last percent of c, a fact lesson 3 will make quantitative. Then redo this lesson's muon calculation using β = 0.999 instead of 0.9998, and check whether the muon still comfortably survives its 15 km fall.
