Length Contraction, Relativity of Simultaneity & Spacetime Diagrams

Lesson 1 showed time isn't absolute. This lesson finishes the job: space isn't absolute either, and neither is "at the same time." Both results fall out of the exact same two postulates and the exact same γ you already derived — no new physics, just following the first result to its logical conclusions. Then you'll get a single picture, the spacetime diagram, that makes all three effects (time dilation, length contraction, relativity of simultaneity) visually obvious at once.

Length contraction, derived from time dilation

Let a spaceship have proper length L₀ — its length measured in its own rest frame, by an observer at rest relative to it. It flies past a stationary observer O at speed v. Two events mark the ship's passage:

  • E1: the ship's nose passes O.
  • E2: the ship's tail passes O.

In O's frame, both events happen at the exact same place — right where O is standing — just at different times. A single clock (O's own) is present at both events, so the elapsed time O measures, Δτ_O, is a proper time by definition. During that proper time, the ship — whose length in O's frame is L, the contracted length you're solving for — sweeps entirely past O:

L = v · Δτ_O        ⟹        Δτ_O = L / v      …(i)

Now switch to the ship's own frame. There, O is the one moving, at speed v, sweeping past the ship's full proper length L₀. The time this takes, as reckoned by the ship's own rulers and clocks, is Δt' = L₀ / v. This is not a proper time — E1 and E2 happen at two different places in the ship's frame (the nose and the tail), so no single clock is present at both. A time interval between two events at different locations is always the dilated (larger) one relative to the proper time between those same two events, by exactly the Lorentz factor:

Δt' = γ · Δτ_O

Substitute (i) and Δt' = L₀/v:      L₀/v = γ (L/v)

                                      L₀ = γL

                                      L = L₀ / γ
Length contraction A moving object's length, measured along its direction of motion, shrinks by exactly the same factor γ that its clocks slow by: L = L₀/γ. Since γ ≥ 1, this is always a contraction, never an expansion — and only along the direction of motion. Lengths perpendicular to the motion (the light clock's mirror separation from lesson 1) are completely unaffected, which is exactly the assumption that made lesson 1's derivation valid in the first place.

Relativity of simultaneity

Einstein's own version of this thought experiment: a train moves past a platform at speed v. Lightning strikes both ends of the train at once — simultaneously, in the platform's frame. A platform observer standing exactly halfway between the two strike points sees both flashes arrive at the same instant, confirming they were simultaneous for them.

Now consider an observer sitting at the train's midpoint. By postulate 2, light from each flash reaches them at speed c — but the train observer is moving toward the flash at the front and away from the flash at the rear. The front flash's light has less distance to cover to catch up with the moving observer; the rear flash's light has more. The train observer therefore sees the front flash first, and concludes — correctly, in their own frame — that the front strike happened before the rear strike. Two inertial observers, both applying the same postulates correctly, disagree about which of two events happened first.

What this does and doesn't break This isn't a limitation of measurement or a trick of light delay that a "true" simultaneity lurks behind — there is no frame-independent fact about whether the two strikes were simultaneous. "At the same time" is only ever meaningful relative to a chosen frame, for events happening at different places. (Events at the same place have a frame-independent time order — nothing here threatens that.) What it emphatically does not do is let anyone send a signal or a cause backward in time; see the light cone section below for exactly which event pairs this reordering can and can't happen to.

One transformation, all three effects

Time dilation, length contraction, and relativity of simultaneity aren't three separate rules to memorize — they're three shadows of a single linear map between two frames' coordinates, the Lorentz boost. For a boost along the x-axis at speed v (β = v/c, γ as before):

ct' = γ(ct − βx)
 x' = γ(x − βct)

or, if the linear-algebra framing helps more than the two separate lines do, as a matrix acting on the coordinate vector (ct, x):

| ct' |   | γ   −γβ | | ct |
|  x' | = | −γβ   γ | |  x |

This is worth comparing directly to a Galilean (Newtonian) boost, t' = t, x' = x − vt — notice the Galilean version leaves time completely alone, which is exactly the assumption special relativity throws out. Setting Δx = 0 in the boost above (two events at the same place) recovers time dilation; setting Δt = 0 (two events "simultaneous," i.e. same t, in the unprimed frame) gives Δt' = −γβΔx/c ≠ 0 whenever Δx ≠ 0 — relativity of simultaneity, in one line. Length contraction takes slightly more bookkeeping (tracking the ship's endpoints at equal t', not equal t) but comes out of the same two equations.

Worked example: quantifying the train-and-lightning case

Take two lightning strikes simultaneous in the platform frame S (Δt = 0), 300 m apart (Δx = 300 m), and a train moving at β = 0.6 (γ = 1.25, from lesson 1's exercise).

Δt' = γ(Δt − βΔx/c) = 1.25 × (0 − 0.6×300/(3×10⁸))
    = 1.25 × (−6×10⁻⁷ s) = −7.5×10⁻⁷ s = −750 ns

In the train's frame, the two strikes are separated by 750 nanoseconds — not simultaneous at all — with the sign showing the far (in the direction of motion) strike registering first. 750 ns sounds tiny, but it's a real, measured-in-principle effect, not a rounding error: it's what postulate 2 demands once Δx ≠ 0.

Reading a spacetime (Minkowski) diagram

Plot time up the vertical axis (conventionally as ct, so it has units of distance like the horizontal axis) and one space dimension along the horizontal axis. Every object traces a curve through this picture called its worldline — its entire history of where it was at every moment, all at once, in one static picture:

  • An object at rest traces a vertical line (position fixed, time advancing).
  • An object moving at constant velocity traces a straight, tilted line — the faster it moves, the closer to horizontal the tilt.
  • A light pulse, moving at exactly c, traces a line at exactly 45° — this is why plotting ct instead of bare t is the standard convention: it makes light's worldline slope exactly 1 in every diagram, regardless of what units you're using.

A boost to a different inertial frame doesn't redraw the diagram — it tilts the axes. The Lorentz transformation above rotates the ct' and x' axes toward each other (toward the 45° light line, symmetrically) rather than rotating them like ordinary Euclidean axes; this is the geometric picture behind relativity of simultaneity — a line of "constant t" (simultaneity, in one frame) is a different line, at a different tilt, than a line of "constant t'" (simultaneity, in the boosted frame).

The light cone, and what stays absolute

From any single event (a point on the diagram — one place, one time), draw the two 45° lines representing light emitted from it in both directions. In a full 3+1-dimensional picture these form actual cones, opening into the future and into the past — the future light cone and past light cone of that event. This structure sorts every other event in spacetime into exactly one of three relationships to the original event, and — unlike simultaneity — this sorting is the same in every frame:

  • Timelike separated: inside the light cone. One event could cause the other (a signal slower than light can connect them). Their time order is the same in every frame — no boost can reverse it.
  • Spacelike separated: outside the light cone. No signal, not even light, can connect them. Their time order genuinely depends on the observer's frame — exactly the train-and-lightning pair above, which is why reordering them broke nothing: neither strike could possibly have caused the other anyway.
  • Lightlike separated: exactly on the cone — connectable only by light itself.
Why causality survives relativity of simultaneity The reason nobody can use a fast enough spaceship to make an effect precede its cause is exactly this classification: if E1 causes E2, they're timelike (or lightlike) separated by definition — a causal influence has to travel between them at or below c. And timelike order is frame-invariant. Relativity of simultaneity only ever reorders spacelike-separated pairs, which by construction can't be causally related in the first place. This is the actual content behind "relativity doesn't let you violate causality," stated precisely instead of as a slogan.

The invariant way to say all this algebraically: define the spacetime interval between two events as Δs² = (cΔt)² − Δx². Unlike Δt or Δx separately, Δs² has the same value in every inertial frame — it's the one thing every observer agrees on, playing the role for spacetime that ordinary distance plays for space alone. Its sign gives you the classification directly: Δs² > 0 is timelike, Δs² < 0 is spacelike, Δs² = 0 is lightlike. (Check it on the train-and-lightning pair: Δt = 0, Δx = 300 m, so Δs² = −(300 m)² < 0 — spacelike, confirming causality was never at risk.) This same invariant quantity reappears, barely disguised, as the core of lesson 3's four-momentum.

Worked example: the muon, seen from its own frame

Lesson 1 explained the cosmic-ray muon's survival using time dilation, from Earth's frame. Length contraction gives the exact same experimental prediction from the muon's own frame — a good consistency check on both effects at once.

Atmosphere depth, proper length in Earth's frame:   L₀ = 15,000 m
Muon's Lorentz factor (from lesson 1, β = 0.9998):  γ ≈ 50.0

Contracted atmosphere depth, in the muon's frame:
  L = L₀/γ = 15,000 / 50.0 ≈ 300 m

Distance the muon can cover in its own (proper) lifetime, own frame:
  d = v·τ₀ = 0.9998 × (3×10⁸ m/s) × (2.2×10⁻⁶ s) ≈ 660 m

In the muon's own frame it never has to survive 15 km at all — the atmosphere is length-contracted down to about 300 m, well inside the ~660 m it can cover before decaying. Earth-frame observers explain the same detected muons via time dilation (the muon's clock runs slow); the muon's own frame explains them via length contraction (the trip is short). Both are correct, in their own frame — this is what it means for the two effects to be two faces of one underlying fact rather than two independent coincidences.

For each pair of events, classify the separation using Δs² = (cΔt)² − Δx².

Check your understanding

1. A ship has proper length L₀ = 100 m and flies past you at γ = 2. How long do you measure the ship to be?

2. Two events are simultaneous (Δt = 0) in your frame but happen at different locations (Δx ≠ 0). In a frame moving relative to yours, are they still simultaneous?

3. On a spacetime diagram (ct vertical, x horizontal), what does a light pulse's worldline look like?

4. Why can relativity of simultaneity reorder the two lightning strikes in the train example without allowing any violation of cause and effect?

Practice before next lesson

By hand: take two events with Δt = 2×10⁻⁶ s and Δx = 1000 m. Compute Δs² and classify the separation. Then pick a boost speed (any β you like) and use the Lorentz transformation to compute Δt' and Δx' in the boosted frame, and recompute Δs² there — confirm it comes out to the same number, even though Δt' and Δx' individually don't match Δt and Δx. That invariance is the property lesson 3 leans on directly.
