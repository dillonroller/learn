E=mc² and Four-Momentum

The most famous equation in physics gets treated as a slogan more often than as a result. It isn't handed down separately from the two postulates you already have — it falls straight out of asking what momentum and energy have to look like for their conservation laws to survive being Lorentz-transformed between frames, the same demand that produced time dilation and length contraction. This lesson derives it, then gives you the single invariant quantity — four-momentum — that ties energy and momentum together the same way the spacetime interval tied together time and space.

Why momentum needs a γ

Classical momentum, p = mv, is conserved in Newtonian mechanics — but "conserved in every frame" is a much stronger demand once frames are related by the Lorentz boost instead of a Galilean one. Plug ordinary mv into a collision, transform to a boosted frame using last lesson's transformation, and total momentum before and after the collision no longer match — the boost's extra γ factors on space and time throw plain mv's conservation off. The quantity that does stay conserved across every inertial frame turns out to be:

p = γmv        where m is the particle's fixed, frame-independent rest mass
m never changes — this isn't "relativistic mass" Older treatments sometimes described γm as a new, speed-dependent "relativistic mass." Modern usage (and this course) drops that framing: m is a fixed property of the particle, the same number in every frame — the rest mass, full stop. The γ isn't inflating the mass; it's telling you that momentum itself has to be defined with an extra factor of γ for the conservation law to survive a Lorentz boost. Keeping m fixed is also what makes the invariant mass-shell relation later in this lesson clean rather than circular.

One immediate consequence, tying directly back to lesson 1's asymptote: as v → c, γ → ∞, so p = γmv → ∞ for any particle with m > 0. Accelerating a massive particle to exactly c would take infinite momentum — and, as the next section shows, infinite energy. That's the precise reason nothing with mass ever reaches the speed of light, not a rule imposed from outside the theory.

Total energy, and where mc² comes from

The matching definition of energy that stays conserved across every frame, alongside p = γmv, is:

E = γmc²

Two special cases immediately worth naming:

  • Rest energy: set v = 0, so γ = 1: E₀ = mc². Even a completely motionless object has energy, purely by virtue of having mass. This is the famous equation — the special case of E = γmc² at zero speed.
  • Kinetic energy: the extra energy an object has from moving, KE = E − E₀ = (γ − 1)mc². At everyday speeds, a Taylor-series approximation of γ recovers the familiar KE ≈ ½mv² — Newtonian mechanics is what this formula looks like when β is tiny, exactly as with time dilation in lesson 1.

Four-momentum: one invariant tying E and p together

Combine energy and momentum into a single object, the four-momentum (E/c, pₓ, pᵧ, p_z) — the direct energy-momentum analogue of the (ct, x, y, z) spacetime coordinates from lesson 2. Exactly as Δs² = (cΔt)² − Δx² was invariant under a boost, four-momentum has its own frame-independent combination. Derive it directly from the two definitions above:

E² − (pc)² = (γmc²)² − (γmvc)²
           = γ²m²c⁴ − γ²m²v²c²
           = γ²m²c²(c² − v²)
           = γ²m²c⁴(1 − v²/c²)
           = γ²m²c⁴ / γ²                [since γ² = 1/(1 − v²/c²)]
           = m²c⁴
The energy–momentum invariant E² = (pc)² + (mc²)². This holds for any speed the particle has — the γ's that appear separately in E and p cancel completely, leaving only the particle's fixed rest mass. Every observer, in every frame, computes different E and different p for the same particle, but every observer computes the exact same E² − (pc)². It's the same trick as the spacetime interval, one level up: individual coordinates (or individual E, p) are frame-dependent; a specific combination of them isn't.
Worked example: 1 gram of mass, converted entirely to energy 
E₀ = mc² = (0.001 kg) × (3×10⁸ m/s)² = 0.001 × 9×10¹⁶ = 9×10¹³ J

Compare to TNT (1 kiloton TNT ≈ 4.184×10¹² J):
  9×10¹³ J ÷ 4.184×10¹² J/kt ≈ 21.5 kilotons

One gram — about a paperclip — carries roughly the destructive energy of the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima (historically estimated around 15 kt, from converting well under a gram of its plutonium's mass into energy). No practical process converts mass to energy with 100% efficiency, but this is the ceiling E = mc² sets, and it's why nuclear reactions (which convert a tiny fraction of a percent of their mass) already dwarf chemical reactions (which convert none, to any measurable precision) by a factor of millions.

Worked example: relativistic vs. classical kinetic energy

A 1000 kg spacecraft moving at β = 0.995 (γ ≈ 10.01, from lesson 2's exercises):

Relativistic:  KE = (γ − 1)mc² = (10.01 − 1) × 1000 × 9×10¹⁶ ≈ 8.11×10²⁰ J

Classical (wrong at this speed):  KE = ½mv² = ½ × 1000 × (0.995×3×10⁸)²
                                      ≈ 4.46×10¹⁹ J

Ratio:  8.11×10²⁰ / 4.46×10¹⁹ ≈ 18.2

The classical formula underestimates the true kinetic energy by more than 18×. This is exactly why particle accelerators pushing protons toward c need vastly more energy per unit speed gained than Newtonian intuition suggests — you're not just fighting friction-like resistance, you're fighting the same γ-blowup from lesson 1.

Check your understanding

1. Why does special relativity use p = γmv instead of the classical p = mv?

2. What is the rest energy of a 1 kg mass?

3. Which combination is the same in every inertial frame, for a given particle?

4. A photon has zero rest mass (m = 0). What does E² = (pc)² + (mc²)² say about it?

Practice before next lesson

By hand: verify the invariant directly for the spacecraft worked example above. Compute p = γmv and E = γmc² for the 1000 kg ship at β = 0.995, then check that E² − (pc)² comes out to (mc²)² — you should find the numbers agree to well within rounding error. This is the same "compute both sides, confirm they match" habit lesson 2's practice exercise built for the spacetime interval.
