From Special to General: the Equivalence Principle & Curved Spacetime

Everything so far assumed inertial (non-accelerating) frames and no gravity — special relativity's own name admits it's a special case. Einstein needed another decade to fold gravity in. The bridge is a single observation he later called "the happiest thought of my life": free fall and weightlessness are the same thing, not just similar things. This lesson follows that thought through to its first quantitative payoff — gravity making clocks run at different rates, which real GPS satellites correct for every single day — and to the conceptual picture (curved spacetime, not a force) that lesson 5 will make precise with an actual equation.

The equivalence principle

Drop a rock and yourself off a cliff together (purely as a thought experiment): in free fall, you and the rock accelerate identically, so relative to you, the rock just floats — weightless, motionless in your hands, exactly as if there were no gravity at all. This isn't a coincidence of Earth's gravity specifically; it works because of a fact Galileo already knew and Newton's laws just barely fail to explain: inertial mass (the m resisting acceleration in F = ma) and gravitational mass (the m that gravity pulls on in F = GMm/r²) are numerically identical for every object ever tested, to extraordinary precision (better than one part in 10¹⁵ in modern torsion-balance experiments) — with no reason in Newtonian physics for them to have to match at all.

Einstein promoted that coincidence to a postulate, the equivalence principle: locally — in a small enough region of space and time that gravity doesn't measurably vary — free fall in a gravitational field is indistinguishable from floating in empty space with no gravity at all, and being at rest in a gravitational field is indistinguishable from constant acceleration in empty space. No local experiment, however precise, can tell the two situations apart.

Einstein's elevator

The classic illustration: you wake up in a sealed, windowless elevator with no way to look outside. You feel your feet pressed to the floor with a force that would read as 1g on a scale. Two scenarios produce this identical sensation:

  • The elevator sits at rest on Earth's surface, and gravity pulls you down.
  • The elevator is in deep space, far from any mass, being accelerated "upward" at 1g by a rocket — the floor is pushing up into you, same as standing on the ground.

No experiment performed entirely inside the sealed elevator — dropping a ball, bouncing a laser off the walls, weighing yourself — can tell you which scenario you're in. Conversely, cut the cable (or shut off the rocket): in both cases you become weightless. This is exactly why astronauts in orbit float — they aren't "outside gravity's reach" (at the International Space Station's altitude, Earth's gravity is only about 10% weaker than at the surface); they're in continuous free fall, which by the equivalence principle is locally indistinguishable from no gravity at all.

Gravitational time dilation, derived from the elevator

The equivalence principle turns a special-relativity result you already have into a genuinely new gravitational one. Consider the accelerating-rocket version of the elevator, height h, accelerating at g. A photon is emitted from the floor with frequency f, headed for the ceiling. Light covers the distance h in roughly t ≈ h/c. In that time, the rocket (and the ceiling detector riding with it) has picked up speed v = g·t = gh/c, moving away from the photon's direction of travel relative to where the floor was at emission. That's a receiver moving away from the source — an ordinary special-relativistic Doppler redshift:

Δf/f ≈ −v/c = −gh/c²        (photon arrives at the ceiling red-shifted, lower frequency)

By the equivalence principle, this has to be exactly what happens to light climbing height h in a genuine, static gravitational field of strength g too — the elevator can't tell the two situations apart, so neither can the photon. Since a lower frequency means a longer period between wave crests, and the period is what a clock built from that light would tick against, the matching statement for time itself is:

Δτ/τ ≈ +gh/c²        (a clock higher up, at weaker gravity, ticks faster than one lower down)

For a real planet's gravity, which weakens with distance rather than staying uniform, the height-times-g product generalizes to a difference in gravitational potential, Φ = −GM/r:

Δτ/τ ≈ ΔΦ/c² = (Φ_high − Φ_low)/c²
Where this derivation stops, honestly This gets you the correct leading-order (weak-field) formula, and it's genuinely how Einstein first found gravitational time dilation before general relativity was fully worked out. It is not a derivation of the full theory — going from "clocks run at different rates in different potentials" to the actual geometry of curved spacetime, and to an equation that tells you the curvature itself, is lesson 5's job, and it needs machinery (tensor calculus) this course deliberately doesn't build, per its own scope.
Worked example: GPS satellite clocks

GPS satellites orbit at altitude ≈ 20,200 km. Two separate relativistic effects act on their onboard clocks, and both matter at the precision GPS needs.

Earth's standard gravitational parameter: GM = 3.986×10¹⁴ m³/s²
r_surface = 6.371×10⁶ m         r_satellite = 6.371×10⁶ + 2.02×10⁷ = 2.657×10⁷ m

Gravitational potential (Φ = −GM/r):
  Φ_surface  = −3.986×10¹⁴ / 6.371×10⁶  ≈ −6.257×10⁷ J/kg
  Φ_satellite = −3.986×10¹⁴ / 2.657×10⁷ ≈ −1.500×10⁷ J/kg
  ΔΦ = Φ_satellite − Φ_surface ≈ +4.756×10⁷ J/kg   (satellite sits in the weaker field)

GR effect (gravitational time dilation), per day (86,400 s):
  Δτ/τ ≈ ΔΦ/c² = 4.756×10⁷ / 9×10¹⁶ ≈ 5.28×10⁻¹⁰
  → 5.28×10⁻¹⁰ × 86,400 s ≈ 4.57×10⁻⁵ s ≈ 45.7 μs/day  (satellite clock runs FAST)

SR effect (velocity time dilation, from lesson 1), orbital speed v = √(GM/r_satellite) ≈ 3873 m/s:
  γ − 1 ≈ v²/(2c²) = (3873)² / (2×9×10¹⁶) ≈ 8.33×10⁻¹¹
  → 8.33×10⁻¹¹ × 86,400 s ≈ 7.2×10⁻⁶ s ≈ 7.2 μs/day  (satellite clock runs SLOW)

Net: 45.7 − 7.2 ≈ 38.5 μs/day — the satellite clock runs fast overall.

38.5 μs/day sounds negligible until you convert it to a position error: GPS computes distance from light-travel time, so an uncorrected timing drift of 38.5 μs translates to c × 38.5 μs ≈ 3×10⁸ × 3.85×10⁻⁵ ≈ 11,500 m — over 11 km of position error accumulating per day. GPS works only because engineers built both corrections in from the start (see Neil Ashby's authoritative "Relativity in the Global Positioning System" for the full accounting). This is general and special relativity, checked against a system you may be carrying in your pocket right now.

Gravity is geometry, not a force

The equivalence principle explains why free-falling objects feel weightless: it's because, locally, free fall is unaccelerated, force-free motion — exactly like a freely drifting object far from any mass, tracing a straight worldline through spacetime. What differs near a massive body isn't that some invisible force is now acting on the falling object; it's that "straight" itself has stopped meaning what it meant in the flat spacetime of lessons 1–3. Two nearby free-falling objects, initially moving in parallel, will drift toward or away from each other over time near a massive body (this is tidal — the actual, physically detectable signature of gravity that the equivalence principle alone can't erase, since it's a difference across a region, not a local effect at a single point). The natural explanation is that spacetime itself is curved by mass and energy, and free-falling objects are simply following the straightest possible paths — called geodesics — through that curved geometry.

This reframes gravity entirely. Newton's gravity is a force reaching across empty space. Einstein's gravity is the shape of spacetime itself, with matter and energy telling spacetime how to curve, and curved spacetime telling matter how to move (a slogan usually attributed to John Wheeler). A useful, and correct, way to picture the connection to lesson 2's light cones: near a massive body, light cones themselves tilt and narrow, increasingly toward the mass, as you get closer — which is precisely how general relativity encodes "gravity gets stronger here" geometrically instead of as a force vector. Lesson 5 introduces the actual equation relating curvature to the matter and energy that causes it.

Practice before next lesson

By hand: use Δτ/τ ≈ gh/c² to estimate the gravitational time dilation between the ground floor and the top of a 500 m tall building on Earth (g ≈ 9.8 m/s²). Convert your fractional result into how many nanoseconds a clock at the top gains over one full year relative to a clock at the bottom — the effect is tiny but was actually measured this way (with atomic clocks carried up towers and mountains) well before GPS made it operationally important.
