Quantum Fields 101: Particles as Excitations of Fields

Pop science says the universe is made of tiny balls, and fields are how the balls push on each other. Quantum field theory (QFT) says the reverse. Its fundamental objects are fields, and a particle is one discrete unit of excitation of one of a field’s vibration modes. This lesson builds that picture from two things you already have: lesson 3’s E² = (pc)² + (mc²)² and Quantum Mechanics’ state vectors. The payoff is translation in both directions. You will read |2,0,1⟩ as a particle inventory, compute its energy, apply a† and a to it, and explain why an electron–positron pair costs at least 1.022 MeV and why every electron is exactly alike.

Why relativity forces fields

Every state space in the Quantum Mechanics course was fixed up front (one qubit: ℂ²; two qubits: ℂ²⊗ℂ²), and the particle count was part of that choice. Relativity breaks this in two ways:

  • Particle number changes. Rest energy is energy like any other, so a collision with enough of it can create new particles. A state space that assumes “exactly one electron” cannot even describe that.
  • Locality must hold. Nothing may influence a spacelike-separated event. A field has an observable at every point, and its equation couples each point only to its neighbours, so locality is built in.

The two collide when you pin a particle down. Confine an electron to less than ħ/mc ≈ 3.9×10⁻¹³ m (its reduced Compton wavelength) and the momentum spread this forces carries energy of order mc², which is enough to make new pairs. Malament’s theorem makes the conflict rigorous. A relativistic quantum theory of a fixed number of particles that obeys locality (plus a few other natural conditions) gives zero probability of finding a particle in any region (SEP, “Quantum Field Theory”, §5.3). QFT never fixes the number, so the theorem does not apply directly, and its local observables are field values, not particle positions. (Related theorems still keep QFT’s particles from being sharply localized; see the honesty box.)

What a quantum field is

A classical field φ(x, t) is a number at every point, evolving in time. In CS terms it is an array indexed by position, and in the continuum limit it has infinitely many entries. A quantum field replaces each entry with an observable φ̂(x), “the field value at x” (strictly, only averages over a small region are well-defined operators, per SEP §5.3), in the same way that Z is “the qubit along z”. One state vector covers the whole field:

               one qubit            a quantum field
observables    X, Y, Z              φ̂(x) at every x
state          |ψ⟩ in ℂ²            |Ψ⟩ in Fock space
particle count fixed by setup       an observable, N̂
The field is not the wavefunction φ̂(x) is a question you can ask at x. |Ψ⟩ holds the amplitudes for the answers, including amplitudes for different particle counts. “Second quantization” is a misleading name: one classical field is quantized once.

Normal modes and the dispersion relation

Because neighbouring points are coupled, the array entries are not independent. A change of basis fixes that. The Fourier transform diagonalizes the coupling into independent normal modes, waves labelled by wavevector k, each oscillating at its own frequency ω_k. (Why momentum labels? Translation symmetry, which is lesson 8’s subject.) The simplest relativistic field equation, Klein–Gordon (Strassler’s “Class 1”), fixes that frequency. Substitute one wave, φ = A cos(kx − ωt):

∂²φ/∂t² − c² ∂²φ/∂x² = −(mc²/ħ)² φ

−ω² + c²k² = −(mc²/ħ)²
×ħ²:   (ħω)² = (ħkc)² + (mc²)²

That is lesson 3’s E² = (pc)² + (mc²)² exactly, once you read E = ħω and p = ħk. The mass sets a minimum frequency, ω_min = mc²/ħ, reached at k = 0. A massless field such as light has ω = ck and can oscillate as slowly as you like. Strassler draws this as a “Pythagorean” triangle, writing hν for ħω.

Quantize each mode: particles are quanta

Each normal mode’s amplitude q_k(t) obeys d²q_k/dt² = −ω_k² q_k, the equation of a mass on a spring, so each mode is a harmonic oscillator. A quantum oscillator has the energies E_n = ħω(n + ½), n = 0, 1, 2, … This lesson states that result; the derivation belongs to Quantum Mechanics’ harmonic-oscillator lesson, and Strassler’s “Waves (Quantum)” states the same result for waves, as (n+½)hν. The |n⟩ are energy eigenstates, so under the Schrödinger equation each one only gains a phase. The rungs are equally spaced, so energy enters or leaves mode k only in lumps of ħω_k.

Particles are quanta A particle with momentum ħk is one quantum of excitation of mode k of its field, and the integer n is the number of such particles. Each quantum carries ħω_k = √((ħkc)² + (mc²)²), which is a relativistic particle’s energy–momentum relation. In Strassler’s words (article 7), “the particles of nature are quanta of relativistic quantum fields.”

Ladder operators and Fock space

Each mode gets a pair of creation and annihilation operators, a† and a:

a†|n⟩ = √(n+1)|n+1⟩     add one quantum
a|n⟩  = √n|n−1⟩         remove one quantum
a|0⟩  = 0               zero vector: nothing to remove
a†a|n⟩ = n|n⟩           number operator;  H_k = ħω_k(a†a + ½)

On |0⟩…|3⟩ (truncated):   ⎡0 1 0  0 ⎤
                      a = ⎢0 0 √2 0 ⎥    a†a = diag(0,1,2,3)
                          ⎢0 0 0  √3⎥
                          ⎣0 0 0  0 ⎦

Fock space is the tensor product of one ladder per mode, built the same way as two-qubit states: |n₁⟩⊗|n₂⟩⊗|n₃⟩ = |n₁,n₂,n₃⟩. Each basis state is an inventory: |2,0,1⟩ is two quanta with momentum ħk₁ and one with ħk₃, holding 2ħω₁ + ħω₃ above the vacuum. The field operator is built from the same ladders, schematically φ̂(x) = Σ_k A_k(a_k e^(ikx) + a†_k e^(−ikx)), where A_k is a normalization constant. Particle number N̂ = Σ_k a†_k a_k is now an ordinary observable, so a field state can be a superposition of different counts. The state (|1,0,0⟩ + |0,2,0⟩)/√2 gives a count of 1 or 2, each with probability ½ by the Born rule. Interactions are products of field operators at a point, so they expand into terms that create, destroy or convert particles (Wilczek, “Quantum Field Theory”, 1999).

Worked example: pair creation as bookkeeping 
Before: photon field 1 quantum (k_γ)   electron field vacuum
After:  photon field vacuum            electron field e⁻(p₁) + e⁺(p₂)

ħω_γ = E₁ + E₂ (+ recoil),  Eᵢ = √((pᵢc)² + (mc²)²) ≥ mc²
⇒ ħω_γ ≥ 2mc² = 2 × 0.511 MeV = 1.022 MeV

Invariant E² − (pc)²:
  lone photon:  (ħω)² − (ħkc)² = 0
  any pair:     ≥ (2mc²)² > 0   (equal only if both are at rest
                                  in the pair’s zero-momentum frame)
  Mismatch: a lone photon cannot pair-produce. A nucleus takes
  the momentum. Next to lead-208 the threshold is
  2mc²(1 + m/M) ≈ 1.022 MeV + 2.7 eV.

The positron is the electron field’s other kind of quantum (Wilczek: the electron field’s operators “destroy electrons” and “create positrons”). Pair production in the nuclear field dominates how high-energy photons lose energy in matter (PDG 2026, Passage of Particles Through Matter, §34.4.5; threshold summary on Wikipedia).

The vacuum is not nothing

The quantum vacuum |0,0,0,…⟩ has every mode on its bottom rung. It holds no quanta but still has zero-point energy: ½ħω_k per boson mode (fermion modes, like the electron’s, contribute −½ħω_k; Strassler 7a), and repeated measurements of the field value still scatter around zero: ⟨φ̂⟩ = 0 but ⟨φ̂²⟩ > 0. Summed over infinitely many modes this diverges. Without gravity it does no harm, because only energy differences are measurable and the offset is discarded. Gravity responds to absolute energy through lesson 5’s stress-energy tensor, and that turns the offset into the cosmological-constant puzzle (Carroll, “The Cosmological Constant”, §1.3).

Two misuses to catch Run the misuse check. “Tap the vacuum for free energy” fails because the vacuum is the lowest-energy state, so there is nothing lower to fall to. “Virtual particles pop in and out of the vacuum” fails because the vacuum is an energy eigenstate. Under unitary evolution it only gains a global phase, so nothing in it changes; the scatter in field measurements is a fixed property of that state, not motion. “Virtual particle” names intermediate terms in interaction calculations, and “a virtual particle is not a particle at all” (Strassler).

Why every electron is identical

Wilczek calls this “the single most profound fact about Nature that quantum field theory uniquely explains.” Any two electrons are exactly alike because “both are excitations of the same underlying ur-stuff, the electron field.” Fock space shows the mechanism. |1,0,1⟩ records how many quanta each mode holds and never which quantum is which. With billiard balls, “A in k₁, B in k₃” and “B in k₁, A in k₃” would be two states. Here they are one, because quanta can be counted but not numbered (SEP §5.1.1). Photon modes take any n, which is how a laser works. Electron modes take only 0 or 1, which is Pauli exclusion (lesson 7’s fermions; Strassler 7a).

Wave–particle duality dissolves the same way. There is always one object, the field. A detector click absorbs one whole quantum, and the Born rule gives the odds of where and whether it happens. That is why a photon sent at a half-silvered mirror is always detected whole in one output beam, never half in each. Its state is (|1,0⟩ + |0,1⟩)/√2 over the two output modes, and occupation numbers are integers. Nor is a photon a tiny ball. A single-mode photon has definite momentum ħk and is spread over the whole mode.

Established versus debated (as of 2026) Established: the formalism and its predictions (pair thresholds, identical particles, photon counting). “Particles are tiny balls” is wrong. Standard working picture: “it is only the fields … that are permanent” (Wilczek), and Hobson argues the stronger thesis that there are no particles, only fields. Still debated: the SEP entry (revised September 2026) lists open problems on both sides. Particle readings face localization no-go theorems and the Unruh effect. Field readings face operator-valued fields that assign no definite value at a point. Fock space is also exact only for free fields: with interactions (Haag’s theorem), countable quanta are cleanly defined only long before or after a collision, though how much that matters is itself contested (SEP §5.5). No winner is picked here. “QFT proves particles don’t exist” overclaims, because quanta are real, countable, robust excitations.

Practice before next lesson

By hand, with ħ = 6.582×10⁻¹⁶ eV·s, hc ≈ 1240 eV·nm, 1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J, c = 3×10⁸ m/s:

  1. Find ω_min = mc²/ħ for an electron-field mode. Compare it with 500 nm light, ω = 2πc/λ. The ratio should come out at roughly 2×10⁵.
  2. A 1 J pulse of 500 nm light: find the energy per quantum (in eV, then J) and the number of quanta. If the pulse filled a single mode, what would n be?
  3. Run the worked example backwards. An electron and a positron at rest annihilate into two photons. Write the before and after inventories and find each photon’s energy.

Check: item 2 gives about 2.5×10¹⁸ quanta, and that number is n. In item 3 the photons fly apart back to back, so each carries 0.511 MeV.
