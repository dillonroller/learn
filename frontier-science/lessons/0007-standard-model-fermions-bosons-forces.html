The Standard Model Zoo: Fermions, Bosons, and the Four Forces

Pop science sells a poster: a dozen little balls of matter, “force particles” tossed between them like tennis balls, and a “God particle” that gives everything its mass. This lesson replaces it with the real catalogue, built on lesson 6’s quantum fields. Every entry is the quantum of one field, and the two big columns, fermions and bosons, are two occupation rules in Fock space. You will place any named particle in the table (fermion or boson, quark or lepton, generation, charge, spin, forces felt) and sort claims about it into established physics, oversimplification, or open question. First up is “the Higgs gives the proton its mass”: one line of arithmetic shows it is off by a factor of about 100.

Two occupation rules: fermions and bosons

Lesson 6 ended on a rule it did not explain: photon modes take any number of quanta, electron modes only 0 or 1 (identical particles). That rule splits every field in nature in two.

  • A fermion field allows n = 0 or 1 per mode, so two identical fermions are never in the same mode: the Pauli exclusion principle. A mode includes the spin state, so an atomic orbital holds two electrons (spin up, spin down) and a third must go a shell higher. Hence atomic shells, the periodic table, and matter that resists squeezing: exclusion “controls the formation of matter” (spin–statistics theorem).
  • A boson field allows any n = 0, 1, 2, …. Piling quanta into one mode is a laser, and a classical radio wave is a mode with enormous n: “lasers can be made from photons … but not from electrons” (Strassler 7a).

The rule is fixed by spin, a quantum’s intrinsic angular momentum in units of ħ. The spin–statistics theorem of relativistic QFT says half-integer spin means fermion and integer spin means boson. The electron’s spin-½ is the qubit state in ℂ² you already know. The Standard Model sorts cleanly:

spin ½   fermions   all 12 matter particles (quarks, leptons)
spin 1   bosons     force carriers: photon, 8 gluons, W⁺, W⁻, Z
spin 0   boson      the Higgs boson
spin 2   boson      graviton: hypothetical, not in the model

Twelve matter fields in three generations

Quarks feel the strong force; leptons do not. Each family has two charge slots, and the four-slot pattern appears three times. Each copy is a generation: same charges and forces, heavier masses. Rest energies mc² in MeV (PDG 2026 quarks, leptons):

               gen 1      gen 2       gen 3
quarks   +2/3  u  2.16    c  1273     t  172 600
         −1/3  d  4.70    s  92.9     b  4186
leptons   −1   e  0.511   μ  105.66   τ  1776.9
           0   ν_e        ν_μ         ν_τ      (all < 1 eV)

feels:  quarks           strong, weak, EM
        charged leptons  weak, EM
        neutrinos        weak only     (all: gravity)
  • You are generation 1. “All stable matter in the universe is made from particles that belong to the first generation” (CERN). Lesson 1’s cosmic-ray muon is a generation-2 “heavy electron”: same charge and spin, 206.8× the mass, and its 2.197 μs life ends in the weak decay μ⁻ → e⁻ ν̄_e ν_μ.
  • Every fermion has an antiparticle of opposite charge (CERN). Positrons are routine: PET scanners image the gamma-ray pairs from their annihilation. Whether neutrinos are their own antiparticles is open.
  • Three is measured, not explained. Z decays count 2.996 ± 0.007 light neutrino types (PDG 2026). Why three, and why masses spanning at least 11 orders of magnitude (the top quark weighs 3.4×10⁵ electrons), nobody knows.

Four forces, three of them in the Standard Model

In field language, a force is a boson field coupling to a charge that matter fields carry. The Standard Model’s three are carried by gauge bosons, the spin-1 quanta of gauge fields, a special kind of quantum field (PDG 2026 bosons):

force     carrier     spin  mass (GeV)     couples to
strong    8 gluons     1    0              color charge
EM        photon γ     1    0              electric charge
weak      W⁺ W⁻, Z     1    80.36, 91.19   weak charge (W: left-handed)
gravity   graviton?    2    —              energy; NOT in the SM
  • Electromagnetic: the massless photon couples to electric charge and reaches arbitrarily far. Neutrinos have no charge, so light ignores them.
  • Strong: each quark carries one of three “colors” (labels, not hues). The 8 massless gluons carry color too, unlike the neutral photon, so they interact with each other. As a result the interaction weakens as quarks come close (asymptotic freedom, Nobel 2004) and stops weakening as they separate. The result is color confinement: quarks and gluons appear only inside color-neutral hadrons, such as the proton uud (charge ⅔+⅔−⅓ = +1) and neutron udd (0). Pull on a quark and the field energy becomes new quark–antiquark pairs (lesson 6’s pair creation), so you get more hadrons, never a free quark. Experiment and lattice simulation establish this; a rigorous derivation does not exist, and the closely related Yang–Mills “mass gap” is a Clay Millennium Problem.
  • Weak: heavy W and Z give a short range, ħ/(m_W c) ≈ 2.5×10⁻¹⁸ m. It alone changes flavor: a W⁻ turns d into u (−⅓ = +⅔ + (−1)), which is neutron beta decay, n → p e⁻ ν̄_e. The W couples only to left-handed particles, so this force alone breaks mirror symmetry (weak interaction; lesson 8, parity).
  • Gravity “is not part of the Standard Model” (CERN); the Einstein field equation describes it. Its would-be quantum, the spin-2 graviton, is “not yet found”.

The gauge group is SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1), and it predicts the carrier count. SU(N) has N²−1 independent directions: 8 for SU(3), the gluons; 3 for SU(2), one per Pauli matrix; plus 1 for U(1). Those 3 + 1 mix into W⁺, W⁻, Z and γ, so 12 in all. Why symmetry makes forces is lesson 8’s gauge symmetry. The SU(2)×U(1) part is electroweak unification: near the W and Z masses (~100 GeV) and above, electromagnetism and the weak force act as one (Nobel 1979). Folding in the strong force or gravity is unconfirmed (CERN). And “exchange” is not a game of catch: a “virtual photon” is a term in a perturbative calculation, not a ball in flight (lesson 6’s warning).

The Higgs field and the Higgs boson

The Higgs field is the model’s only spin-0 field. In its lowest-energy state it is not zero but has the same value everywhere (lesson 8 shows how that hides the electroweak symmetry). That value gives the W and Z their masses while leaving the photon massless: it “doesn’t just give masses to things — it rearranges the forces” (Strassler). In the Standard Model it also gives each charged fermion a mass proportional to that particle’s own coupling, a coupling that is measured rather than predicted. Experiment has confirmed this for the heaviest ones (top, bottom, tau) and the muon; the electron’s and the light quarks’ couplings are still too small to measure directly. It does not give mass to gluons or photons, and how neutrinos get mass is not established.

The Higgs boson is one quantum of ripple, “a wave in that field” (CERN): spin 0, 125.13 ± 0.11 GeV (PDG 2026), announced by ATLAS and CMS on 4 July 2012 (ATLAS; CMS). The boson is the evidence that the field exists; it hands out no mass. Even CERN’s page loosely says the field “gives mass to all elementary particles”. The 2013 Nobel citation picked its verb with care: a mechanism that “contributes to our understanding of the origin of mass”.

Worked example: where the proton’s mass comes from 
proton = uud     m_u = 2.16 MeV   m_d = 4.70 MeV
quark rest energy:   2 × 2.16 + 4.70 = 9.02 MeV
proton rest energy:  938.27 MeV
Higgs-given share:   9.02 / 938.27 ≈ 0.96 %
the rest:            938.27 − 9.02 = 929.25 MeV ≈ 99 %

The other 99% is energy: quark and gluon motion plus energy stored in the gluon field. A bound system’s mass is its total rest energy over c², which is lesson 3’s rest energy at work. The proton is “three quarks …, lots of gluons, and lots of quark-antiquark pairs” (Strassler). The electron, whose mass the model does attribute to the Higgs, adds just 0.054% to a hydrogen atom.

Where this number stops, honestly Confined quarks cannot be weighed, so their masses are defined in a calculational scheme (PDG quotes them at a 2 GeV scale). A full lattice-QCD bookkeeping that also counts sea quarks finds quark energy 33%, gluon-field energy 37%, a quantum “trace anomaly” 23%, and 9% from quark-mass terms (Yang et al. 2018). So the Higgs-linked share is 1% naively, about 9% carefully. Either way, over 90% of the proton is QCD energy.

Established, oversimplified, or open (as of 2026)

Run the misuse check on any particle claim: which field, which coupling, which measurement?

  • Established: every particle in the table, the Higgs included; electroweak unification (Nobel 1979), with the W and Z masses measured to 0.01% or better (PDG 2026); confinement as an experimental fact; and neutrino mass, since oscillations show that “the neutrino, for a long time considered to be massless, must have a mass” (Nobel 2015).
  • Oversimplified: forces as thrown balls; the proton as three quarks; the Higgs giving everything mass; “the four forces are unified”; and “the God particle”, a book-title nickname (Lederman’s editor vetoed “The Goddamn Particle”) that physicists, Higgs included, criticise (Wikipedia).
  • Open: quantum gravity; dark matter, about 27% of the universe against ordinary matter’s 5%, and on CERN’s list of questions the model does not answer (CERN); dark energy and Λ; why three generations with these masses; how neutrinos get mass; why there is matter but almost no antimatter; and a proof of confinement.
A cautionary tale: muon g−2 For years, headlines said the muon’s magnetic moment disagreed with the Standard Model. Fermilab’s final result (June 2025, 127 parts per billion) sits close to the 2025 lattice-based prediction, “dampening the possibility of new physics” (Fermilab). As of 2026, theorists are still reconciling lattice and older data-driven calculations. An anomaly is a question, not a discovery.

Primary resource

Matt Strassler, “The Known (Apparently) Elementary Particles”. A particle theorist walks the whole table, up to the presumed but undiscovered graviton, pushes back on the simplifications this lesson sorts, and is plain about what the model cannot explain, such as the masses.
