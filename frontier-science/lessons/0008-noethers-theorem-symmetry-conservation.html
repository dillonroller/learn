Symmetry & Conservation: Noether's Theorem as the Thread Connecting It All

School physics hands you conservation laws as separate brute rules: energy, momentum and charge can be neither created nor destroyed. The real structure is tighter. Each law follows from a symmetry of the laws of physics, by a theorem Emmy Noether published in 1918, and when the symmetry fails, the law fails with it. You have leaned on this already. Lesson 3 required energy and momentum conservation to survive a Lorentz boost. Lesson 5 found energy-momentum conservation built into Einstein’s equation. And Quantum Mechanics’ precessing qubit kept its 0/1 odds frozen while its +/− odds swung. The win: given a symmetry or a scenario, name the conserved quantity Noether guarantees (or see that none is guaranteed), and verify a quantum conservation law by multiplying 2×2 matrices.

What a symmetry is, precisely

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves the laws unchanged (the equations of motion, or the Hamiltonian that generates them), not necessarily the state. Gravity looks the same in every direction around the Sun, yet Earth’s orbit is an ellipse. The law is symmetric even though this solution is not, and only the law matters.

A continuous symmetry comes in a family labelled by a real number, so it can be made arbitrarily small: shift time by Δt, shift space by Δx, rotate by θ, boost by v, multiply a quantum field by a phase eiθ. A discrete symmetry is all-or-nothing: the mirror flip x → −x (P), swapping particles with antiparticles (C), running time backwards (T). Lesson 1’s principle of relativity was the course’s first symmetry principle: the laws are unchanged by a Lorentz boost, and lesson 2’s invariant interval is what that symmetry preserves. Einstein reversed the old order of explanation. It became natural, in Wigner’s words, “to derive the laws of nature and to test their validity by means of the laws of invariance” (SEP §2.1).

Noether’s theorem

Noether’s (first) theorem, 1918 In a theory described by a Lagrangian or Hamiltonian, every continuous symmetry of the laws yields a quantity that stays constant in time.

It grew out of general relativity. Hilbert and Klein, debating the status of energy conservation in Einstein’s theory, enlisted Noether. Her 1918 paper proves two theorems: the first ties global symmetries to conservation laws, the second handles local symmetries (SEP §2.1.2). Both qualifiers matter. “Every symmetry gives a conserved quantity” is, in John Baez’s words, “a half-truth” (Noether’s Theorem in a Nutshell, with a six-line classical proof).

In relativity the first two rows are one fact. A boost mixes t with x, and E with p, so spacetime-translation symmetry yields one four-component law: conservation of four-momentum (E/c, pₓ, p_y, p_z). That answers lesson 3’s opening move. Energy and momentum conservation survive every boost because both come from a single symmetry of spacetime. Susskind’s Theoretical Minimum lectures 4–5 derive the classical table from a Lagrangian.

The quantum version: [Q, H] = 0

In quantum mechanics the theorem takes three lines of the Schrödinger equation. A continuous symmetry is a family of unitaries U(θ) = e^(−iθQ), generated by a Hermitian Q, that leaves H unchanged:

U(θ)† H U(θ) = H   for all θ
   ⇔   [Q, H] ≡ QH − HQ = 0

d|ψ⟩/dt = −(i/ħ) H|ψ⟩
d⟨ψ|/dt = +(i/ħ) ⟨ψ|H

d⟨Q⟩/dt = (i/ħ)⟨ψ|HQ|ψ⟩
        − (i/ħ)⟨ψ|QH|ψ⟩
        = (i/ħ)⟨[H, Q]⟩
        = 0  whenever [Q, H] = 0

The symmetry’s generator is the conserved observable, and since every function of Q also commutes with H, Q’s whole outcome distribution is frozen. Energy is the case Q = H: by unitary evolution U(t) = e^(−iHt/ħ), H generates time translation, and [H, H] = 0. As the SEP (§2.2) puts it, symmetry observables, “commuting with the Hamiltonian of the system, play the role of the conserved quantities.”

Worked example: the precessing qubit, explained by one commutator QM lesson 4 used H = (ħω/2)Z, with the Pauli matrices: 
Z = ⎡1  0⎤  X = ⎡0 1⎤  Y = ⎡0 −i⎤
    ⎣0 −1⎦      ⎣1 0⎦      ⎣i  0⎦

ZH − HZ = (ħω/2)(ZZ − ZZ) = 0
  ⇒ ⟨Z⟩ conserved

ZX − XZ = ⎡ 0 1⎤ − ⎡0 −1⎤ = ⎡ 0 2⎤
          ⎣−1 0⎦   ⎣1  0⎦   ⎣−2 0⎦
        = 2iY ≠ 0
d⟨X⟩/dt = (i/ħ)⟨[H, X]⟩
        = (i/ħ)(iħω)⟨Y⟩ = −ω⟨Y⟩

|ψ(t)⟩ = (e^(−iωt/2)|0⟩
        + e^(iωt/2)|1⟩)/√2
⟨Z⟩ = 0 always
  P(0) = (1 + ⟨Z⟩)/2 = ½
⟨X⟩ = cos ωt
  P(+) = (1 + ⟨X⟩)/2 = cos²(ωt/2)
At ωt = π/3:
  P(0) = 0.5, ⟨X⟩ = 0.5, P(+) = 0.75
The symmetry: e^(−iθZ/2) turns the Bloch sphere about z and leaves H ∝ Z unchanged. If the qubit is a spin-½ in a magnetic field along z, this is literally the rotation row: S_z = (ħ/2)Z is its angular momentum about z. A turn about x changes H, so nothing protects ⟨X⟩.

The same fact explains why lesson 6’s field modes carry momentum labels ħk. In a translation-invariant theory momentum commutes with H, and commuting Hermitian operators share an eigenbasis, so energy eigenstates can have definite momentum.

No symmetry, no guarantee

Remove the symmetry and the guarantee goes with it. A ball rolls over a static hill whose height varies with x. The laws differ from one x to the next, so x-momentum is not conserved, but they are the same at every moment, so energy is.

An expanding universe has no time-translation symmetry, because spacetime itself changes. So there is no general global energy law. In Sean Carroll’s “Energy Is Not Conserved”, photons “redshift, losing energy as space expands,” while vacuum energy (lesson 5’s Λ) keeps a constant density in a growing volume, so its total rises. Big Bang nucleosynthesis predictions depend on radiation losing energy this way. With 1 + z = a_now/a_then (cosmological redshift), a 2.0 eV photon (λ = hc/E ≈ 620 nm) travelling while the universe doubles in size arrives at about 1240 nm and 1.0 eV.

What survives is lesson 5’s local law ∇_μT^μν = 0: at every point, energy only flows. The Weiss–Baez FAQ says it exactly: “The differential form extends with nary a hiccup; not so the integral form.” Static and asymptotically flat spacetimes still have a conserved total energy (same FAQ).

Honestly: the physics is agreed, the words are not Some experts say total energy is conserved once the gravitational field gets an energy, defined with coordinate-dependent pseudo-tensors (FAQ above). Carroll: “We all agree on the science; there are just divergent views on what words to attach to the science.” The opposite overreaction is also wrong. Local conservation holds everywhere, so “GR allows free energy” fails the misuse check.

Gauge symmetry: when a symmetry dictates a force

Now make the phase symmetry stricter: demand the electron field’s laws be unchanged even when the phase varies from point to point. That is a gauge (local) symmetry.

global: ψ(x) → e^(iθ) ψ(x)
  one θ everywhere ⇒ charge conserved
local:  ψ(x) → e^(iθ(x)) ψ(x)
  ∂ψ → e^(iθ)(∂ψ + i(∂θ)ψ)
  the i(∂θ)ψ term is new

Nothing in the free electron’s equation cancels the extra term. Only a new field that shifts along with θ can, and that field is electromagnetism, whose quanta are photons. The SEP (“Quantum Field Theory” §2.3) states it directly: “the requirement of local gauge invariance can only be fulfilled by introducing additional terms, namely those for the electromagnetic field”. With SU(2)×U(1) and SU(3) in place of the phase, the same move yields the W, Z and photon and the gluons, lesson 7’s gauge bosons and forces. Symmetry dictates the form of the interactions.

Gauge symmetry is not another row of the table A local gauge transformation redescribes one physical situation; it never maps it to a different one. Such symmetries are typically regarded as “surplus” or “redundant” structure (SEP §2.5–2.6). They fall under Noether’s second theorem and add no new charge. Charge conservation comes from the global part.

Spontaneous symmetry breaking

Spontaneous symmetry breaking: the laws are symmetric, the lowest-energy state is not. The SEP (§4.2) example is a vertical stick pressed from above. The setup is symmetric about the stick’s axis, but past a critical force the stick bends in some direction. The Higgs field’s ground state likewise has a nonzero value that picks a direction in the electroweak SU(2)×U(1) space. That hides the symmetry and gives the W and Z their masses, while the photon stays massless (lesson 7; Nobel 2013). The laws never stop being symmetric.

Discrete symmetries, and the broken mirror

P, C and T have no small version, so Noether’s theorem does not apply to them. In quantum mechanics, if the mirror operator P commutes with H, the derivation above conserves its eigenvalue, parity = ±1. That is a multiplicative label, not a Noether charge (Wikipedia). Physicists assumed it for thirty years. In 1956 Lee and Yang noted it had never been tested for the weak force. At the end of 1956 Chien-Shiung Wu and a National Bureau of Standards team aligned cobalt-60 nuclei and found that more beta-decay electrons left one end than the other: the experiment’s mirror image does not happen (NIST; SEP §2.4). Lee and Yang won the 1957 Nobel Prize. Symmetries are empirical claims, and they can fail.
