Chords & Triads

In lesson 3 you built major scales on any root and met the idea of a key: a 7-note palette. That lesson ended with a promise: once you know your key, you know which chords belong to it. Today you collect. You’ll stack notes from a key into three-note chords, measure them in half steps, and finish by playing your first chord progression, then moving it to a new key.

Play one, skip one: the triad

A chord is three or more notes played together (musictheory.net). The basic chord is the triad: three notes stacked in thirds (Hutchinson, §6.1). On a scale, stacking thirds means play one, skip one, play one, skip one, play one:

D 
Ethird
F 
Gfifth

Stacked in thirds like this, the bottom note is the root (it names the chord), the middle one is the third, and the top one is the fifth. Because you skip a letter each time, the letters always run every other letter: C–E–G, D–F–A, E–G–B. Three neighbouring notes like C–D–E make a cluster, not a triad.

Try it now Right hand: thumb on Middle C, middle finger on E, little finger on G (fingers 1, 3, 5). Press all three together. Now move the same shape up one white key at a time: D–F–A, then E–G–B. The shape is identical, but listen: the three chords don’t all sound alike.

Two sizes of third

“Third” counts letter names: C, D, E is three letters, so C up to E is a third. But every third in this lesson’s chords comes in one of two sizes when you count keys:

  • A major third is 4 half steps. C→E: C♯, D, D♯, E.
  • A minor third is 3 half steps. D→F: D♯, E, F.

The root-to-fifth span in C–E–G is 7 half steps (C→G), called a perfect fifth (musictheory.net counts it the same way, on the keyboard).

A third is not 3 half steps The name counts letters; the size counts keys. In these chords a third is 3 or 4 half steps, depending on which letters you land on, and that one-key difference is exactly what you’ll hear as minor versus major. Same with the fifth: 5 letters, but 7 half steps in major and minor chords. Check both on lesson 2’s step counter.

Three recipes

W W H W W W H was the recipe for a major scale. A triad’s recipe is just two stacked thirds, bottom gap then top gap:

QualityRecipe5thExampleSound
Major4 + 37C–E–Gbright
Minor3 + 47C–E♭–Gdarker
Diminished3 + 36B–D–Ftense

Major and minor share the same 7-half-step fifth; only the middle note moves, by one key. Diminished squeezes the fifth to 6, a span theorists class as dissonant: less stable, as if it needs to resolve (Open Music Theory, Intervals). The 7-half-step fifth is consonant. Open Music Theory suggests “happy”, “sad” and “scary” as beginner labels for the three, but trust your own ear. In chord symbols a bare letter means major (C), m means minor (Cm) and ° means diminished (B°).

Spell by letters first, then fix with ♯ or ♭: C minor is C–E♭–G, never C–D♯–G, because the third of a C chord must be some kind of E. (Two major thirds, 4 + 4, is not major: it is the rare augmented triad (Hutchinson), which never occurs in a major key.)

Black keys don’t decide the quality D major is D–F♯–A (one black key). D minor is D–F–A (all white). Quality comes from the half-step gaps, never from key colour.

The seven chords of a key

Now combine the two ideas. Take a key’s seven notes and stack a triad on each one, using only notes from that key. These are the key’s diatonic triads (“diatonic” means within the key; Hutchinson, §7.2). C major is all white keys, so start there:

RootTriadGapsChord
CC–E–G4 + 3C major
DD–F–A3 + 4D minor
EE–G–B3 + 4E minor
FF–A–C4 + 3F major
GG–B–D4 + 3G major
AA–C–E3 + 4A minor
BB–D–F3 + 3B diminished

Read the qualities down: Major, minor, minor, Major, Major, minor, diminished. That pattern is the same in every major key (musictheory.net, Open Music Theory), because every major scale comes from the same W W H W W W H recipe, so the gaps fall in the same places.

So every major key holds three minor chords and one diminished chord. The diminished one isn’t a mistake: it belongs to the key, it is just tense and unstable, so composers use it sparingly.

Try it now Walk the 1-3-5 hand shape up the white keys from C to B: C, Dm, Em, F, G, Am, B°. Listen for the three darker chords and the tense one at the top, then land back on C.

Roman numerals: chords by position

Musicians name a key’s chords by the scale position of their root, written as a Roman numeral (musictheory.net). The case shows the quality: uppercase = major, lowercase = minor, lowercase + ° = diminished (Open Music Theory). Here are C major and G major side by side:

IiiiiiIVVvivii°
CCDmEmFGAmB°
GGAmBmCDEmF♯°

G major’s chords come from its scale, G A B C D E F♯. The F♯ turns up in three of them: Bm (B–D–F♯), D (D–F♯–A) and F♯° (F♯–A–C).

Numerals are relative to the key. The G major chord is V in C but I in G. So a progression written in numerals can be lifted out of one key and dropped into any other.

Your first progression

I, IV, V and vi are the most common chords in pop music, and one classic order of them, I–vi–IV–V, is called the doo-wop schema (Open Music Theory, Four-Chord Schemas).

Try it now In C: C – Am – F – G, four slow counts each, then back to C. Now read the same numerals off the G row: G – Em – C – D. Same progression, new key. You just transposed harmony.
The composer’s insight Writing a progression is choosing an order of numerals; choosing a key is choosing where it sounds. The seven chords are your colours: I is home, the minor chords add shade, vii° adds tension. Your hand will jump between chords for now; later, inversions will keep it nearly still.
