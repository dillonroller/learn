# Philosophy Resources

## Knowledge

### Method & the discipline itself
- [Bertrand Russell, _The Problems of Philosophy_, ch. XV "The Value of Philosophy"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5827/5827-h/5827-h.htm#link2HCH0015)
  Public domain, ~15 minutes. The "residue" argument: questions that become definitely answerable get reclassified as sciences. Use for: lesson 1, and any time the "philosophy never settles anything" objection comes up.
- [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Thought Experiments](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/thought-experiment/)
  Thought experiments as "devices of the imagination," their uses and their known weaknesses. Use for: lesson 5's account of where counterexamples come from.
- [SEP — Reflective Equilibrium](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/reflective-equilibrium/)
  The dominant method in moral and political philosophy: mutual adjustment of principles and case judgments. Use for: lesson 5, and the "how do you argue about ethics at all" question in lesson 3.
- [SEP — Fallacies](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/fallacies/)
  Scholarly treatment, including the straw man as a variant of ignoratio elenchi. Use for: lesson 5's principle of charity; complements [[../logic/lessons/0012-informal-fallacies.html]].

### Epistemology
- [SEP — The Analysis of Knowledge](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-analysis/)
  JTB stated precisely, the Gettier cases, and every major repair (no false lemmas, reliabilism, safety, sensitivity) in order. Use for: lesson 2. The single best entry point to the field.
- [SEP — Epistemology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology/)
  Wider survey — justification, skepticism, the structure of knowledge. Use for: background around lesson 2.
- [SEP — Rationalism vs. Empiricism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rationalism-empiricism/)
  The a priori / a posteriori axis. Use for: lesson 2's second half, and the stakes of lesson 12's transcendental arguments.
- [IEP — The Gettier Problem](https://iep.utm.edu/gettier/)
  Faithful summary of Gettier's three-page 1963 paper. Use for: a shorter route to the original argument.
- [SEP — The Problem of Induction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/induction-problem/)
  Hume in full rigor. Use for: lesson 9's Hume entry, and the deductive/inductive distinction inherited from Logic.

### Ethics
- [SEP — Deontological Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-deontological/)
  Defines deontology explicitly against consequentialism and maps where each breaks. Use for: lesson 3 — the most useful single entry because it is comparative.
- [SEP — Consequentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/)
  Classic utilitarianism (Bentham 1789, Mill 1861, Sidgwick 1907), act vs rule, and the standard objections. Use for: lesson 3.
- [SEP — Virtue Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)
  Arete, phronesis, eudaimonia, and why action-guidance is the tradition's weak point. Use for: lesson 3, and as the frame for Stoicism in lesson 8.
- [SEP — Metaethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/metaethics/)
  The layer underneath: what kind of claim a moral claim is. Use for: lesson 3's opening distinction, and the question of what deontic logic's formulas evaluate.

### Metaphysics
- [SEP — Free Will](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/freewill/)
  The full debate including Frankfurt-style cases (Frankfurt 1969). Use for: lesson 4's inconsistent triad.
- [SEP — Compatibilism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/)
  Depth on the dominant position. Use for: lesson 4, and the Stoic position in lesson 8.
- [SEP — Personal Identity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/)
  Locke's psychological criterion, the branching problem, Parfit's response. Use for: lesson 4's second half.
- [SEP — Metaphysics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/metaphysics/)
  What unifies the field, and why the boundary is contested. Use for: framing lesson 4.

### Philosophy of mind
- [SEP — Consciousness](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness/)
  The whole landscape, including Chalmers' 1995 hard problem / easy problems division. Use for: lesson 7.
- [SEP — Qualia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qualia/)
  Mary's room (Jackson 1982) and Nagel's bat (1974) worked through in detail, with the physicalist replies. Use for: lesson 7's two knowledge arguments.
- [SEP — Zombies](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/zombies/)
  The conceivability-to-possibility inference and the attacks on it. Use for: lesson 7's third argument.
- [SEP — Functionalism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/functionalism/)
  Multiple realizability and its consequences. Use for: lesson 7, and anything about machine minds.

### Movements & thinkers
- [SEP — Existentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/existentialism/)
  Extracts arguable claims from a deliberately literary tradition better than any single primary text. Use for: lesson 6. See also [Sartre](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/), [Camus](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/), [Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/).
- [SEP — Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)
  Sections 4.2 and 4.3 (Virtue, Indifferents) are where the historical doctrine and the popular version part company. Use for: lesson 8. See also [Epictetus](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/), [Marcus Aurelius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marcus-aurelius/), [Seneca](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/).
- [IEP — Stoicism](https://iep.utm.edu/stoicism/)
  Good second pass, with more on Stoic physics and logic than lesson 8 covers.
- [SEP — Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)
  The hinge of the modern period: the transcendental turn, and the categorical imperative. Use for: lesson 9, and as background to lesson 12.
- Per-thinker entries used in lesson 9: [Plato](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato/), [Aristotle](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle/), [Descartes](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/descartes/), [Hume](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hume/), [Mill](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mill/), [Nietzsche's Moral and Political Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche-moral-political/), [Wittgenstein](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/), [Private Language](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/private-language/), [Frege](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frege/).

### Logic extensions (lessons 10–12)
- [SEP — Deontic Logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-deontic/)
  Standard deontic logic (SDL) and its catalog of paradoxes. Use for: lesson 10.
- [SEP — Epistemic Logic](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-epistemic/)
  Knowledge/belief operators, factivity, introspection axioms. Use for: lesson 11.
- [SEP — Transcendental Arguments](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/transcendental-arguments/)
  The argument form, its Kantian origin, the standard objections. Use for: lesson 12.

## Wisdom (Communities)

- [r/askphilosophy](https://www.reddit.com/r/askphilosophy/)
  Well-moderated, academic-leaning, insists on citing primary sources. Use for: checking whether an argument reconstruction is fair to the original philosopher — exactly the [principle of charity](lessons/0005-philosophical-argumentation.html#principle-of-charity) check from lesson 5.
- [r/philosophy](https://www.reddit.com/r/philosophy/)
  Larger, more general-audience. Use for: seeing how philosophical arguments get discussed (and misused) outside academia.

## Gaps
- No resource yet identified specifically for non-classical logics (paraconsistent, relevance, fuzzy) — deferred, "maybe eventually" per the user.
- Nothing gathered on political philosophy (Rawls, justice, the state). Lesson 9 touches Mill's harm principle and stops there; gather if the user wants that branch.
- Eastern philosophy is named in the topic index blurb but has no resources and no lesson. Either gather sources or narrow the blurb — currently the index promises more than the curriculum delivers.
- Primary texts are cited through SEP rather than read directly. Fine so far, but if the user wants to read a philosopher rather than read about one, the natural first candidates are Camus' _The Myth of Sisyphus_ and Marcus Aurelius' _Meditations_.
