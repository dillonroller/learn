What is Philosophy? Why It Still Matters

You arrived in this topic sideways — through a "should" in a Logic exercise, which turned into deontic logic. That was the right door. But it skipped the question underneath: what kind of question was "what does 'should' mean?" in the first place, and why couldn't any amount of evidence settle it?

This lesson gives you one portable skill: telling apart the three kinds of question, so you know when you're in a fight about facts, a fight about words, or a fight about values — and stop using the wrong tools on each.

Philosophy is the residue

A common jab at philosophy is that it never settles anything. Bertrand Russell's answer, in 1912, was that this is an artefact of how the field works: as soon as a philosophical question becomes answerable by definite methods, it stops being philosophy and becomes a science.

Russell, The Problems of Philosophy, ch. XV "Those questions which are already capable of definite answers are placed in the sciences, while those only to which, at present, no definite answer can be given, remain to form the residue which is called philosophy." Physics was "natural philosophy" until it got instruments. The study of the mind was philosophy until it became psychology. Formal logic was philosophy until soundness and completeness proofs turned it into mathematics. Read the chapter.

So philosophy isn't the set of questions that are unanswerable in principle. It's the set that no established method currently decides — which is exactly why the reasoning has to carry the whole load. In a field with no experiment to appeal to, the argument is the evidence. That's why you started with Logic: it's the instrument this field doesn't otherwise have.

The three kinds of question

Most unproductive arguments are unproductive because the two sides are answering different kinds of question. Here is the triage:

1. Empirical — settled by observation "Does this additive cause liver damage?" There is a fact of the matter, and a study could in principle find it. If you're arguing about an empirical question, the correct move is to go and look. Arguing harder is a waste.
2. Conceptual — settled by analysis "Does knowing something require being certain of it?" No experiment decides this, because it's a question about what the concept knowledge covers. The method is conceptual analysis: propose a definition, hunt for a case that fits the definition but not the concept, repair, repeat. Lesson 2 is a worked example of this method running for sixty years.
3. Normative — settled by argument from values "Should the additive be banned?" Even with every empirical fact in hand and every concept sharp, this is still open, because it asks what ought to be the case. This is the is-ought gap you already met: no pile of descriptive premises validly yields a prescriptive conclusion without a normative premise to bridge them. Lesson 3 is about where those bridging premises come from.

The three interact — a normative argument usually rests on empirical premises, and both rest on concepts — but they fail in different ways, and the repair for each is different. Misdiagnosing which one you're in is the single most common way a real argument goes nowhere.

What conceptual analysis actually looks like

Conceptual analysis is the characteristic philosophical move, and it has a shape you'll recognise from validity: it runs on counterexamples.

  1. Propose necessary and sufficient conditions for the concept. ("X is knowledge if and only if X is a justified true belief.")
  2. Look for a case that satisfies the conditions but clearly isn't the thing — or is the thing but fails the conditions.
  3. If you find one, the analysis is refuted. Repair it or replace it.

That's a counterexample argument, which you already know: one row of the truth table where the premises hold and the conclusion fails is enough to kill validity. Same logic, applied to definitions instead of inferences. Lesson 5 makes this method explicit and gives you the rest of the toolkit.

Try it: triage the question

For each question, pick the kind — and notice that the giveaway is what would settle it, not what it's about.

Check your understanding

1. On Russell's account, why does philosophy look like it never settles anything?

2. What most reliably tells you a question is normative rather than empirical?

3. Conceptual analysis proceeds mainly by which move?

4. Two people argue whether an AI system "really understands" language. What kind of dispute is this, most likely?

Primary resource

Read chapter XV, "The Value of Philosophy," of Bertrand Russell's The Problems of Philosophy (1912, public domain — about fifteen minutes). It is the best short defence of why the residue is worth working on. For the field's own map of itself, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy is the standard reference this whole course cites.
