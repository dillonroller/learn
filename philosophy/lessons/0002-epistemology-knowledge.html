Epistemology — How Do We Know Anything?

Epistemology is conceptual analysis pointed at one word: knowledge. This lesson runs the analysis end to end — the classic definition, the three-page paper that broke it, and what the field did next. By the end you should be able to take any claim of the form "I know that p" and say precisely which condition is doing the work and which one is failing.

The classic analysis: justified true belief

The standard starting point, abbreviated JTB, says: S knows that p if and only if —

The three conditions Truth — it is true that p. You cannot know something false. (You can be convinced of something false; that isn't knowledge, it's error.)
Belief — S believes that p. Not weak confidence: outright, full belief.
Justification — S is justified in believing that p. Something entitles the belief: evidence, a reliable source, a proof. A lucky guess that happens to be true isn't knowledge.

You have already met the truth condition in formal dress. In lesson 11, Kp → p — factivity — is exactly the truth condition, and the reason Bp → p is not a theorem is exactly that belief lacks it. Epistemic logic is JTB's truth and belief conditions turned into axioms; what it deliberately leaves out is justification, which turns out to be the hard one.

Each condition earns its place by ruling out a failure mode. Drop truth and error counts as knowledge. Drop belief and you "know" things you'd deny. Drop justification and every lucky guess counts. So all three are necessary. The question that broke the field is whether together they're sufficient.

Gettier's counterexample

In 1963, Edmund Gettier published a three-page paper showing they are not. The recipe: take a belief that is justified but false, validly infer something from it that happens to be true for an unrelated reason. The conclusion is justified (it follows from justified premises), true (by luck), and believed — all three conditions met — yet plainly not knowledge.

A Gettier case, in the modern style You look at the office wall clock; it reads 3:00. The clock has been reliable for years, so your belief is well justified. It is in fact 3:00, so your belief is true. You believe it. JTB is satisfied.
But the clock stopped exactly twelve hours ago. You are right by coincidence — you would have believed "3:00" no matter what time it actually was.
Justified. True. Believed. Not knowledge.

Notice the structure: this is the counterexample method, executed perfectly. Gettier did not argue that JTB is implausible. He constructed a case satisfying the definition that the concept clearly excludes — the analysis-killing equivalent of the one truth-table row where the premises are true and the conclusion is false.

What broke, and the repairs

The diagnosis most people accept: JTB doesn't exclude epistemic luck. The belief is true, and justified, but the justification isn't connected to what makes it true. The repairs are attempts to add that connection:

No false lemmas Add a fourth condition: the belief must not be inferred from anything false. Kills the original Gettier cases (they all route through a false premise) — but not later cases built to avoid any false step.
Reliabilism Replace justification with a causal requirement: the belief must be produced by a reliable, truth-conducive process. A stopped clock is not a reliable process, so the case is excluded. Cost: it makes knowledge depend on facts about your environment that you may have no access to.
Safety and sensitivity Require a modal condition, in the vocabulary of modal logic: in nearby possible worlds where you form the belief the same way, it is still true (safety); or, had p been false you would not have believed it (sensitivity). The stopped clock fails both — in nearby worlds it is 3:07 and you believe 3:00 anyway.

None commands consensus. That's the honest state of the field: sixty years on, there is no agreed analysis of knowledge — which is itself evidence for Russell's residue point. What you get from it is not a definition but a diagnostic: when someone claims to know something, ask which condition is shaky, and whether their justification is actually connected to the truth.

Where justification comes from

The second great axis of epistemology: what sources can justify a belief at all?

A priori vs a posteriori A priori justification is independent of experience — you know ¬(P ∧ ¬P), or that all bachelors are unmarried, without checking cases.
A posteriori justification depends on experience — you know the additive causes liver damage only by observing.
Rationalists hold that substantive truths about reality are knowable a priori. Empiricists hold that experience is the only source of substantive knowledge, and a priori truths are true by definition alone — informative about words, not the world.

This is the fault line Kant tried to dissolve, and it powers the argument form in lesson 12: a transcendental argument is a bid for substantive a priori knowledge, derived not from experience but from what experience presupposes. If empiricism is right, that bid can't succeed — which is precisely why the form is contested.
