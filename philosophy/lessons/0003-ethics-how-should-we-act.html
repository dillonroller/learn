Ethics — How Should We Act?

Lesson 10 left you with a hole. The is-ought gap says every argument ending in "should" needs a normative premise, and deontic logic gives you notation for such premises — Op, Pp, Fp — but no account of where they come from. Normative ethics is the attempt to supply them systematically. This lesson gives you the three answers that have survived, and the skill of spotting which one an argument is running on.

Two different questions

Keep these apart or you will argue past people constantly:

Normative ethics — which actions are right, and in virtue of what? This supplies the O/P/F premises. The three families below are answers to this.
Metaethics — what kind of claim is "torture is wrong" in the first place? Is it true or false at all (realism), or an expression of attitude that isn't truth-apt (anti-realism)? Note the stakes for lesson 10: if normative claims aren't truth-apt, it's unclear what deontic logic's formulas are even evaluating.

This lesson is normative ethics. Metaethics is the deeper hole underneath it.

1. Consequentialism — judge by outcomes

An act is right if and only if its consequences are at least as good as those of any alternative. The classic version is utilitarianism — Bentham (1789), Mill (1861), Sidgwick (1907) — where "good" means aggregate well-being.

Its strength: it explains why consequences matter, which every other theory has to accommodate somehow, and it gives a decision procedure that scales to policy. Its cost: it is agent-neutral and aggregative, so it permits — sometimes requires — harming one person to benefit many. The standard pressure test is the transplant surgeon: five patients need organs, one healthy visitor could supply them all. Naive consequentialism says cut. Nearly everyone's judgment says don't.

The standard repair Rule consequentialism: evaluate not individual acts but the rules whose general adoption would produce the best outcomes. "Surgeons may harvest patients" is a catastrophic rule, so the surgeon must not. The objection back: if you follow the rule even in the rare case where breaking it would do more good, you've stopped being a consequentialist; if you don't, you're back to act consequentialism.

2. Deontology — judge by constraints

Some acts are forbidden regardless of consequences. Kant is the central figure: morality consists of categorical imperatives — requirements that bind unconditionally, not "if you want X, do Y." His best-known formulation says to treat people never merely as a means but always also as an end. The transplant surgeon uses the visitor purely as raw material; that's the violation, and no amount of downstream good repairs it.

Its strength: it captures the intuition that people have rights which can't be traded away by arithmetic, and it explains the moral difference between doing and allowing. Its cost: the constraints can look fetishistic at the limit — Kant notoriously held that lying is impermissible even to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding — and conflicts between duties need a tiebreaker the theory struggles to supply.

This is what deontic logic was built for Deontology is the theory whose structure SDL's operators fit most naturally: F(UsingMerelyAsMeans) is a flat prohibition, unconditioned on outcomes. And conflicting duties — Op and O¬p together — are exactly what the axiom Op → Pp rules out, which is why moral dilemmas are a formal problem and not just an emotional one.

3. Virtue ethics — judge by character

Aristotelian in origin, and it changes the question. Instead of "what act is right?", it asks "what would a person of good character do?" Three concepts do the work, per the SEP entry:

Arete, phronesis, eudaimonia Arete (virtue/excellence) — an entrenched disposition of character, not a one-off act. Honesty is a standing trait, not a behaviour you produce on demand.
Phronesis (practical wisdom) — the judgment to know what the situation actually calls for. Without it, good intentions misfire; it is what makes a virtue reliably hit its target.
Eudaimonia (flourishing) — the life that goes well for a human being, which the virtues are constitutive of rather than merely instrumental to.

Its strength: it handles the texture of real moral life — motive, character, relationships — that act-evaluation flattens. Its cost: it is weakest exactly where the others are strongest, at giving action guidance in a hard case. "Do what the virtuous person would do" is not much help when you don't know what that is. This is also the tradition Stoicism belongs to, taken to its most demanding conclusion.

How to actually use these

The useful posture is not picking a team. It's this: the three theories are sensitive to three things that genuinely matter — outcomes, constraints, character — and each is best understood as the claim that its own factor is fundamental and the others derivative. In practice, use them as three lenses on the same case, and treat disagreement between them as a signal that the case is genuinely hard.

The method philosophers use to adjudicate is reflective equilibrium: mutually adjust your principles and your judgments about particular cases until they cohere — revising a principle when it yields a monstrous verdict, and revising a judgment when a principle you have strong reason to hold says you were wrong. It's the main engine of argument in ethics, and lesson 5 unpacks it as a method.
