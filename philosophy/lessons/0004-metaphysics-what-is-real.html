Metaphysics — What is Real?

Metaphysics asks what exists and what it fundamentally is. Its bad reputation comes from the worry that it's unconstrained speculation — but the good arguments in it are tightly constrained, because they work by finding inconsistent triads: three claims each of which looks obviously true, which cannot all be true together. Your skill for this lesson is locating that triad, because once you see it you know exactly what every position in the debate is paying to buy.

The free will problem, as a triad

Three claims. Take any two and the third must go.

The inconsistent triad 1. Determinism. The state of the world at any time, plus the laws of nature, fixes every later state. Nothing else could have happened.
2. Free will requires alternatives. You act freely only if you could have done otherwise.
3. We have free will. At least sometimes, we act freely and are morally responsible.

Each position in the debate is just a choice of which one to drop:

Hard determinism — keeps 1 and 2, drops 3. Determinism is true, free will needs alternatives, so there is no free will. Bites the bullet on moral responsibility.
Libertarianism (metaphysical) — keeps 2 and 3, drops 1. We have genuine alternatives, so determinism must be false somewhere in the causal chain that produces action. Owes an account of how an undetermined event is any more yours than a determined one — randomness doesn't obviously buy control.
Compatibilism — keeps 1 and 3, drops 2. Determinism is true and we are free, because freedom was never about alternative possibilities. It's about acting from your own reasons, unobstructed by coercion or compulsion. The dominant position among philosophers, and the one that trades a metaphysical claim for a conceptual one: it reanalyses "free."

Frankfurt cases — the attack on premise 2

Compatibilism's strongest single move. In a 1969 paper, Harry Frankfurt built thought experiments designed to show you can be morally responsible for an act even though you could not have done otherwise.

The structure Jones decides, entirely on his own, to do something. Unknown to him, a neuroscientist has implanted a device that monitors his deliberation: had Jones started to decide otherwise, the device would have intervened and caused the decision anyway.
The device never fires — Jones does it for his own reasons. So he could not have done otherwise (the device guaranteed the outcome), yet he is plainly responsible (he did it himself, unaided).

If that verdict is right, responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities — it requires the right kind of source, the act flowing from the agent's own deliberation. Premise 2 falls, and the triad dissolves without denying determinism or responsibility. Note the method: this is the Gettier manoeuvre again — a constructed case that satisfies one side of a claimed equivalence and not the other. Lesson 5 names the pattern.

Personal identity — the second classic problem

What makes the person reading this the same person who existed ten years ago, given that essentially every material part has been replaced? Locke's answer was psychological: a person is "a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself... in different times and places." Identity travels with psychological continuity — memory and connected mental life — not with the body.

The pressure test A teleporter scans you atom by atom, destroys the original, and assembles a perfect copy on Mars with every memory intact. Did you travel, or did you die and get replaced?
Now the version that breaks the theory: the machine malfunctions and doesn't destroy the original. There are two of you, psychologically continuous with the person who stepped in. Both cannot be you — identity is one-to-one. So psychological continuity cannot be sufficient for identity, even though nothing about either copy changed.

The branching case is why the debate is live. Some bite the bullet (neither copy is you); some add a no-branching clause (you are the continuous one, provided there's only one — which makes your identity depend on whether a duplicate exists elsewhere, an oddly extrinsic fact); some, following Derek Parfit, conclude that identity was the wrong question all along and what actually matters is psychological continuity itself, whether or not it preserves a single "you."

Why this isn't idle

Both problems cash out. Which horn you take on free will determines whether moral responsibility survives at all — and therefore whether any O claim can properly attach to a person. And personal identity is not academic when the technology gets close: uploading, gradual neural replacement, and split-brain cases are the teleporter argument with hardware. The triad method is what lets you argue about them without flailing.
