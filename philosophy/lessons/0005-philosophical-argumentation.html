Logic & Argumentation — The Philosopher's Toolkit

You already have the formal half of this. Validity and soundness, truth tables, natural deduction, informal fallacies, argument reconstruction — all of it transfers unchanged. This lesson is the other half: the moves philosophers make with that machinery, which the formal curriculum doesn't name. Five of them, and you've already watched four in action in lessons 2 through 4.

From memory first Without scrolling back: what does a Gettier case do to the JTB analysis, and what does a Frankfurt case do to the alternatives requirement? If you can state both, you already have this lesson's central method — it's the same one twice.

1. The counterexample method

The workhorse. A philosophical thesis is nearly always a universal claim in disguise: "knowledge is justified true belief" means ∀x (JTB(x) ↔ Knows(x)); "responsibility requires alternatives" means ∀x (Responsible(x) → CouldHaveDoneOtherwise(x)). From predicate logic you know the cheapest way to defeat a universal: produce one instance where it fails.

The recipe 1. Restate the thesis as an explicit universal or biconditional — this is where most of the work is, since theses are rarely stated that carefully.
2. Construct a case satisfying the left side and failing the right (or vice versa).
3. The case need only be coherent, not actual. Teleporters and neuroscientists with implanted devices are fine.
4. If the intuition about the case is stable under reflection, the thesis is refuted and must be repaired or dropped.

Gettier and Frankfurt are the two cleanest instances in the canon, and you've now seen both. Step 3 is what non-philosophers object to most and it's the load-bearing one: a conceptual claim about what knowledge or responsibility requires is refuted by any coherent case, because it claims to hold necessarily, not usually.

2. Thought experiments

The device that generates those cases. Thought experiments are "devices of the imagination" used to isolate one variable by stipulating away everything else — the same logic as a controlled experiment, minus the lab. The transplant surgeon holds the consequences fixed so only the constraint is in play; Mary's black-and-white room (coming in lesson 7) holds complete physical knowledge fixed so only experience is in play.

Their known weakness: intuitions about wildly unfamiliar cases may be unreliable, and a stipulation can quietly smuggle in the conclusion. The fair challenge to any thought experiment is always one of: is the case actually coherent?, is the intuition stable?, or does the stipulation beg the question?

3. The principle of charity

Reconstruct your opponent's argument in its strongest defensible form before attacking it. This is not politeness — it is self-interest. An argument that only defeats the weak version of a position tells you nothing about whether the position is true, and you have learned nothing.

Its failure mode has a name The straw man — "an opponent's point of view is distorted in order to make it easier to refute." Charity is the discipline that prevents it. Operationally: before objecting, state the other position well enough that its holder would accept your statement of it. If you can't, you don't yet understand it well enough to object.

This is also where Philosophy and Rationality meet — the same move appears there as steelmanning, aimed at everyday disagreement rather than at texts.

4. Reductio ad absurdum

Assume your opponent's thesis, derive a contradiction or an absurdity, conclude the thesis is false. You know the formal rule as negation introduction: from P ⊢ ⊥ infer ¬P. The philosophical version relaxes ⊥ to "absurd" — a conclusion so unacceptable that rejecting the premise is cheaper than accepting it.

That relaxation is exactly where these arguments are fought. A strict reductio is airtight; an "absurdity" reductio is only as strong as the shared judgment that the conclusion really is unacceptable. Your opponent's move is always available: bite the bullet — accept the supposedly absurd conclusion and keep the thesis. Hard determinism is a bullet-bite about moral responsibility; naive consequentialism's defenders sometimes bite on the transplant surgeon. When someone bites, the reductio has failed as a refutation and the disagreement has relocated to the premise about what's absurd.

5. Reflective equilibrium

What you do once the other four have left you with a mess. The method is "the mutual adjustment of principles and judgments in the light of relevant argument and theory" — you hold both your general principles and your case-by-case verdicts as revisable, and adjust in whichever direction has the weaker support, until they cohere.

What it looks like in practice Principle: "maximize aggregate welfare." Case verdict: "the surgeon must not harvest the visitor."
These conflict. Equilibrium is reached by either revising the principle (add a constraint against using persons merely as means) or revising the verdict (conclude the intuition about the surgeon is a bias worth overriding). Both are legitimate; which is correct depends on which has more independent support.
What is not legitimate is holding both and not noticing. That's the state most people's ethical views are actually in.

It is the dominant method in moral and political philosophy, and it's the honest answer to "how do you argue about ethics without a decision procedure?" It is also, obviously, not a proof — which is why transcendental arguments, the subject of lesson 12, are so attractive to people who want something stronger.
