Existentialism

Existentialism has the worst signal-to-noise ratio of anything in this course — it is mostly encountered as a mood rather than a set of claims. It is, in fact, a set of claims, and they are arguable. This lesson extracts four of them and shows what each is actually asserting, so you can evaluate them rather than absorb them.

Worth flagging up front: existentialists chose literary forms — novels, plays, aphorisms — deliberately, on the view that abstract argument distorts the phenomena they were describing. That's a philosophical position about method, and it's partly why the claims need extracting before they can be assessed.

1. Existence precedes essence

The slogan, and the load-bearing claim. A paper knife has an essence before it exists: someone conceived its function, then made it. The existentialist denies that humans work that way. There is no pre-given nature that specifies what you are for. The SEP entry puts it as a conception of the self "not as a substance or thing with some pre-given nature... but as a situated activity or way of being."

What follows If there is no human essence, there is no fact about what you are supposed to be that could settle how you ought to live. Sartre's conclusion: you first exist, and only then, through your choices, become anything in particular. What you are is the running total of what you have done.

Notice this is a metaphysical claim with immediate ethical consequences — which is why it lands harder than most. It is also in direct tension with virtue ethics: Aristotle's whole account rests on there being a human function, which is exactly what this denies.

2. Anxiety is the recognition of freedom

Kierkegaard's contribution, and it is a precise claim rather than a mood. Anxiety (angst) is distinguished from fear: fear has an object — the dog, the deadline — whereas anxiety has none. Its real content is the recognition that nothing external determines your next choice. Standing at a cliff edge, the vertigo is not fear of falling but the awareness that nothing is stopping you from jumping.

So anxiety is not a malfunction to be medicated away; it's the felt form of a true belief about your situation. You are not held in place. That reading is what makes the claim testable-ish and worth arguing about — and note that Kierkegaard's own resolution was religious, a move the later secular existentialists refused while keeping his diagnosis.

3. Bad faith — the flight from freedom

Sartre's sharpest concept. Bad faith (mauvaise foi) is lying to yourself that you have no choice — recasting a decision as a fact about your circumstances, your role, or your nature.

What it sounds like "I can't leave the job — I'm a lawyer." You are a person who has been practising law and could stop.
"That's just how I am." That's how you have been, which is the evidence, not the constraint.
"I had no choice." You had options you found unacceptable, which is a different claim — and a much more defensible one.
Sartre's own example is a waiter performing "being a waiter" a little too precisely, as if the role were a nature rather than something he is choosing, moment to moment, to enact.

The philosophical bite: bad faith is a self-deception, and self-deception is puzzling on its own terms — to hide a truth from yourself you must know it in order to know what to hide. Sartre's insistence that we manage this anyway is one of the most contested parts of his view, and it connects straight to the introspection axioms from lesson 11: bad faith is precisely a counterexample to the assumption that agents have transparent access to their own attitudes.

4. The absurd, and Camus' response

Camus defines the absurd not as a property of the universe but as a relation: the collision between our demand for meaning and a universe that supplies none. Neither term alone is absurd. Take away the demand and there's no conflict; take away the silence and there's no conflict.

That framing sets up the argument of The Myth of Sisyphus, which opens by asking whether the absurd entails suicide. Camus says no, and rejects the other exit too — the "leap" of adopting a belief system that dissolves the tension. Both, on his account, are ways of escaping the absurd rather than facing it. What's left is revolt: living with the contradiction intact and conscious, refusing both resignation and false consolation. Sisyphus, condemned to roll his boulder forever, is the image — and Camus' famous claim is that we must imagine him happy, precisely because his scorn for his fate is something the gods cannot take.

Authenticity — and what it does not mean

Authenticity is owning your choices as yours: acting in awareness that nothing external is making you, and accepting responsibility for the result. It is the opposite of bad faith.

The standard misreading Authenticity is not "follow your feelings" or "be true to your real self" — that self-help reading imports exactly the pre-given essence that claim 1 denies. There is no inner true self to be true to. Authenticity is about the manner in which you choose, not about which option you pick, and it is fully compatible with choosing the conventional thing — as long as you own it as a choice rather than a fate.

Try it: bad faith or not?

Some of these are bad faith. Some are honest statements of a hard constraint. Distinguish them — the line is whether a genuine choice is being disguised as a fact.

Check your understanding

1. What does "existence precedes essence" deny?

2. On Camus' account, where does the absurd reside?

3. Why is authenticity not "being true to your real self"?

4. What makes bad faith philosophically puzzling, not just morally criticizable?

Primary resource

Read SEP — Existentialism, which does the work of extracting arguable claims from a literary tradition better than any single primary text. Then go to the sources through SEP — Sartre, SEP — Camus, and SEP — Kierkegaard. Of the primary works, Camus' The Myth of Sisyphus is the most readable entry point.
