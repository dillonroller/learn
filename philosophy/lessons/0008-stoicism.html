Stoicism — Ancient & Modern

Stoicism is the most widely misrepresented position in this course, because a popular version circulates that keeps the vocabulary and drops the arguments. The historical doctrine is a tightly interlocking system in the virtue ethics tradition, and it is far stranger and more demanding than the productivity-blog version. Your skill for this lesson is telling the two apart.

The central claim: virtue is the only good

Not "the most important good." The only one. Health, wealth, reputation, and the lives of the people you love are, in the technical Stoic sense, indifferent — they make no difference to whether your life goes well, because a good life consists entirely in the excellent functioning of your own rational agency, which nothing external can touch.

This is what the SEP entry calls one of their "most contentious ethical doctrines," alongside the claim that human happiness is fully in our power. The two hang together: if happiness depended on anything external, it would not be fully up to you. The doctrine is not a mood of detachment; it's the conclusion of an argument about what kind of thing a good life could be.

Indifferent does not mean "doesn't matter"

This is where the caricature goes wrong, and the Stoics anticipated it. Indifferents are sorted into preferred (proêgmena) and dispreferred. Health, wealth and reputation are preferred: they have value (axia), they are "according to nature," and it is your proper function to pursue them.

The precise position A virtuous Stoic is, in SEP's words, "not lackadaisical in her attitude toward indifferents... but rather energetic and conscientious in her pursuit of those which are preferred."
So: pursue health vigorously. Work hard for your family's security. Care about your reputation. What you may not do is make your happiness contingent on getting any of it — because that would make the good life hostage to things outside your control.
Ancient critics called this incoherent: how can something be worth energetic pursuit and yet make no difference to how your life goes? That objection is still the main one, and it is a fair fight rather than a knock-down.

What is actually "up to us"

Epictetus' famous division is narrower than the popular version. It is usually paraphrased as "focus on what you control, not on what you don't," which sounds like ordinary prudence. The Stoic claim is much stronger and much more specific: what is up to us is assent to impressions — and essentially nothing else.

Impression, then assent An impression arrives unbidden: "this is a disaster." You do not control that it arrived. What is up to you is whether you assent to it — whether you endorse it as true and let it become your judgment.
Chrysippus' image: push a cylinder and a cone down the same slope with the same shove. The push is the impression, but the rolling is determined by the shape of the object. Likewise the impression is given to you; the assent comes from your own character. This is how the Stoics reconciled a deterministic universe with genuine responsibility — a compatibilist position, worked out centuries before that debate got its modern name.

So the division isn't between things you can and can't influence in the ordinary sense — your body, your job, your reputation are all partly influenceable and all on the "not up to us" side. The only thing fully up to you is the judgment you make.

Emotions are judgments, not weather

The most cognitively interesting Stoic doctrine, and the one with the clearest modern descendants. A passion, for the Stoics, is not a feeling that happens to you — it is an impulse generated by an act of assent, which means it contains a judgment and can therefore be true or false.

Fear isn't a sensation plus a belief; it is the assent to "something bad is coming." Grief is assent to "a great evil has befallen me." Because the judgment is a component rather than a consequence, emotions become directly criticisable: if the judgment is false, the emotion is a mistake, not just an inconvenience. Cognitive behavioural therapy descends fairly directly from this claim, and its founders said so.

This is also the honest source of the "Stoics suppress emotion" charge, and why it's wrong in an interesting way. The Stoics didn't recommend suppressing passions — they held that a passion, being a false judgment, should be dissolved by correcting the judgment. Whether emotions really are judgments is an empirical and conceptual question the modern psychology of emotion is still arguing about.

Pop Stoicism, and what it drops

Three substitutions to watch for "Focus on what you control." Keeps the slogan, drops the claim that assent is the only thing up to us — which is what makes the doctrine radical rather than banal.
"Don't let things bother you." Recommends suppression. The Stoic prescription is correcting a false judgment, which is a different operation with a different failure mode.
"Stoicism makes you more effective." Instrumentalizes it. If virtue is the only good, then Stoicism pursued as a productivity technique is straightforwardly not Stoicism — it makes the external outcome the point.

None of this means the popular version is useless. It means it's a different thing, and you should know which one you're endorsing. Marcus Aurelius' Meditations — a working emperor's private notebook, never written for publication — is the best evidence that the demanding version was actually lived.

Check your understanding

1. What do the Stoics mean by calling health and wealth "indifferent"?

2. On Epictetus' division, what precisely is "up to us"?

3. Why does treating emotions as judgments make them criticisable?

4. Why is "Stoicism will make you more effective at work" in tension with the doctrine?

Primary resource

Read SEP — Stoicism, particularly sections 4.2 (Virtue) and 4.3 (Indifferents), which are where the popular version and the historical one part company. Then SEP — Epictetus for what is up to us, SEP — Marcus Aurelius, and SEP — Seneca. The IEP entry on Stoicism is a good second pass on the physics and logic this lesson sets aside.

Ask your teacher anything. If you've read popular Stoicism and want to know whether a specific claim is in the historical doctrine, bring the exact wording to Claude Code — the substitutions are usually subtle.
