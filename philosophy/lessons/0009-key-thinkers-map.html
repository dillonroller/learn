Key Thinkers: Plato to Wittgenstein

A list of philosophers and their doctrines is nearly useless — it's the kind of thing that produces fluency without retention. So this lesson is organised differently. Each thinker gets one entry, and the entry states the move they made: the question they reframed so thoroughly that everyone after them had to work in the new terms. Where that move appears in this course, it's linked — this lesson doubles as a spaced review of lessons 1 through 8.

Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) — the move: knowledge is not perception

Plato separated appearance from reality and claimed the second is what knowledge is of. His theory of Forms holds that particular things are imperfect instances of perfect abstract universals; the geometer's triangle is the real object of knowledge, the drawn one only an approximation. The cave allegory is the picture of this: what we take for reality is shadow.

Live inheritance: the demand that knowledge be more than true opinion is the ancestor of the entire JTB analysis — and Plato's Theaetetus already tries and rejects "knowledge is true belief with an account." SEP — Plato.

Aristotle (384–322 BCE) — the move: systematize everything, starting from the particulars

Aristotle inverted his teacher: the reality of a thing is in the thing, not in a separate realm. He then invented the form of inquiry itself — logic as a formal discipline (the syllogism stood essentially unchallenged for two millennia, until Frege), plus biology, physics, poetics, rhetoric, and ethics.

Live inheritance: virtue ethics is his, including arete, phronesis and eudaimonia — and his claim that humans have a characteristic function is exactly what existentialism denies. SEP — Aristotle.

Descartes (1596–1650) — the move: start from doubt

Descartes asked what survives if you deliberately doubt everything doubtable — senses, memory, even mathematics under the hypothesis of a systematic deceiver. His answer: the doubting itself. Cogito, ergo sum. From that fixed point he tried to rebuild knowledge on foundations, which set the agenda for modern epistemology.

Live inheritance: he also bequeathed the mind-body problem. Cartesian dualism — mind and body as distinct substances — is rejected almost universally now, but the hard problem is the same puzzle without the substances. SEP — Descartes.

Hume (1711–1776) — the move: follow empiricism all the way down

Hume took the empiricist principle seriously enough to see what it destroys. If all substantive knowledge comes from experience, then: we never observe causation, only constant conjunction; induction has no non-circular justification, since arguing that it has worked before assumes the very principle at issue; and the self is not observed either, only a bundle of perceptions.

Live inheritance: the is-ought gap is his, and it is the reason lesson 3 exists. Hume is the most destructive philosopher on this list, and every subsequent epistemology is partly a response to him. SEP — Hume.

Kant (1724–1804) — the move: ask what experience presupposes

Kant said Hume woke him from his "dogmatic slumber," and his response is the hinge of modern philosophy. Instead of asking how the mind conforms to objects, ask how objects must conform to the mind's structure in order to be experienced at all. Space, time and causality are not discovered in the world; they are the conditions under which anything can be an object of experience for us. Hence the distinction between things as they appear (phenomena) and things as they are in themselves (noumena), which we cannot reach.

Live inheritance: this is the transcendental argument of lesson 12 in its original setting, and the attempt to rescue substantive a priori knowledge from Hume. His categorical imperative is the backbone of deontology. SEP — Kant.

Mill (1806–1873) — the move: make the moral calculus public

Mill inherited Bentham's utilitarianism and made it defensible: distinguishing higher from lower pleasures, answering the charge that it is a doctrine fit for swine, and extending it into a political philosophy. On Liberty's harm principle — that the only legitimate ground for coercing someone is preventing harm to others — is his, and remains the default framing of most liberal political argument.

Live inheritance: consequentialism in its standard form, and the policy reasoning that goes with it. SEP — Mill.

Nietzsche (1844–1900) — the move: ask where a value came from

Nietzsche's innovation is methodological: genealogy. Rather than asking whether a moral claim is true, ask how that particular valuation came to seem obvious — what it was for, whom it served. His claim that "God is dead" is a diagnosis rather than a celebration: the collapse of a shared foundation for values, with nihilism as the predicted consequence.

Live inheritance: the crisis existentialism was formed to answer, and a permanent challenge to metaethics — though note the genealogical move is not itself an argument that a value is false, and treating it as one is a version of the genetic fallacy from Logic lesson 12. SEP — Nietzsche's Moral and Political Philosophy.

Wittgenstein (1889–1951) — the move: suspect the question

The only philosopher on this list to produce two major philosophies and refute the first with the second.

Early (Tractatus, 1921): language pictures the world; propositions have sense by sharing logical form with facts; everything else — ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life — is literally unsayable. "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent."

Late (Philosophical Investigations, 1953): meaning is not picturing, it is use. Words get their sense from their role in a form of life, and philosophical problems typically arise when language is taken outside the context that gave it meaning. Hence the private language argument: a language whose terms refer to purely private sensations, unverifiable by anyone else, could not have rules — and a "rule" no one could ever check is not a rule. That argument bears directly on qualia, and on whether talk of purely private experience is as coherent as it feels.

Live inheritance: the diagnostic posture itself — before answering a philosophical question, ask whether it's well formed. It is the main rival to the conceptual analysis programme this course has mostly assumed. SEP — Wittgenstein.

One more, because your Logic curriculum runs through him Gottlob Frege (1848–1925) — the move: replace the syllogism. Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced quantifiers and variables, giving us predicate logic and ending Aristotle's two-thousand-year monopoly. Nearly everything in the Logic curriculum after lesson 5 is downstream of him, as is Gödel's work on what such systems can prove. SEP — Frege.

Check your understanding

2. What does the genealogical method ask about a moral value?

4. Why is it a mistake to treat a genealogy of a value as refuting it?

Primary resource

The single most useful entry to read in full is SEP — Kant, because the transcendental turn is the hinge this whole map rotates on: everything before is what he was answering, and much of what follows is a reaction to him. Each thinker above links to their own SEP entry for depth.
