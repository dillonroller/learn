Joint Distributions, Covariance & Correlation

Every random variable so far has been studied alone, and that left three debts. Lesson 2 used "uncorrelated" without defining it. Lesson 3 promised "a correlation term" in the variance of a sum without deriving it. Lesson 4's CLT assumed "independent" random variables without defining independence. All three need one tool — a description of two random variables together. This lesson builds it, then asks what correlation does and does not tell you.

Joint distributions — two functions on one Ω

Lesson 1 made a random variable a deterministic function X : Ω → ℝ. Two of them on the same Ω are one function ω ↦ (X(ω), Y(ω)) into ℝ², and that pair has a distribution like anything else.

Joint distribution For discrete X and Y, the joint PMF is p(x,y) = P(X=x, Y=y), the probability of the event {ω : X(ω)=x and Y(ω)=y}. Lay it out as a table — a row per x, a column per y, nonnegative entries summing to 1. (A continuous pair has a joint density f(x,y) that you integrate instead of summing; every example here stays discrete.)

Running example: a bag holds cards 1, 2, 3. Draw two in order; X = first card, Y = second — once with replacement, once without.

Marginals — sum out the other variable

Marginal distribution pX(x) = Σᵧ p(x,y) and pY(y) = Σₓ p(x,y). The events {X=x, Y=y} for different y are disjoint and make up {X=x}, so this is just additivity. The name is literal: the sums sit in the table's margins.

Compare the margins: identical. In both experiments each card is uniform on {1,2,3} — even the second card drawn without replacement, which deserves a second look (its column: 1/6 + 1/6 + 0 = 1/3). Yet the tables differ. The marginals do not determine the joint. Everything about X alone plus everything about Y alone says nothing about how they move together.

Dividing a row by its margin gives the conditional PMF p(y | x) = p(x,y) / pX(x) — Rationality's P(A | B) applied to {Y=y} and {X=x}. Without replacement, p(y | X=1) = (0, ½, ½): the first card changes the odds for the second.

Independence, finally defined

Independent random variables X and Y are independent iff p(x,y) = pX(x) · pY(y) for every cell — equivalently, every event about X is independent of every event about Y (MIT 18.05, Reading 7a). This is what lesson 3's Binomial trials and lesson 4's "iid" quietly assumed.

The test is mechanical: multiply the margins, compare with each cell. With replacement every cell is 1/9 = 1/3 · 1/3: independent. Without replacement one cell rules it out: p(1,1) = 0 ≠ 1/9. One failing cell proves dependence; independence needs them all. The consequence you will use most, independence ⇒ E[XY] = E[X]·E[Y], is the double sum for the expectation factoring:

E[XY] = Σₓ Σᵧ x·y·pX(x)·pY(y) = (Σₓ x·pX(x)) · (Σᵧ y·pY(y)) = E[X]·E[Y]

Covariance — do the deviations share a sign?

Covariance With μX = E[X], μY = E[Y]: Cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)]. Shortcut: Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] − E[X]·E[Y].

The shortcut is linearity with the μ's as constants: E[XY − μYX − μXY + μXμY] = E[XY] − μXμY. Set Y = X to recover lesson 2's Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²: Cov(X,X) = Var(X). The sign is a vote over quadrants — outcomes where X and Y sit on the same side of their means add positive products, outcomes where they straddle add negative ones.

Without replacement: E[XY] = (1·2 + 1·3 + 2·1 + 2·3 + 3·1 + 3·2)/6 = 11/3 and E[X]E[Y] = 4, so Cov = −1/3 — a high first card leaves lower cards behind. Two properties, both from linearity (MIT 18.05, Reading 7b proves them), carry the rest: covariance is symmetric and bilinear, Cov(X₁+X₂, Y) = Cov(X₁,Y) + Cov(X₂,Y); and Cov(aX+b, cY+d) = ac·Cov(X,Y).

The variance of a sum — the promised correlation term

Variance of a sum For any X and Y, Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X,Y). In general, Var(ΣXᵢ) = ΣVar(Xᵢ) + 2·Σi<j Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ).

Proof, one line of bilinearity: Var(X+Y) = Cov(X+Y, X+Y) = Cov(X,X) + 2·Cov(X,Y) + Cov(Y,Y). This sharpens lesson 3's rule: variances add exactly when Cov(X,Y) = 0, i.e. when X and Y are uncorrelated. Independence is sufficient, not necessary — lesson 4's Var(Sₙ) = nσ² needed only pairwise-uncorrelated terms (Pishro-Nik §5.3.1). On the cards without replacement: 2/3 + 2/3 + 2·(−1/3) = 2/3. Directly: the pair drawn is {1,2}, {1,3} or {2,3}, so X+Y is uniform on {3,4,5}, variance 2/3. With replacement it is 4/3 — and E[X+Y] = 4 either way.

How much of the joint does each question need? E[X+Y]: nothing, the marginals suffice. Var(X+Y): one number, Cov(X,Y). The full distribution of X+Y: the whole table.
Payoff: the shuffled deck's variance Lesson 2's fixed-point indicators Xᵢ for a shuffled n-card deck are dependent, so lesson 3's rule could not give their variance. Now: Var(Xᵢ) = (1/n)(1 − 1/n), and Cov(Xᵢ,Xⱼ) = 1/(n(n−1)) − 1/n² for i ≠ j. The n(n−1) ordered pairs contribute 1/n in total, so Var = (1 − 1/n) + 1/n = 1. Mean 1, variance 1: the signature of Poisson(1), which the count does approach as n grows (rencontres numbers).
"Var(X − Y) = Var(X) − Var(Y)" — No. Var(−Y) = Var(Y) and Cov(X,−Y) = −Cov(X,Y), so Var(X − Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) − 2·Cov(X,Y). Variances never subtract; only covariance can cancel spread. The extremes: Var(X + X) = 4·Var(X), Var(X − X) = 0.

Correlation — covariance without units

Correlation coefficient ρ(X,Y) = Cov(X,Y) / (σX σY), for positive SDs. It is the covariance of the standardized variables (X − μX)/σX and (Y − μY)/σY, it is unitless, and ρ(aX+b, cY+d) = ρ(X,Y) for a, c > 0. Uncorrelated means ρ = 0, i.e. Cov = 0.

Why −1 ≤ ρ ≤ 1: apply the variance-of-a-sum formula to the standardized variables, each of variance 1.

0 ≤ Var(X/σX − Y/σY) = 2 − 2ρ   ⇒   ρ ≤ 1
0 ≤ Var(X/σX + Y/σY) = 2 + 2ρ   ⇒   ρ ≥ −1

Equality is the informative case. ρ = 1 forces the first variance to 0, so X/σX − Y/σY is constant (with probability 1) and Y = aX + b with a > 0; ρ = −1 gives a < 0. So |ρ| = 1 exactly for perfect linear relationships — the first hint that ρ measures linearity, not dependence. Cards without replacement: ρ = (−1/3)/(2/3) = −1/2.

What correlation does not tell you

"Uncorrelated means independent" — No. Let X be uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and Y = X². Then E[XY] = E[X³] = 0 = E[X]·E[Y], so Cov = 0 — yet Y is a function of X. One cell proves it: P(X=0, Y=0) = 1/3, but P(X=0)·P(Y=0) = 1/9. The implication runs one way: independent ⇒ uncorrelated. (Even two Normal variables can be uncorrelated and dependent; zero correlation guarantees independence when the pair is jointly Normal.)
"Correlation measures how strongly Y depends on X" — No, only how linearly. Above, Y is a function of X yet ρ = 0: the contributions from x = −1 and x = 1 cancel exactly.
"ρ = 0.8 means Y rises 0.8 per unit of X" — No. ρ is unitless; the best-fit slope is ρ·σY/σX (simple linear regression), in Y-units per X-unit. All four datasets of Anscombe's quartet have r ≈ 0.816 and fitted slope 0.500, yet look nothing alike: a noisy line, a clean curve, a perfect line with one outlier, a vertical stack plus one far point. The Datasaurus Dozen goes further: a dinosaur-shaped point cloud and twelve shapes morphed from it, all with the same means, SDs and correlation to two decimals. (A dataset's r is just ρ of the distribution putting probability 1/N on each of its N points.)
"Correlation implies causation" — No. Roll three dice Z, D₁, D₂ and let X = Z + D₁, Y = Z + D₂. Bilinearity gives Cov(X,Y) = Var(Z) = 35/12 and ρ = ½, though neither affects the other — Z drives both. Reichenbach's common cause principle: if A and B are correlated and neither causes the other, a common cause exists that, once conditioned on, makes them independent. Telling the cases apart is causal inference, a subject of its own — and Hume's old point: we observe conjunction, never causation.

Check your understanding

1. You compute Cov(X,Y) = 0 from a joint table. Which conclusion is justified?

2. Lesson 3 said variances add for independent X and Y. What is the exact condition for Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y)?

3. Two measured quantities X and Y have ρ = 0.9 across a large dataset. Which reading is justified?

4. You know the full marginal PMF of X and the full marginal PMF of Y, and nothing else. What can you compute?

Practice before next lesson

Flip a fair coin three times; X = heads in flips 1–2, Y = heads in flips 2–3. (1) Build the 3×3 joint table from the 8 outcomes and read off both marginals. (2) Find a cell that proves dependence. (3) Compute Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y] from the table. (4) Get Var(X+Y) from the variance-of-a-sum formula, then again directly from X + Y = F₁ + 2F₂ + F₃, a sum of independent pieces where lesson 3's rule applies. The two routes must agree.
