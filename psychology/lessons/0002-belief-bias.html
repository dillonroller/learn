Belief Bias — When "Sounds True" Beats "Is Valid"

In Lesson 1, content helped: dress the Wason task up as a drinking-age rule and success jumps from ~10% to ~75%. This lesson is the mirror image — content that hurts. Logic’s normative rule is that validity is about form, not content; the experiments below measure how far real reasoners — including people who just passed a logic exam — drift from it when a conclusion sounds true. Last lesson’s practice had you hunt for the not-Q case that could break a rule. Today’s skill is the same hunt, aimed at arguments.

Belief bias — grading the argument by its conclusion The tendency to judge a deductive argument valid when its conclusion is believable and invalid when it isn’t, whether or not the conclusion actually follows. It collapses two questions — does it follow? and is it true? — that Logic 1’s validity/soundness matrix keeps apart.

The experiment: four arguments, one skeleton

Evans, Barston & Pollard (1983) asked people whether a syllogism’s conclusion could be logically deduced from its premises, crossing validity with believability. The materials, as laid out in Todd Davies’s Stanford slides:

  • Valid, believable: No police dogs are vicious. Some highly trained dogs are vicious. ∴ Some highly trained dogs are not police dogs.
  • Valid, unbelievable: No nutritional things are inexpensive. Some vitamin tablets are inexpensive. ∴ Some vitamin tablets are not nutritional.
  • Invalid, believable: No addictive things are inexpensive. Some cigarettes are inexpensive. ∴ Some addictive things are not cigarettes.
  • Invalid, unbelievable: No millionaires are hard workers. Some rich people are hard workers. ∴ Some millionaires are not rich people.

Ignore the words and look at the shape. All four share the premises No A are B and Some C are B; the conclusion is either Some C are not A (V) or Some A are not C (I). With the universal and existential quantifiers, only the order of terms in the conclusion separates valid from invalid:

P1  ∀x(A(x) → ¬B(x))
P2  ∃x(C(x) ∧ B(x))
V   ∃x(C(x) ∧ ¬A(x))   valid
I   ∃x(A(x) ∧ ¬C(x))   invalid

V is forced: the C that is B can’t be an A, since no A is B. I isn’t: imagine the only addictive things are pricey cigarettes and the cheap ones aren’t addictive — premises true, conclusion false. Odd but coherent, which is all a counterexample needs. Averaged across the paper’s three experiments, acceptance rates were:

BelievableUnbelievable
Valid89%no conflict56%conflict
Invalid71%conflict10%no conflict
Belief-logic conflict — when truth and validity point opposite ways In no-conflict problems belief and logic agree (valid-believable: accept; invalid-unbelievable: reject), and people were right 89% and 90% of the time. In conflict problems they disagree (valid-unbelievable; invalid-believable), and accuracy fell to 56% and 29%. The 2×2 design exists to pull those two situations apart.

The asymmetry: belief hits invalid arguments hardest

Logic × belief interaction — belief bias is larger on invalid arguments Believability moved acceptance by 61 points for invalid arguments (71% vs 10%) but only 33 for valid ones (89% vs 56%). The bias isn’t symmetric: the dangerous case is a bad argument for something you already think is true.

Don’t over-correct into “people can’t do logic.” Averaged over belief, valid arguments were accepted 73% of the time and invalid ones 41% — a big effect of form. What fails is the specific case where form and belief collide.

Selective scrutiny — believable conclusions get waved through Evans et al.’s model, as Stupple & Ball (2008) summarize it: believable conclusions are “responded to heuristically (and simply accepted),” while unbelievable ones “motivate more rigorous analytic processing.” Checking is reserved for unwelcome conclusions, so an invalid-but-believable argument never meets the counterexample that would sink it.

Later work refined this: people spend as long on believable problems as unbelievable ones — what differs is the direction of effort. Mercier & Sperber (2011, §3.3): if participants agree with a conclusion they try to confirm it, if they disagree they try to prove it wrong — and only the second search finds counterexamples. So “just take more time” is no fix by itself. (Exactly how belief and logic compete — in sequence or in parallel — is still debated.)

Why it looks like System 1 vs System 2

The dual-process reading: the belief verdict is the fast System 1 answer; checking validity is System 2 work that must override it. Three findings fit:

  • Starve it of time. When Evans & Curtis-Holmes (2005) required answers within 10 seconds, belief bias rose and logically correct decisions fell.
  • Starve it of working memory. In De Neys (2006), a secondary task that tied up working memory hurt accuracy on conflict problems but not when belief already cued the right answer.
  • The monitor is lax. Kahneman’s Nobel lecture calls System 2’s monitoring “normally quite lax”: 47 of 93 Princeton students gave the intuitive wrong answer to the bat-and-ball puzzle.

Notice what is missing: stakes. Nobody cares whether some millionaires are rich, yet the effect is large on neutral items like that — which is what separates belief bias from motivated reasoning.

Does logic training protect you?

Macchi et al. (2019) tested 130 undergraduates, 80 of whom had passed a 64-hour university logic course (they did the task on exam day). Asked “Does the conclusion follow from the premises?”, 96% of trained and 94% of untrained students correctly accepted a valid-believable syllogism. For All flowers fade. All roses fade. ∴ All roses are flowers., only 10% of trained vs 4% of untrained rejected it — no significant difference.

Training-transfer gap — the competence is there; the trigger isn’t Experiment 2 took a fresh sample (162 students, 61 trained) and changed one phrase. “Does the conclusion necessarily follow?” lifted correct rejections to 77% (trained) and 66% (untrained); “Can you infer, following the criteria of Logic…?” gave 67% vs 41% — the one wording where training clearly paid off. The paper reports the same pattern on the Wason task (Bagassi & Macchi 2016): 13% of logic-trained participants vs 5% of untrained. Training supplies the rules; something has to signal that it is time to use them.

Macchi’s team reads this partly as pragmatics: in conversation, “roses are flowers” is a cooperative, obviously true remark, and people need telling that a logic task plays by different rules.

A cue is not an immunity Macchi’s own review of earlier work is sobering: instructions stressing that only logically necessary conclusions count were mostly unsuccessful, and when very long instructions explaining validity got 83% of people to reject invalid-believable syllogisms (Newstead et al. 1992), a replication managed only 56% (Evans et al. 1994). Wording helps; it doesn’t reliably remove the pull. Hence three steps below, not one.

The content-blind check

Content-blind check — necessarily? / swap the terms / hunt a counterexample 1. Ask “necessarily?” Not “is the conclusion true?” but “could the premises be true and this false?”
2. Strip the content. Swap the content words for letters or nonsense words, or symbolize in FOL. The believability pull leaves with the content.
3. Hunt a counterexample — hardest when you like the conclusion. Build a coherent world, or substitute new terms into the same form, with true premises and a false conclusion. Found one: invalid. Can’t, and can see why: valid. Only then, separately, ask whether the premises are true.

Step 3 is Philosophy’s counterexample recipe turned on yourself. On Logic 1’s matrix, the penguin argument (“all birds can fly…”) is valid-unbelievable: a false conclusion from a valid form means a false premise — valid but unsound. The cigarette item is the reverse trap: “some addictive things are not cigarettes” is true, and the argument is still invalid.

Where this connects back to Logic and Rationality

The 2×2 design is Logic 1’s validity/soundness matrix turned into an experiment. The template All A are B; All C are B; ∴ All C are A is the quantified cousin of affirming the consequent. On the Rationality side, keep the neighbours distinct: confirmation bias skews which evidence you seek; belief bias skews your verdict on premises already in front of you; motivated reasoning needs a stake, belief bias doesn’t. All three share a search that runs in the direction of the initial belief — the free pass for liked conclusions that Lesson 3 finds behind persuasive fallacies.

Practice before next lesson

This week, catch one argument whose conclusion you agree with — a product pitch (“all good laptops have long battery life; this one does; so it’s a good laptop”), a workplace claim, a friend’s advice. Write it as premises and conclusion, then run the content-blind check: ask “necessarily?”, swap in letters, and try hard to build a world where the premises hold and the conclusion fails. Note how strong the pull to wave it through was — that pull is what you are learning to see.
