Why Fallacies Persuade

Logic lesson 12 catalogued informal fallacies and what’s wrong with each. It never asked the follow-up: if these arguments are bad, why do they work? You already have the machinery. System 1 answers fast and unchecked, and belief bias showed those answers overriding validity. This lesson points the same lens at the everyday catalog — and four mechanisms cover the whole list.

Appearance condition — a fallacy is an argument that seems better than it is A mistake counts as a fallacy only if it has, in Powers’s phrase, “an appearance, however quickly seen through, of being valid” (SEP, Fallacies §4.2). On this traditional view, shared by Walton, a careless slip isn’t a fallacy; a seductive one is. (It is contested: van Eemeren and Grootendorst keep appearances out of the definition precisely because they belong to psychology.) What the mistake is “is not a question of psychology” (§4.4) — that’s Logic’s job. Why it seems good is a fact about minds. So naming the fallacy (normative) doesn’t explain why it worked on you (descriptive).

Mechanism 1: answering an easier question

Attribute substitution — System 1 swaps a hard question for an easy one Kahneman and Frederick (2002) define a heuristic judgment as assessing a hard target attribute via a related one that “comes more readily to mind” (Kahneman’s Nobel lecture, §4). His own example is Rationality’s availability heuristic: asked what share of long-distance relationships break up within a year, you answer do swift breakups come readily to mind? In the Linda problem, rankings by resemblance and by probability correlated at .99 — two groups, one easier question. And “the judge is normally unaware of the substitution.”

Now apply it to arguments. “Is this argument good?” is hard; nearby questions are effortless. Is the source impressive? (appeal to authority) Do I respect this person? (ad hominem) Do lots of people agree? (appeal to popularity) Which of these two is better? (false dilemma — Kahneman suggests “passive adoption of the formulation given” is a general principle.) Each easy answer usually correlates with the hard one, which is why System 1 leans on it. And because the swap is invisible from inside, knowing its name doesn’t switch it off — compare the training-transfer gap from belief bias. Only a deliberate System 2 check (“setting the speaker aside, what’s the evidence?”) restores the real question.

Peripheral route — persuasion by cue rather than by argument The Elaboration Likelihood Model describes the same swap: a central route weighs arguments, a peripheral route runs on cues like source expertise and attractiveness, and motivation and ability decide which route dominates. In Petty, Cacioppo & Goldman (1981), students got strong or weak arguments from a high- or low-expertise source. When the issue was personally relevant, attitudes tracked argument quality; when it wasn’t, they tracked expertise. Distraction works the same way: in Petty, Wells & Brock (1976) it boosted an easily countered message by blocking the counterarguing. Consensus behaves like expertise: in the sister heuristic-systematic model, Axsom, Yates & Chaiken (1987) found an overheard audience’s enthusiasm swayed listeners only under low involvement; under high involvement only argument quality mattered.
Rule: Low motivation or ability → cues carry the weight. High → argument quality does.

Mechanism 2: fluency feels like truth

Illusory truth effect — repetition makes a claim feel true In Hasher, Goldstein & Toppino (1977), students rated 60 plausible statements in three sessions two weeks apart; the 20 that recurred were rated truer each session, while new ones were not. Repetition makes a statement easier to process, and that fluency gets read as truth. Fazio et al. (2015) found it “even when participants knew better”: people who knew the Scottish skirt is a kilt still rated “A sari is the name of the short plaid skirt worn by Scots” truer after repetition — knowledge neglect.

That is a plausible reason circular reasoning lands (an inference from the effect, not a direct test): the conclusion comes back dressed as a premise, so by the time you weigh it, it already feels familiar. Slogans run on the same fuel. The parallel with belief bias: there, logic training didn’t fire without a cue; here, stored knowledge doesn’t.

Mechanism 3: coherence beats probability

Conjunction fallacy — a more detailed story feels likelier, but can’t be The axioms give P(A ∧ B) ≤ P(A), yet 89% of people ranking by probability put Linda as “a bank teller and active in the feminist movement” above “a bank teller” (Nobel lecture, §4). In Tversky & Kahneman (1983), half of 245 students judged “a massive flood somewhere in North America in 1983, in which more than 1000 people drown”; the other half judged the strictly narrower “an earthquake in California sometime in 1983, causing a flood in which more than 1000 people drown.” The narrower event got the higher estimate (geometric means 3.1% vs 2.2%), and professional forecasters erred the same way. Details improve a story’s coherence, “although such additions can only lower probability.”

That is the engine of the slippery slope. The chain is valid hypothetical syllogism; the persuasion lives in the links. Each step makes the story more vivid while the odds of sliding all the way multiply down: if each link is 70% likely given the one before, three links give 0.7³ ≈ 34% and five give 0.7⁵ ≈ 17%. Hasty generalization runs on a cousin, the “law of small numbers”: the intuition that a small sample represents its population as well as a large one (representativeness). Two rude people feel like a pattern because they look like one.

Mechanism 4: a free pass for your own side

Selective laziness — strict with others’ arguments, lenient with your own Mercier and Sperber’s argumentative theory says reasoning exists to make arguments that convince others and to vet the ones aimed at you — so we should be sharp critics of incoming arguments and lazy critics of our own. Trouche, Johansson, Hall & Mercier (2016) tested it with choice blindness. Participants solved short syllogisms and wrote an argument for each answer, then judged “other participants’” arguments — one secretly their own. Of those who missed the swap, 56% and 58% (two experiments) rejected their own argument: 66% when it defended an invalid answer, 41% a valid one (Experiment 1). Same words, sharper criticism, once they seemed to be someone else’s (Lombrozo’s summary).

Put to the test, people generally spot argumentative fallacies aimed at them and are “convinced to the degree that they are well grounded” (Mercier & Sperber 2011, §2.1); logic problems even get easier inside a real argument, echoing the content effect on the Wason task. The blind spot is your own side: belief bias’s selective scrutiny, generalised. It is how Mercier and Sperber explain confirmation bias, and motivated reasoning is the same pull felt from inside. The counter is a disguise test: put the argument in the mouth of someone you disagree with, or flip it to the conclusion you dislike. If it suddenly looks thin, it was thin before.

When the “fallacy” is a decent argument

Bayesian account of fallacies — fallacies come in degrees Hahn and Oaksford (2007) treat the classic fallacies as “argument forms that can be differentially strong, dependent on their content,” and find people’s strength ratings for circular arguments, slippery slopes and arguments from ignorance sensitive to the factors a Bayesian model predicts. A fallacy is then an argument whose content gives it low probability (SEP §3.5); a slope is as strong as the chance of really sliding all the way (Corner, Hahn & Oaksford 2006, via Mercier & Sperber §2.1).

This is why the shortcuts exist: experts usually are more reliable, and testimony from someone with a stake usually is weaker. The cue is valid on average, so System 1 uses it — and so does the fallacy. Logic’s “careful” callouts drew the line (conflict of interest isn’t ad hominem; a slope is fine when every link holds). The Bayesian version makes it quantitative: update proportionally on the cue, and grade the result as an inductive argument — by strength, not validity.

The fallacy → mechanism map

The mapping is many-to-many; this is the main pull for each, and when the argument is actually decent.

Fallacy or biasWhat System 1 answers instead
Appeal to authority“Is the source impressive?” Decent: a real expert in this field, no stake.
Appeal to popularity“Do lots of people agree?” Decent: many judged independently and saw the facts.
Ad hominem“Do I rate this person?” Decent: weighing testimony when the stake is real.
Straw man“Is this weaker version wrong?” plus an own-side pass. Antidote: steelmanning.
False dilemma“Which of these two?” — the formulation given, adopted.
Slippery slope“Does the story hang together?” Decent: every link probable.
Circular reasoning“Does this feel familiar?” In the arguer: motivated reasoning.
Hasty generalization“Does it look like a pattern?” Plus noticed confirmations, own-side pass.
Availability heuristic“Do examples come easily?” for “how often?”
Confirmation biasA reasoner built to argue its own side: selective laziness.

Where this connects back to Logic and Rationality

Logic 12’s formal/informal split explains the fit: an informal fallacy’s flaw lives in its content, and content is what System 1 reads — who said it, how familiar it sounds, whether the story hangs together, whose side it’s on. Rationality’s bias lesson said the bias behind a fallacy tells you why this person erred now; these mechanisms are that bridge, and they keep its pairings (confirmation bias → hasty generalization, motivated reasoning → circular reasoning). Caveat: the effects are classic findings, but the unifying theories are contested — Mercier and Sperber’s paper ran with 24 peer commentaries.

Try it: diagnose the pull

Part A. In each scenario someone was persuaded. Pick the mechanism doing the persuading — you know the fallacy names, so they’re left out.

Part B: predict the persuasion. Each item varies the conditions, or asks whether the “fallacy” is decent evidence. One is a belief-bias retrieval check — use the content-blind check.

Check your understanding

1. Logic’s fallacy catalog already tells you what is wrong with an appeal to authority. What does the psychological account add?

2. In Fazio et al. (2015), people who knew the Scottish skirt is a kilt still rated “A sari is the name of the short plaid skirt worn by Scots” truer after repetition. What does that show?

3. A slippery-slope argument adds two more vivid steps between the first move and the disaster. What happens to the probability of reaching the disaster along that chain?

4. In Trouche et al. (2016), people unknowingly judged their own earlier argument as if someone else had written it. Among those who missed the swap, what happened?

Practice before next lesson

This is the course’s last lesson, so make it a habit. This week, catch one argument you found convincing — an ad, a colleague’s pitch, your own case for a purchase — and run the disguise test: flip it to the opposite conclusion, or imagine it from someone you usually disagree with. If it now looks weak, name which mechanism was carrying it. If it still looks strong, check whether it’s a decent inductive argument in the Bayesian sense. Write both down; that is this course’s goal in practice: saying what is happening cognitively when a fallacy works.
