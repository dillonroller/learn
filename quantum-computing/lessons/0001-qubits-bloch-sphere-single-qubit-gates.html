Qubits, the Bloch Sphere & Single-Qubit Gates

You already know a qubit is a vector: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, normalized so |α|² + |β|² = 1. Quantum computing asks a different question than quantum mechanics does about that same object: not "what does nature do with it," but "what can I do to it, on purpose, to compute something." The things you're allowed to do are called gates. This lesson gives you the simplest possible picture of a qubit — a point on a sphere — and the gates that move it around.

One qubit, one sphere

A general qubit state has four real numbers hiding inside it: the real and imaginary parts of α and β. Two of those four are not physically meaningful:

  • Normalization removes one degree of freedom — |α|² + |β|² = 1 is one equation constraining four numbers.
  • Global phase removes another. Multiplying the entire state by eiγ — i.e. |ψ⟩ and eiγ|ψ⟩ — changes no Born-rule probability at all, for any measurement. Two states that differ only by an overall phase are the same physical state.

That leaves exactly two real numbers. Use the leftover global-phase freedom to make α real and non-negative, and write the state as:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + e^(iφ) sin(θ/2)|1⟩       θ ∈ [0, π],  φ ∈ [0, 2π)

θ and φ are exactly a latitude and a longitude. Plot them and every possible single-qubit state becomes one point on the surface of a sphere — the Bloch sphere. |0⟩ sits at the north pole (θ=0), |1⟩ at the south pole (θ=π). The equator (θ=π/2) is the set of equal-magnitude superpositions, distinguished only by the relative phase φ between α and β — the phase that does matter, unlike the global phase you just factored away.

Two poles you'll see everywhere On the equator, φ=0 gives |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and φ=π gives |−⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2. These aren't exotic — they're the states you get by applying the very first gate below to |0⟩ and |1⟩.

A gate is a rotation of the sphere

A single-qubit gate is a 2×2 matrix U applied to the state vector: |ψ'⟩ = U|ψ⟩. For this to always output a valid, normalized state — for any input state — U must be unitary: U†U = I, where U† is the conjugate transpose. This is the exact same requirement that governs time evolution under the Schrödinger equation; a gate is nothing but a deliberately chosen, short burst of unitary evolution.

Geometrically, every unitary on one qubit is a rotation of the Bloch sphere — it moves points on the sphere to other points on the sphere, preserving distances and angles, never stretching or collapsing anything. That single geometric fact is worth holding onto: whatever a single-qubit gate "does," it's a rotation, full stop.

The Pauli gates: X, Y, Z

The three Pauli gates are 180° rotations about the sphere's three axes:

X = |0 1|     180° about the x-axis. Swaps |0⟩ ↔ |1⟩ — the "bit flip."
    |1 0|

Z = |1  0|    180° about the z-axis. Leaves |0⟩ alone, sends |1⟩ → −|1⟩ — the "phase flip."
    |0 -1|

Y = |0 -i|    180° about the y-axis. Does both at once (up to a phase).
    |i  0|

Notice Z does nothing visible if you only ever measure in the {|0⟩,|1⟩} basis — |1⟩ and −|1⟩ give identical Born-rule probabilities on their own. Z's effect only shows up once that qubit is combined with another via superposition or entanglement, which is exactly why phase gates matter so much once you reach interference in lesson 4.

The Hadamard gate: the superposition-maker

H = (1/√2) |1  1|
           |1 -1|

H is a 180° rotation about the diagonal axis halfway between x and z. Its whole job is turning basis states into equator states and back:

Worked example: H|0⟩ H|0⟩ = (1/√2)|1 1; 1 -1| · |1; 0| = (1/√2)(|0⟩ + |1⟩) = |+⟩. One gate, applied to a definite state, produces an equal superposition. This is the standard first step of almost every quantum algorithm you'll meet in this course: start in |0⟩, apply H (or many H's) to spread the state out before doing something clever with the phases.

H is also its own inverse: H(H|ψ⟩) = |ψ⟩ for any |ψ⟩ — check it on |0⟩ above and you'll get back |0⟩. That's not a coincidence; every quantum gate must be invertible (more on exactly why in lesson 3), and H happens to be its own inverse specifically.

S and T: fine-grained phase gates

Z is a 180° turn about the z-axis. S and T are smaller turns about the same axis — 90° and 45° respectively:

S = |1 0|      quarter turn about z.  S² = Z.
    |0 i|

T = |1 0    |  eighth turn about z.   T² = S.
    |0 e^(iπ/4)|

Both leave |0⟩ completely untouched and multiply |1⟩'s amplitude by a phase. T in particular will matter later: {H, T, CNOT} turns out to be enough gates to approximate any quantum circuit at all — the subject of lesson 3.

Circuit notation

A single qubit is drawn as a horizontal wire, read left to right, with gates as boxes on it:

|0⟩ ──[H]──[T]──  (measure)

That's the whole notation for one qubit: prepare a starting state on the left (almost always |0⟩), apply gates in time order left-to-right, measure on the right. Lesson 2 adds a second wire and the gates that connect them.

Practice before next lesson

By hand: compute Z|+⟩ (where |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2) and simplify it in terms of |+⟩ and |−⟩. You should find Z swaps |+⟩ and |−⟩ — the mirror image of how X swaps |0⟩ and |1⟩. That symmetry (X flips the computational basis, Z flips the Hadamard basis) is a preview of a pattern that keeps recurring in quantum circuits.
