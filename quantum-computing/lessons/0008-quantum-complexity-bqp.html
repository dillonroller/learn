Quantum Complexity: BQP and the Limits of Quantum Speedup

You have just finished Shor's period finding: factoring in polynomial time, exponentially faster than any known classical method. Earlier you met the opposite kind of result: Grover's √N is a ceiling. Both are true. This lesson adds the map that holds them together: the class BQP, which statements about it are theorems and which are beliefs, and a four-bin sort for any claimed quantum speedup. It also keeps the promise made in the Deutsch-Jozsa lesson to show why quantum computers are not known to solve NP-complete problems efficiently.

BQP: what "efficient" means for a quantum computer

Complexity classes are sets of decision problems: is x in the language L? Since any quantum computation is a circuit over a universal gate set, L is in BQP (bounded-error quantum polynomial time) when some circuit family C₁, C₂, … satisfies (de Wolf §13.2; Complexity Zoo):

1. Size      C_n has poly(n) gates from a fixed
             universal set, on poly(n) qubits.
2. Uniform   a classical poly-time program,
             given n, prints C_n.
3. Bounded   for every x of length n, measuring
   error     qubit 1 of C_n|x⟩|0…0⟩ gives L(x)
             with probability ≥ 2/3.

BPP is the same with a classical randomized circuit, and P is the deterministic version with no error. The 2/3 is arbitrary: run the circuit k times and take a majority vote, and the error falls exponentially in k, so any constant above 1/2 gives the same class. BQP is not "what a quantum computer solves exactly"; bounded error is built in.

Worked example: majority vote at p = 2/3 Exact binomial tails, error = P(at most half of k runs are right): 
runs k    1       11      51      101
error    33.3%   12.2%   0.69%   0.027%
101 runs turn one-in-three into about one in 3,700, a constant overhead that never moves a problem into or out of BQP.

Uniformity matters because otherwise C_n could have answers wired in, such as a bit recording whether the n-th Turing machine halts, which no algorithm can compute (de Wolf §13.2, footnote 1).

The map: P ⊆ BPP ⊆ BQP ⊆ PP ⊆ PSPACE

Every inclusion in this chain is a theorem:

  • P ⊆ BPP: a randomized algorithm may ignore its coins.
  • BPP ⊆ BQP: the Toffoli gate runs any classical circuit reversibly, and H on |0⟩ then a measurement is a fair coin.
  • BQP ⊆ PP: Adleman, DeMarrais and Huang, 1997 (Complexity Zoo). PP, probabilistic time with no gap requirement, sits inside PSPACE. Take this one as stated.
  • BQP ⊆ PSPACE: a classical machine can compute any output probability of a quantum circuit in polynomial memory and exponential time. The proof is interference written as arithmetic.
Worked example: an amplitude as a sum over paths Insert every intermediate basis state z: ⟨y|U₂U₁|x⟩ = Σ_z ⟨y|U₂|z⟩⟨z|U₁|x⟩. With U₁ = U₂ = H and x = 0, each path 0 → z → y multiplies two Hadamard entries: 
y = 0:  z=0   (1/√2)(1/√2)  = +1/2
        z=1   (1/√2)(1/√2)  = +1/2   sum 1
y = 1:  z=0   (1/√2)(1/√2)  = +1/2
        z=1   (−1/√2)(1/√2) = −1/2   sum 0
The two paths into |1⟩ cancel: that is interference, and HH|0⟩ = |0⟩. A classical program can do this for any circuit, visiting paths one at a time and keeping only the current path and a running total. In a Hadamard-plus-Toffoli circuit (a gate set that already suffices for all of BQP) only Hadamards branch, so h Hadamards give at most 2ʰ paths: exponential time, polynomial memory (de Wolf §13.3).

Compare the brute force of storing every amplitude: 50 qubits are 2⁵⁰ amplitudes, about 18 petabytes at 16 bytes each. The path sum trades memory for time, and every known classical simulation of general circuits takes exponential time (de Wolf ch. 9, footnote 3).

Keep this in perspective: nobody can yet prove quantum is faster If P = PSPACE, the chain collapses: BQP ⊆ PSPACE = P ⊆ BPP. So a proof of BPP ≠ BQP would also prove P ≠ PSPACE, open since the 1960s (de Wolf §13.2; Aaronson, Lecture 24). "Quantum computers are exponentially faster" is a well-supported belief, resting on decades without fast classical factoring and on oracle separations, which are proofs only relative to a black box. Oracles cut both ways: relative to some, P = BQP (Complexity Zoo).
Factoring an n-bit N, best known classical:2^(O(n^(1/3)·(log n)^(2/3))), heuristicShor:poly(n)

Factoring (as a decision problem) is in BQP by Shor, and in NP ∩ coNP, as the cryptography lesson on factoring hardness noted. That is why most computer scientists think it is not NP-complete (Aaronson): if a coNP problem were NP-complete, all of NP would reduce to it, forcing NP = coNP. Shor tells you nothing about SAT.

BQP vs NP: open in both directions

Is NP ⊆ BQP? Believed not. The main evidence is Grover's optimality in complexity language: Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani proved that relative to a random oracle, with probability 1, NP cannot be solved by a quantum machine in time o(2^(n/2)). Grover over all 2ⁿ assignments meets that bound, so brute-force quantum SAT costs about 2^(n/2), the square root of an exponential, which is still an exponential.

Worked example: Grover against an n-variable formula 
              n = 100          n = 200
brute force   2^100 ≈ 1.3e30   2^200 ≈ 1.6e60
Grover        (π/4)·2^50       (π/4)·2^100
                ≈ 8.8e14         ≈ 1.0e30
Doubling the number of variables cancels the whole quadratic gain: Grover at n = 200 (about 1.0e30 iterations) costs roughly what brute force costs at n = 100 (1.3e30).
The trap: an oracle result is not a proof about SAT BBBV rules out quantum algorithms that treat the formula as a black box. A real formula has structure a cleverer algorithm might exploit; none is known (de Wolf §13.2). A proof that NP ⊄ BQP would imply P ≠ NP (Aaronson, Lecture 24).

Is BQP ⊆ NP? Also open. A quantum computer might solve problems whose answers have no short classical certificate. Raz and Tal (2018) built an oracle relative to which BQP is not even in the polynomial hierarchy PH, which contains NP. De Wolf: "it may well be that BQP and NP are incomparable". But an oracle result is not a real problem: Aaronson notes there are no good concrete candidates among ordinary decision problems yet (Lecture 24), so the field has no settled expectation here.

PROVEN    P ⊆ BPP ⊆ BQP ⊆ PP ⊆ PSPACE
          factoring, discrete log ∈ BQP
BELIEVED  BPP ≠ BQP   (proof ⇒ P ≠ PSPACE)
          NP ⊄ BQP    (proof ⇒ P ≠ NP)
          factoring not NP-complete
OPEN      BQP ⊆ NP ?  (oracle: BQP ⊄ PH)

Provable limits: parity, majority and total functions

Running time is where almost nothing is proven. In the query model you count only oracle calls, and lower bounds are real theorems, some showing quantum provably cannot help much.

Parity. Given N bits through an oracle, compute x₁ ⊕ … ⊕ x_N. Classically you must read every bit, since an unread bit could flip the answer. Deutsch's algorithm learns x₁ ⊕ x₂ with one query (set f(0) = x₁, f(1) = x₂; it outputs f(0) ⊕ f(1)). Run it on pairs.

Worked example: N = 8 
x        1 0 | 1 1 | 0 1 | 1 1
Deutsch    1  |  0  |  1  |  0    (4 queries)
parity   1 ⊕ 0 ⊕ 1 ⊕ 0 = 0       (six 1s: even)
Four quantum queries against eight classical, and N/2 is optimal even with bounded error (Beals et al. 1998; Aaronson, Lecture 24). A factor of 2 is a constant: no asymptotic speedup.

Majority needs order N quantum queries, so again no asymptotic gain (Aaronson, Lecture 24). For any symmetric function (one depending only on the number of 1s) the quantum speedup is at most quadratic, and Grover's OR is the quadratic case (de Wolf ch. 11).

Total functions are defined on every input, with no promise. Beals et al. proved that T quantum queries can be replaced by O(T⁶) deterministic ones; Aaronson, Ben-David, Kothari and Tal (2020) tightened this to O(T⁴), matching a known separation up to log factors. Polynomial, never exponential, so every exponential query gap needs a promise, like Deutsch-Jozsa's "constant or balanced" or Simon's hidden XOR mask (de Wolf ch. 3).

What these theorems are not claiming Query bounds count oracle calls, not running time. Deutsch-Jozsa's gap is only against deterministic algorithms: a randomized one decides it with two queries and success probability 2/3 (de Wolf ch. 2, exercise 12). Even Simon's exponential separation from randomized algorithms "does not prove an exponential separation in the usual circuit model, because we are counting queries rather than ordinary operations" (de Wolf §3.3.2).

Where quantum speedups come from

Every claimed speedup lands in one of four bins. Interference is the mechanism in all of them; what differs is how much structure it has to work with.

  1. Exponential over the best known classical algorithm, from structure. The abelian hidden subgroup problem (period finding, factoring, discrete log) falls to the QFT (de Wolf ch. 6). Simulating quantum dynamics, Feynman's original motivation, is the other family (de Wolf ch. 9). "Best known" is the honest qualifier: no gap is proven against every classical algorithm. The non-abelian case is open: graph isomorphism is a hidden subgroup problem over the symmetric group with no efficient quantum algorithm, and classically it is already quasi-polynomial (Babai 2015), so it belongs in the fourth bin, not this one.
  2. Quadratic, provably no better for black boxes, from amplitude amplification. Error correction makes every logical gate expensive, and Babbush et al. conclude quadratic speedups will not yield a runtime advantage on early fault-tolerant machines; quartic ones look far more practical.
  3. None asymptotically: parity, majority, anything whose quantum lower bound matches classical up to a constant.
  4. No proven speedup: heuristics and open cases, such as graph isomorphism above, VQE and QAOA, and quantum annealing. As of 2026 the evidence is benchmarks and projections; a March 2026 QAOA paper calls beating strong classical solvers "an open challenge" and can only project a crossover on future error-corrected hardware (He et al.).

Dequantization. Problems change bins. Kerenidis and Prakash's quantum recommendation system ran in time polylogarithmic in the matrix size, a leading candidate for an exponential machine-learning speedup. Ewin Tang (2018) gave a classical algorithm with the same kind of data access (ℓ²-norm sampling) running in poly(k)·log(mn) time, only polynomially slower. Dequantization means matching a claimed quantum speedup with a classical algorithm given equivalent input access; Aaronson saw it as "killing off one of [quantum machine learning's] flagship applications" (blog). Sampling-based "supremacy" experiments are a different kind of claim, covered under reading quantum-advantage claims.

Computability vs complexity: which thesis is at stake

The Church-Turing thesis (anything physically computable is Turing-computable) survives: the path sum is a slow but finite Turing-machine simulation of any circuit. No quantum computer decides halting (Logic: computability) or computes Kolmogorov complexity.

The extended Church-Turing thesis adds that classical simulation of any reasonable model costs at most a polynomial blowup (Aaronson, Lecture 1). That is what quantum computing threatens: if factoring is in BQP but not BPP, the thesis is false (de Wolf §5.1 calls it the "strong" thesis). Aaronson calls quantum computing "a glaring counterexample", but it rests on factoring ∉ BPP, the same unproven belief as before.
