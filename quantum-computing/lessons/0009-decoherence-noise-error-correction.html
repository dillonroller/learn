Decoherence, Noise & Quantum Error Correction

Every algorithm so far, from Deutsch-Jozsa's interference to Shor's period finding, assumed that relative phases stay where the gates put them. Lesson 7 then priced RSA-2048 at a bit over 4,096 perfect qubits, yet every published estimate counts far more physical ones. This lesson explains the gap: real qubits lose their phases through decoherence, and one idea protects quantum information anyway, measuring parities of qubits instead of the qubits. You will encode a qubit in the 3-qubit bit-flip code, decode any single bit flip from its syndrome, and compute when encoding helps.

Decoherence: entanglement you did not ask for

A qubit is never perfectly isolated. Stray fields, defects in the chip and the control wiring all interact with it, and an interaction is unitary evolution of qubit plus environment. If the environment ends up in a different state depending on whether the qubit is |0⟩ or |1⟩:

(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) ⊗ |E⟩
  ──▶  α|0⟩|E₀⟩ + β|1⟩|E₁⟩

This is Quantum Mechanics lesson 4's remark that measurement is just entanglement with a measuring device, except that nobody built the device and nobody reads it. Unless |E₀⟩ = |E₁⟩, the qubit is no longer in a product state with its surroundings.

Worked example: interference gets multiplied by ⟨E₀|E₁⟩ Measure the qubit in the {|+⟩, |−⟩} basis. Projecting onto |+⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 leaves (α|E₀⟩ + β|E₁⟩)/√2. Its squared norm, using |α|² + |β|² = 1 and then α = β = 1/√2, is 
P(+) = ½ + Re(α*β·⟨E₀|E₁⟩)
     = ½ + ½·Re⟨E₀|E₁⟩

⟨E₀|E₁⟩  P(+)  environment has
1        1     learned nothing
0.6      0.8   a partial record
0        0.5   a full record
The odds of |0⟩ and |1⟩ never move. What vanishes is the cross term, the part interference runs on. That is why noise kills quantum speedup in particular: Deutsch-Jozsa, Grover and the QFT all work by making amplitudes cancel.
What decoherence is not It is not the state being destroyed, and not a physical collapse. The joint evolution stays unitary; the phase information now sits in correlations with an environment you cannot control or measure. Decoherence explains why interference disappears; as the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on decoherence puts it, it “does not explain why we do observe measurement results in the first place”, so on its own it does not solve the measurement problem.

T1 and T2: two ways the Bloch vector shrinks

A qubit entangled with its environment has no pure state of its own, so its Bloch vector moves inside the sphere and gets shorter. Two time scales describe how (Krantz et al. 2019, §III.B):

  • T1, energy relaxation. An excited |1⟩ gives its energy to the environment and decays to |0⟩. The Bloch vector slides toward the north pole, and P(1) decays like e−t/T1.
  • T2, dephasing. The relative phase φ between |0⟩ and |1⟩ drifts randomly. The equatorial part of the Bloch vector shrinks toward the z-axis, even if the z-component stays put.

They are not independent. Relaxation also erases phase: once the qubit has decayed to |0⟩ there is no phase left. The cross term involves the amplitude β, not the probability |β|², so it decays at half the rate of P(1), and the rates add: Γ₂ = Γ₁/2 + Γφ, where Γ = 1/T and Γφ ≥ 0 is pure dephasing. Hence T2 ≤ 2T1.

Worked example: precession with a jittery frequency In Quantum Mechanics lesson 4, |+⟩ evolving under H = (ℏω/2)Z gave P(+) = cos²(ωt/2) = (1 + cos ωt)/2, a clean fringe. Now let the splitting fluctuate between runs: ω̄ + δ half the time, ω̄ − δ the other half. Averaging the two fringes: 
P(+) = ½ + ½·cos(ω̄t)·cos(δt)

δt        0     π/4    π/2
cos(δt)   1     0.71   0
At t = π/(2δ) the two kinds of run are π out of phase, and P(+) = ½ for every ω̄: the |+⟩ test is a coin. Each run was a pure state; what kills the fringe is the spread of phases across runs, Bloch vectors fanning out around the equator. A continuous Gaussian spread of width σ gives contrast e−σ²t²/2, which never revives. (Drift that stays constant within a run can be undone by an echo pulse.)

Typical T1 and T2 values, and how many gates fit inside them, belong to lesson 10.

Why quantum error correction looks impossible

Classical machines fight noise with redundancy: store bit b as bbb and take a majority vote. Three things block the quantum version (de Wolf, §21.3):

  1. No copying. The no-cloning theorem forbids |ψ⟩ ↦ |ψ⟩|ψ⟩|ψ⟩.
  2. Looking destroys. Checking a qubit by measuring it collapses α|0⟩ + β|1⟩.
  3. Errors are continuous. A bit is flipped or not; a qubit can be rotated by any small angle about any axis.

Each has an answer; the next four sections give them.

The 3-qubit bit-flip code: entangle, don't copy

Lesson 3 promised that error correction cannot make three copies and vote. Here is what it does instead: two CNOTs from the data qubit q0 onto two fresh qubits.

q0: α|0⟩+β|1⟩ ──●──●──
                │  │
q1: |0⟩       ──⊕──┼──
                   │
q2: |0⟩       ─────⊕──

output: α|000⟩ + β|111⟩

Each CNOT copies the basis label: |0⟩|00⟩ ↦ |000⟩ and |1⟩|00⟩ ↦ |111⟩, and linearity does the rest. Compare three genuine copies:

encoded: α|000⟩ + β|111⟩
         (2 terms, entangled)
copies:  (α|0⟩+β|1⟩)⊗3
       = α³|000⟩ + … + β³|111⟩
         (8 terms, a product state)

The two agree only when α or β is 0, the basis-state case that the no-cloning proof showed a copier can handle. No single qubit of the encoded state carries α and β. Measure any one of them and you see |0⟩ with probability |α|² and no phase at all. The information lives in the correlations among all three.

Syndrome measurement: ask about parities, never values

Suppose X hits one qubit. Instead of reading the qubits, ask two yes/no questions: do q0 and q1 agree? Do q1 and q2 agree? These are the parity checks Z₀Z₁ and Z₁Z₂. Each is computed into a fresh ancilla by two CNOTs, and only the ancillas are measured:

q0:     ──●────────────
          │
q1:     ──┼──●──●──────
          │  │  │
q2:     ──┼──┼──┼──●───
          │  │  │  │
a0: |0⟩ ──⊕──⊕──┼──┼──[M] s_a
                │  │
a1: |0⟩ ────────⊕──⊕──[M] s_b

The pair (s_a, s_b) = (q0⊕q1, q1⊕q2) is the error syndrome; 1 means “these two differ”. Writing X₀ for X on q0:

error  state            syndrome  fix
none   α|000⟩+β|111⟩    (0,0)     none
X₀     α|100⟩+β|011⟩    (1,0)     X₀
X₁     α|010⟩+β|101⟩    (1,1)     X₁
X₂     α|001⟩+β|110⟩    (0,1)     X₂

In every row, both branches have the same parities: with X on q1, |010⟩ and |101⟩ both have q0 ≠ q1 and q1 ≠ q2. So after the ancilla CNOTs the joint state factors as (α|010⟩ + β|101⟩) ⊗ |11⟩, and the ancillas read (1,1) with probability 1. A measurement with a certain outcome does not disturb the state: α and β survive, and the syndrome says where the error is but nothing about what is encoded. Apply X to q1 and you are back at α|000⟩ + β|111⟩.

The trap: “to check for errors, measure the data” Measuring q0 in the computational basis returns 0 with probability |α|² and leaves |000⟩, or 1 and leaves |111⟩. Either way the superposition you were protecting is gone. The parity checks work because they ask a question that both branches answer the same way.

Phase flips, and Shor's 9-qubit code

Now let Z hit any of the three qubits. Z leaves |0⟩ alone and negates |1⟩, so all three cases give the same state:

Z on q0, q1 or q2:  α|000⟩ − β|111⟩
syndrome:           (0,0)

The code reports “no error” while the encoded qubit has taken a phase flip. Lesson 1 warned that Z does nothing visible in the {|0⟩,|1⟩} basis; parity checks in that basis cannot see it either.

The fix is a change of basis. The Hadamard turns a phase flip into a bit flip (HZH = X), so apply H to all three qubits after encoding: α|+++⟩ + β|−−−⟩. A Z now swaps one |+⟩ ↔ |−⟩, and the checks X₀X₁ and X₁X₂ locate it with the same table. This phase-flip code is blind to X instead. Shor's 9-qubit code nests the two: encode with the phase-flip code, then encode each of its three qubits with the bit-flip code (IBM, “The 9-qubit Shor code”):

|0_L⟩ = (|000⟩ + |111⟩)⊗3 / 2√2
|1_L⟩ = (|000⟩ − |111⟩)⊗3 / 2√2

Bit-flip checks inside each block catch an X; comparing the signs of the three blocks catches a Z. The code corrects an X, a Z, or both on any one of the nine qubits.

Continuous errors become discrete

The third obstacle falls to linearity. I, X, Y, Z span all 2×2 matrices, so any single-qubit error is E = c₀I + c₁X + c₂Y + c₃Z, and Y = iXZ is a bit flip and a phase flip together (de Wolf, §21.3). Measuring the syndrome forces the error to pick one term.

Worked example: a 0.2-radian over-rotation A faulty gate applies Rx(ε) = cos(ε/2)·I − i·sin(ε/2)·X to q0 of α|000⟩ + β|111⟩, with ε = 0.2: 
  cos(0.1)·(α|000⟩+β|111⟩)
      syndrome (0,0)
− i·sin(0.1)·(α|100⟩+β|011⟩)
      syndrome (1,0)
Measure the syndrome. With probability cos²(0.1) = 0.990 it reads (0,0) and the state collapses to the clean codeword: the measurement itself removed the error. With probability sin²(0.1) = 0.010 it reads (1,0) and the state collapses to a plain X on q0, times a global phase −i that changes nothing; apply X to q0. Either way you get α|000⟩ + β|111⟩ back exactly, and neither probability depends on α or β.

So a code that corrects X, Z and their product Y on a qubit corrects every single-qubit error, small rotations included (IBM, “Discretization of errors”). Decoherence is covered too: expanding the qubit-environment entanglement in Paulis gives Pauli errors each tagged with an environment state, and the syndrome measurement picks one; de Wolf (§21.3, footnote 2) notes that such non-unitary errors are correctable too.

Logical qubits, and when encoding pays

The encoded qubit, spanned by |0_L⟩ = |000⟩ and |1_L⟩ = |111⟩, is a logical qubit. The three qubits that carry it are physical qubits. Encoding is not free, because three qubits give errors three places to land. Let each physical qubit independently suffer an X with probability p, with everything else perfect. One flip is fixed; two fool the decoder: X on q0 and q1 gives syndrome (0,1), and its “correction” on q2 completes a logical X. So the logical error rate is

P_L = P(2 flips) + P(3 flips)
    = 3p²(1−p) + p³ = 3p² − 2p³

P_L < p  ⟺  p(2p−1)(p−1) > 0
         ⟺  p < 1/2
Worked example: below and above break-even 
p      P_L         verdict
0.01   2.98×10⁻⁴   34× better
0.1    0.028       3.6× better
0.5    0.5         break-even
0.6    0.648       worse
Below break-even you can repeat the trick: encode each of the three qubits again (9 qubits), and again (27). Each level roughly squares the error. At p = 0.01: 
k   qubits 3ᵏ   P_L
0   1           10⁻²
1   3           2.98×10⁻⁴
2   9           2.66×10⁻⁷
3   27          2.13×10⁻¹³
Qubits grow exponentially in k while the error falls doubly exponentially, about (3p)2ᵏ/3. So k = O(log log T) levels push a T-step computation's error below 1/T, at only polylogarithmic overhead (de Wolf, §21.2 and §21.6).

The threshold theorem

The real theorem drops the toy's free lunches: syndrome extraction is itself noisy, and gates must act on encoded qubits without turning one error into two. With all of that counted:

Threshold theorem A quantum circuit of size N can be run with high accuracy on noisy hardware, provided the error probability at each location is below a fixed constant pth > 0. The noisy circuit has size O(N·logc N) (IBM, “The threshold theorem”; proved in 1996–97 by Aharonov and Ben-Or and by Knill, Laflamme and Zurek, among others).
Keep this in perspective: the threshold is not 50%, and not one number The toy break-even of 1/2 assumed perfect checks and only X errors. If a correction round has L places where a fault can occur, a pair of faults among them can sink it, so P_L grows like L²p² and break-even drops to roughly 1/L² (de Wolf, §21.6). The 9-qubit Shor code already stops helping above about 3.23% (same IBM page). The threshold depends on the noise model, the gate set and the code: surface-code estimates are about 0.1% to 1% (IBM), and the best rigorous lower bounds are around 0.1% (de Wolf). It is a theorem under assumptions, chiefly that errors are not strongly correlated, not a promise about any machine.

The experimental evidence is recent (as of 2026). In a landmark 2024 experiment, Google ran surface-code memories (a code laid out on a 2D grid of qubits; its details are beyond this course) at distances 3, 5 and 7. Below threshold a distance-d surface code's logical error scales as εd ∝ (p/pth)(d+1)/2, so each step d → d + 2 divides it by Λ ≈ pth/p. Google measured Λ = 2.14 ± 0.02. The distance-7 logical qubit used 101 physical qubits (49 data, 48 measurement, 4 for leakage removal), failed 0.143% of cycles, and outlived the chip's best physical qubit 2.4-fold (Google Quantum AI, Nature 2025).

Surface code, distance d:2d² − 1 physical qubits10⁻⁶ per cycle at Λ = 2.14:d = 27, 1,457 qubits

That badge, from the same paper's extrapolation, is the price of one good logical qubit, and the paper puts many applications' needs below 10⁻¹⁰. The multiplier is why lesson 7's RSA-2048 estimates run from 20 million down to under 100,000 physical qubits for one to a few thousand logical ones. Error correction is not error mitigation, which post-processes noisy results at a sampling cost that grows exponentially with circuit size; that is lesson 10's subject.
