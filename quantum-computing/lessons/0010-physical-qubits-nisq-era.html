Physical Qubits & the NISQ Era

Lesson 9 gave you the theory of fragility: qubits relax and dephase on timescales T1 and T2, and error correction wins only below a threshold. Lesson 7 priced Shor against RSA-2048 at under a million physical qubits. This last lesson fills the space between: what a physical qubit is on the two leading platforms, and one rule of thumb (a noisy machine runs about 1/ε gates) that lets you read a real spec sheet and say what the machine can and can’t do.

The checklist: DiVincenzo’s criteria

In 2000 David DiVincenzo listed what any physical system must provide to compute (DiVincenzo 2000). You already use every item:

  1. Scalable, well-characterized qubits: the |0⟩ and |1⟩ of lesson 1, made physical and copied many times.
  2. Initialization to a state such as |00…0⟩, where every circuit starts. Error correction needs a steady supply of fresh ancillas too.
  3. Coherence much longer than a gate. DiVincenzo asked for 10⁴–10⁵ gate times per coherence time, from the fault-tolerance estimates of his day.
  4. A universal gate set, like {H, T, CNOT}. Hardware has its own native gates, but all single-qubit rotations plus any one entangling two-qubit gate are universal (Brylinski & Brylinski).
  5. Qubit-specific measurement: Born-rule readout of the qubit you choose.

Two more criteria, about converting to photonic “flying” qubits and sending them, matter for networks, not for one computer.

Superconducting qubits: an artificial atom on a chip

An LC circuit made of superconducting metal has no resistance, and quantum mechanically it is a harmonic oscillator: frequency ω = 1/√(LC), levels equally spaced ℏω apart (Krantz et al. §II). That ruins it as a qubit, because a drive tuned to |0⟩→|1⟩ is equally resonant with |1⟩→|2⟩ and the state climbs out of the two-level subspace. A Josephson junction, a thin insulating barrier between superconductors, acts as a nonlinear inductor and squeezes the upper levels together. The resulting transmon has anharmonicity α = f₁₂ − f₀₁ of about −100 to −300 MHz on a 3–6 GHz qubit, so a pulse at f₀₁ moves only the bottom two levels. The qubit is a macroscopic circuit, not a tiny atom.

Gates are the precession of Quantum Mechanics lesson 4 put to work: the splitting ω is the qubit frequency, and a resonant microwave pulse rotates the Bloch vector, a short burst of unitary evolution whose axis is set by the pulse’s phase. Two-qubit gates run through fixed couplers between neighbours: Google’s Willow averages 3.47 neighbours per qubit, IBM’s 127-qubit Eagle at most three. A gate between distant qubits first needs SWAPs, three CNOTs’ worth of extra noise each.

The trap: “colder means no decoherence” Cold removes thermal excitation, not noise. Willow’s mean T1 is still 68 µs (spec sheet), limited by defects in the chip’s materials, quasiparticles far above their equilibrium density, and control noise (Krantz et al. §III).

Trapped ions: identical qubits from nature

A trapped-ion qubit is two internal levels of one charged atom: hyperfine levels split by microwave frequencies (about 8 GHz in ¹³⁷Ba⁺), or an optical transition. Radio-frequency fields hold a string of ions in vacuum, and every ion of a species is identical, so there is no fabrication spread. Lasers or microwaves drive the same resonant Bloch rotations. Entangling gates use the string’s shared vibrations: the Mølmer-Sørensen gate applies state-dependent forces through a common motional mode, a quantum bus, and entangles the internal states while the motion returns to where it started. Its natural form is an XX-type rotation; Helios wraps it in single-qubit pulses to expose the native gate RZZ(θ) = e−iθZZ/2. Any pair can share a mode, so connectivity is all-to-all. Coherence runs from 0.2 s to 600 s depending on qubit type and technique, while two-qubit gates take 1–100 µs (Bruzewicz et al. 2019). Larger machines shuttle ions between zones, the quantum charge-coupled device (QCCD) design.

Ions win on fidelity and connectivity, transmons on speed.

Neutral atoms in laser tweezers trade differently again, scaling to arrays of over 6,100 coherent atomic qubits (2024); photonic and spin qubits are also in development.

NISQ: noisy, intermediate-scale, uncorrected

John Preskill named the current era in 2018: NISQ, Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum. “Intermediate scale” means 50 to a few hundred qubits, past brute-force simulation (lesson 2: 53 qubits are 2⁵³ amplitudes, 144 petabytes at 16 bytes each). “Noisy” means imperfect control and no error correction, so every physical error lands in the answer (Preskill 2018). Run without error correction, both machines above are NISQ devices.

The noise budget: about 1/ε gates

If each two-qubit gate fails independently with probability ε, a circuit of G gates runs fault-free with probability

(1 − ε)^G ≈ e^(−εG) = e^(−λ),   λ = εG

where λ is the expected number of faults, the “circuit fault rate” of Cai et al. At G = 1/ε, λ = 1 and success is 1/e ≈ 0.37; beyond that it falls exponentially. Preskill’s version: at about 0.1% error, don’t expect to run “many more than about 1000 gates”.

What fits in the budget: variational algorithms

A variational algorithm is built to fit: a shallow circuit with tunable angles θ prepares |ψ(θ)⟩, repeated sampling estimates an expectation value such as the energy ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩, and a classical optimizer picks the next θ. For molecular ground states this is the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE); for combinatorial optimization, QAOA (Preskill §6.1). Will they beat classical methods? Preskill: “Nobody knows.” As of 2026 they remain in lesson 8’s unproven-heuristic bin.

Error mitigation is not error correction

Error mitigation leaves the noise in every run and repairs the average afterwards. Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) deliberately amplifies the noise by factors c = 1, 2, 3, measures the expectation value at each, and extrapolates to c = 0; IBM used it in 2023 on 127 qubits and up to 2,880 CNOTs (Kim et al.). Error correction instead encodes redundantly and removes errors during the run.

The answer depends on the noise model you assume. Worse, the shot cost grows exponentially in λ. Even a magic filter that kept only the fault-free runs (a fraction e^(−λ)) would need e^λ times a noiseless machine’s shots: about 150 at λ = 5, about 10⁴ at λ = 9. Real estimators, which must resolve a signal shrinking like e^(−λ), pay e^(βλ) for some β > 0 (Cai et al. §II.D). Quek et al. prove that in the worst case even shallow circuits need superpolynomially many samples. Mitigation stretches the budget; it does not scale.

Reading a “quantum advantage” headline

Ask three questions. What task? Useful, or built to be classically hard? Verified how? Can anyone check the output? Against which classical algorithm? The best known, given time to respond? Preskill’s warning: classical hardware and algorithms keep improving, so the target moves.

  • Google 2019: random circuit sampling on 53 qubits, 200 s against a claimed 10,000 years (Arute et al.), a task chosen to be hard rather than useful. In 2021 a tensor-network method produced a million higher-fidelity samples in 15 hours on 512 GPUs (Pan, Chen & Zhang).
  • IBM 2023: mitigated expectation values on 127 qubits, “beyond brute-force classical computation”. Within weeks a tensor-network simulation beat the quantum data for accuracy (Tindall et al.); brute force was the wrong baseline.
  • Google, October 2025: an expectation value (an out-of-time-order correlator) on 65 of Willow’s qubits in about 2 hours, estimated at 13,000 times longer on the Frontier supercomputer. “Verifiable”: another quantum computer can reproduce it. The authors call their molecular application “not yet beyond classical” (Google Research).
  • Factoring “records”: Gutmann and Neuhaus matched them with a 1981 VIC-20, an abacus and a dog (2025). Many earlier demonstrations used circuits compiled with knowledge of the answer. Running Shor on cloud hardware without the answer built in (though still with circuits specific to each modulus), Bagourd et al. (Dec 2025) saw a clear signal for N = 15 and 21 and only marginal evidence for 35.
Keep this in perspective: where things stand (as of 2026) The real progress is in error correction: Google’s below-threshold surface-code memory (lesson 9), and a Harvard–QuEra neutral-atom processor of up to 280 physical qubits that ran sampling circuits on 48 logical qubits under an error-detecting code (Bluvstein et al.). In 2026 Quantinuum ran computations on Helios with 48 to 94 logical qubits in high-rate error-detecting and distance-4 codes that beat their unencoded versions, with some runs discarded by postselection (arXiv 2602.22211). These are milestones toward the million-qubit machines Shor needs, which nobody has built.

That closes the course. Interference is the mechanism; Grover, phase estimation and Shor choreograph it; BQP bounds what it can buy; noise erodes it and error correction defends it; and today’s hardware allows about a thousand gates of it.

Primary resource

John Preskill, “Quantum Computing in the NISQ era and beyond” (Quantum 2, 79, 2018). The paper that named the era: it defines NISQ, explains the roughly 1000-gate ceiling at 0.1% error, gives the honest “Nobody knows” on VQE and QAOA, separates supremacy from usefulness, and names fault tolerance as the real goal. Short, readable, and still the best map of this lesson.
