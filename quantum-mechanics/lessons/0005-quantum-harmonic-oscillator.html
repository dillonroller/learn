The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator

Lesson 4 called H “the operator corresponding to total energy” but never said which energies are possible, and its only example was a 2×2 matrix. This lesson solves a real, infinite-dimensional system with nothing but operator algebra, and “energy is quantized” stops being a slogan about lumps: it becomes a four-line theorem about one operator’s eigenvalues.

Solving the equation means diagonalizing H

Stationary states (time-independent Schrödinger equation) An eigenvector of the Hamiltonian, H|E⟩ = E|E⟩, is a stationary state: under U(t) = e^(−iHt/ℏ) it only picks up a global phase, U(t)|E⟩ = e^(−iEt/ℏ)|E⟩, so no probability ever changes.

Expand any state in H’s eigenbasis, |ψ⟩ = Σ cₙ|Eₙ⟩, and its evolution is just Σ cₙ e^(−iEₙt/ℏ)|Eₙ⟩. Solving the equation is finding H’s eigenvectors. Measuring energy is the Born rule in that basis: outcome Eₙ with probability |cₙ|², then collapse to |Eₙ⟩, with the same open tension as before (Zwiebach, MIT 8.04 notes 9). So the possible energies are the eigenvalues of H, and “quantized” means “H’s spectrum is discrete”, a property of a particular H, not a law: “a continuous set of possible energies is also allowed” (notes 10), and a free particle on an infinite line can have any energy E ≥ 0.

You have met one already. Lesson 4’s precession example had H = (ℏω/2)Z (Pauli Z), so |0⟩ and |1⟩ were its stationary states, at energies ±ℏω/2, a gap of ℏω. (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 mixes the two energies, so the relative phase e^(iωt) turns: that was the precession. The oscillator has the same ℏω gap, repeated forever.

A particle on a line: the minimal toolkit

Wavefunction ψ(x) = ⟨x|ψ⟩ A qubit has two components, α and β (lesson 1). A particle on a line has one complex component per position x, so the state space is infinite-dimensional and sums become integrals. ψ(x) is the state written in the position basis, not a physical wave and not a smeared-out particle. |ψ(x)|² is a probability density: P(a ≤ x ≤ b) = ∫ₐᵇ |ψ(x)|² dx, and any single exact x has probability 0.
Hermitian operators, x̂ and p̂, and [x̂, p̂] = iℏ Q is Hermitian if Q† = Q. Its eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal; observables are represented by such operators, and measuring Q yields one of its eigenvalues (notes 9). H being Hermitian is what makes U(t) unitary, a fact used there without a name. Position x̂ (multiply by x) and momentum p̂ = −iℏ d/dx are Hermitian and do not commute: 
[x̂, p̂] f = x(−iℏf′) + iℏ(xf)′ = iℏ f
Take [x̂, p̂] = iℏ as the postulate. It is the only quantum input used below.

The oscillator Hamiltonian, and why it is everywhere

H = p̂²/2m + ½ m ω² x̂²

Not a toy: at a minimum x₀ of any smooth potential, V′(x₀) = 0, so Taylor gives V(x) ≈ V(x₀) + ½V″(x₀)(x − x₀)², an oscillator with mω² = V″(x₀) (Zwiebach, notes 13). Hence diatomic bonds like HCl, atoms in a crystal, LC circuits and modes of the electromagnetic field (MIT 5.61, overview). It is the leading approximation near the bottom of a well; the reality check shows where it breaks.

Factoring H: ladder operators

For numbers, u² + v² = (u − iv)(u + iv). Try it with operators, minding the order:

a  = √(mω/2ℏ) (x̂ + ip̂/mω)        a† = √(mω/2ℏ) (x̂ − ip̂/mω)

a†a = (mω/2ℏ)(x̂² + p̂²/m²ω²) + (i/2ℏ)[x̂, p̂]
    = (mω/2ℏ)(x̂² + p̂²/m²ω²) − ½

⇒  H = ℏω(a†a + ½) = ℏω(N + ½)      N = a†a      [a, a†] = 1

The commutator leaves over the −½, which becomes the +½ℏω in every energy below. Order matters: a a† = a†a + 1. a† is the adjoint of a, and a ≠ a†, so neither a nor a† is Hermitian; N = a†a is (Zwiebach, notes 14–15). Two commutators do the rest:

[N, a†] = a†[a, a†] = a†          [N, a] = [a†, a] a = −a

if N|ν⟩ = ν|ν⟩:  N(a†|ν⟩) = (ν + 1) a†|ν⟩       N(a|ν⟩) = (ν − 1) a|ν⟩
lengths:         ‖a†|ν⟩‖² = ⟨ν|a a†|ν⟩ = ν + 1    ‖a|ν⟩‖² = ⟨ν|a†a|ν⟩ = ν
The ladder rules a|n⟩ = √n |n−1⟩ · a†|n⟩ = √(n+1) |n+1⟩ · a|0⟩ = 0 · N|n⟩ = n|n⟩ · H|n⟩ = ℏω(n + ½)|n⟩.
a† (creation) climbs one rung, a (annihilation) descends one; the square roots are the lengths above. n counts quanta of ℏω. It is not a position: every |n⟩ has a spread-out wavefunction.

Quantization, derived rather than assumed

Theorem: N has eigenvalues exactly 0, 1, 2, …, so Eₙ = ℏω(n + ½)
  1. No negatives. For a normalized eigenvector, ν = ⟨ν|N|ν⟩ = ‖a|ν⟩‖² ≥ 0.
  2. Descent. If ν > 0, a|ν⟩ has squared length ν > 0, so it is a genuine eigenvector with eigenvalue ν − 1. Repeat.
  3. Non-integers fail. From ν = 2.5 every step is legal: 2.5 → 1.5 → 0.5 → −0.5, a negative eigenvalue, contradicting step 1.
  4. The only exit. Descent stops only where a|ν₀⟩ = 0, the zero vector; then ν₀ = ‖a|ν₀⟩‖² = 0, so ν was a whole number. Upward never stops: ‖a†|n⟩‖² = n + 1 > 0.

Equal spacing ℏω, starting at ℏω/2, from two inputs: [x̂, p̂] = iℏ and “a squared length is never negative” (MIT 5.73, “killer condition”). No lumps were assumed. The textbook alternative solves a second-order differential equation, where quantization appears because only special E give normalizable solutions (notes 13, Hermite polynomials). Equal spacing is special to this H: hydrogen’s levels go as −1/n².

Zero-point energy and the Gaussian ground state

The lowest energy is E₀ = ℏω/2, not the 0 at the bottom of the well, and the ground state is not a particle sitting still. In position form a|0⟩ = 0 is a first-order equation:

(x + (ℏ/mω) d/dx) ψ₀ = 0    ⇒    ψ₀(x) = (mω/πℏ)^(1/4) e^(−mωx²/2ℏ)

|ψ₀(x)|² = √(mω/πℏ) e^(−mωx²/ℏ)  =  Normal(0, σ²) density, σ² = ℏ/2mω

The ground-state energy “implies that molecules are not completely at rest, even at absolute zero temperature” (HyperPhysics). The ½ℏω is usually explained with the uncertainty principle (not derived here); here it fell out of [a, a†] = 1.

Worked example: ladder arithmetic on a superposition

|ψ⟩ = (|1⟩ + i|3⟩)/√2 Normalized: ½ + ½ = 1 ✓.
Lower: a|ψ⟩ = (√1|0⟩ + i√3|2⟩)/√2, squared length (1 + 3)/2 = 2 = ⟨N⟩, as step 1 says.
Apply H: H|ψ⟩ = ℏω(3/2·|1⟩ + i·7/2·|3⟩)/√2, not a multiple of |ψ⟩: not stationary.
Measure energy: 3ℏω/2 or 7ℏω/2, probability ½ each.
Expectation: ⟨H⟩ = ½·3ℏω/2 + ½·7ℏω/2 = 5ℏω/2 = E₂, an outcome this state gives with probability zero. An expectation need not be possible.
Evolve: the parts gain e^(−3iωt/2) and e^(−7iωt/2); their relative phase e^(−2iωt) turns while the energy odds stay ½.

Reality check: HCl, and why a perfect oscillator is a bad qubit

HCl absorbs infrared light when its bond climbs |0⟩ → |1⟩; the photon supplies the gap ℏω (harmonic dipole transitions obey Δn = ±1, MIT 5.61, not derived here). The H³⁵Cl constants in the NIST WebBook (ωₑ = 2990.946, ωₑxₑ = 52.8186, ωₑyₑ = 0.2243 cm⁻¹) put that gap at 2886 cm⁻¹ ≈ 0.358 eV ≈ 8.65×10¹³ Hz. A perfect oscillator would put 0→2 at 2 × 2886 = 5772 cm⁻¹; the same constants give 5668 cm⁻¹, 1.8% lower. Real bonds are anharmonic, and equal spacing holds only near the bottom of the well (HyperPhysics): an honest leading approximation.

An LC circuit is an oscillator with Eₖ₊₁ − Eₖ = ℏωᵣ for every k, so a pulse that drives 0→1 drives 1→2 just as well and no two levels can be isolated as a qubit. Superconducting qubits add a Josephson junction to make the spacing unequal (Krantz et al. 2019, §II.A), which is Quantum Computing lesson 10’s territory. One level up, each mode of the electromagnetic field is an oscillator, Σₖ ℏωₖ(a†ₖaₖ + ½), with a† read as creating one photon of energy ℏωₖ (SEP, Quantum Field Theory §5, which notes this particle reading is contested). That is Quantum Fields 101.

Misuse check: “free energy from the zero point”

Apply lesson 1’s discipline to a device said to draw power from an oscillator’s zero-point energy: what is the Hamiltonian, what is its spectrum, and is there a lower state to drop into? To give away ΔE the system must end ΔE lower, but the spectrum starts at ℏω/2 and a|0⟩ = 0: no rung below. Physicists answering Scientific American say the same: releasing it “is impossible, because then the molecule would be left with less than the minimum amount” (Paul Deck), and for the vacuum, the state of least energy, “there is no way to extract the vacuum energy” (John Baez). The ½ℏω is real; it is also the floor.

Try it: ladder arithmetic

Use only the ladder rules and Eₙ = ℏω(n + ½). The cards mix operations on purpose, so first decide which rule applies.

Check your understanding

1. What, precisely, does “the oscillator’s energy is quantized” claim?

2. Repeatedly lowering an N-eigenstate would eventually reach a negative eigenvalue. What stops that?

3. Which pair consists of two Hermitian operators, that is, observables?

4. The ground state is ψ₀(x) ∝ e^(−mωx²/2ℏ). What does |ψ₀(x)|² describe?
