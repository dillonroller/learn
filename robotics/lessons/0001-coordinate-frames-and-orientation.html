Position, Orientation, and Why Order Matters

What a quaternion actually is, why the Go2 reports one, and a real bug hiding in plain sight in captured telemetry from a real robot.

A single robot joint only needs one number to describe: q, in radians — "how bent is this one hinge." (You'll see exactly that in Lesson 4, tuning one joint's Kp/Kd.) That's enough for a joint, which only rotates one way. The robot's whole body is different: it can face any direction and tilt any way, all at once. Describing that fully is what this lesson is about.

Pose = position + orientation

A robot's full pose is two things bolted together:

  • Position — where it is: 3 numbers (x, y, z) in some reference frame.
  • Orientation — which way it's facing/tilted, relative to that same frame.

"Relative to some frame" matters: a reference frame is just an agreed-upon origin and set of axes everyone's numbers are measured against. The Go2's IMU reports orientation relative to the world frame (roughly: gravity points straight down, a fixed compass direction is "forward"). A robot with multiple sensors also has a body frame (attached to the robot itself, moving with it) and needs to track how the two relate — this is exactly what ROS2's tf2 system exists to manage, and it's real, standard robotics infrastructure, not something invented for this lesson.

Why orientation needs a quaternion, not just 3 numbers

The obvious way to describe "which way is it facing" is three angles — roll, pitch, yaw — how much it's tilted around each axis. The Go2 actually reports this too, as rpy, right alongside the quaternion. Three numbers, easy to picture. So why bother with four?

Because roll/pitch/yaw (Euler angles) have a well-known failure mode called gimbal lock: at certain orientations (pitch at ±90°), two of the three rotation axes line up on top of each other, and you permanently lose the ability to represent rotation around one of the original directions — not a rounding error, a real loss of a degree of freedom. A quaternion — four numbers, one "scalar" part and a 3-part "vector" — represents the same orientation without that singularity, at the cost of being a little less immediately intuitive to read.

Primary source Don't just take that claim from this lesson — "Quaternions and 3D rotation, explained interactively" (3Blue1Brown & Ben Eater) is the best beginner-accessible, genuinely interactive treatment of this available, and is worth the time before the next lesson. It builds the intuition for what a quaternion's four numbers mean geometrically, which this lesson deliberately keeps light.

Scalar-first vs. scalar-last — the gotcha

Here's the part that isn't a math curiosity — it's a real bug waiting to happen. A unit quaternion is conventionally written (w, x, y, z), where w is the scalar part. But not every library agrees on the order those four numbers are stored in, and nothing about a bare array of 4 floats tells you which convention you're looking at.

LibraryOrder
MuJoCo (what the Go2 sim uses)(w, x, y, z) — scalar first
ROS2 geometry_msgs/Quaternion(x, y, z, w) — scalar last

Per the MuJoCo docs, a rotation by angle a around axis (x,y,z) "corresponds to the quaternion (cos(a/2), sin(a/2)·(x,y,z))" — scalar first, identity written "1 0 0 0". Per the official ROS2 message definition, fields are declared x, y, z, w, identity (0, 0, 0, 1).

The Go2's imu_state.quaternion field comes from a MuJoCo <framequat> sensor, copied straight into the DDS message with no reordering (verified in unitree_mujoco's bridge code, both the Python and C++ versions). So this project's own telemetry is in (w, x, y, z) order — the MuJoCo/Unitree convention, not the ROS one. If you ever pipe this into ROS2 tooling, that reordering has to happen somewhere, explicitly — silently assuming the wrong order doesn't crash anything, it just quietly computes the wrong orientation.

Try it: guess the orientation, then reveal it

Each of these is a real, valid unit quaternion representing a rotation around a single axis. Guess what it represents before revealing — guessing wrong is fine, that's the point.

Same 4 numbers, read the wrong way Steps 4 and 5 above use the exact same four numbers — a real sample from this project's captured telemetry at the end of a stand-up ramp (pct: 1.0): [0.99985, -0.0000213, -0.017562, -0.0000564]. Read correctly as (w,x,y,z), that decodes to a 2.0° tilt — essentially upright, matching this project's own notes that "the robot's IMU orientation was nearly upright" even while the joint tracking failed (that joint-vs-orientation contrast is the whole subject of Lesson 6, on locomotion & balance). Read the same four numbers as (x,y,z,w) instead, and they decode to a 180.0° roll — completely upside-down. Nothing about the array itself would warn you which one you're looking at.
