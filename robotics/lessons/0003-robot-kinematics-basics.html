Joints, Links, and 12 Degrees of Freedom

What "12 joints, 3 per leg" on the Go2 actually means mechanically — zero-background, grounded in the robot's real joint definitions.

Setting up the next lesson The next lesson tunes one joint's Kp/Kd and watches it settle short of a target angle. Before that makes sense, you need the mechanical vocabulary for what a "joint" even is on this robot — this lesson is about what all 12 of them, together, actually are — mechanically — before later lessons get into how they're sensed and coordinated.

Links, joints, and degrees of freedom

A robot's moving body is built from rigid links — solid segments that don't bend — connected by joints, which are exactly the connections that allow one link to move relative to another. Every joint on the Go2 is a revolute joint: it only rotates, around a single fixed axis, like a hinge. (The other common type, a prismatic joint, slides along a line instead of rotating — the Go2 doesn't have any.)

A degree of freedom (DOF) is one independent way something can move. One revolute joint, rotating around one axis, is exactly one rotational DOF — nothing more. A chain of links connected by joints, one after another, is called a kinematic chain. Each Go2 leg is a 3-joint kinematic chain: body → hip joint → thigh link → thigh joint → calf link → calf joint → foot.

Primary source This section compresses "2.2 Degrees of Freedom of a Robot" — Kevin Lynch & Frank Park, Modern Robotics (Northwestern, free), which covers joint types and DOF counting properly (including Grübler's formula, which this lesson doesn't need). Worth the ~15 minutes if you want the fuller version.

Three joints per leg — but not the same kind of "three"

Checking the Go2's real MuJoCo model directly (go2.xml), each leg's three joints are mechanically different, not three copies of the same hinge:

JointRotates aroundWhat it does
*_hip_jointthe body's front-back axisabduction/adduction — swings the whole leg out to the side or back in
*_thigh_jointthe body's left-right axispitch — swings the thigh forward/backward
*_calf_jointthe same left-right axis as thighpitch (the knee) — bends the lower leg

That distinction matters for the diagram below: thigh and calf rotate around the same axis, so they live in one flat plane you can draw in 2D (a side view). Hip rotates around a different axis, so it moves the leg out of that plane entirely — you cannot show all three joints correctly in one flat side-view drawing, which is a common shortcut beginner diagrams get wrong.

12 joints = 4 legs × 3 DOF each. But the robot has 18.

4 legs × 3 revolute joints = 12 actuated degrees of freedom — 12 things you can directly command with a motor. But that's not the robot's whole story. The body itself isn't bolted to anything: it's free to translate in 3 directions and rotate in 3 more, exactly like any rigid object floating in space. By the same DOF counting above, a free rigid body in 3D has 6 more DOF — nobody drives them with a motor; they're a consequence of everything else the legs do.

12 (legs) + 6 (free-floating body) = 18 total DOF, only 12 of which you can command directly. This is exactly the floating-base framing used throughout legged-robot control research — see MIT's Underactuated Robotics course (Russ Tedrake, free), which treats the body's own 6 DOF as an unactuated part of the system the legs have to control indirectly. That's the mechanical reason "standing" is a harder problem than "put each joint at its target angle" — more on that in the locomotion & balance lesson.

Check your understanding

1. A Go2 leg has 3 revolute joints in series: hip, thigh, calf. How many degrees of freedom does that give the leg?

2. The Go2's motor_state array has 12 elements, ordered FR, FL, RR, RL (verified from go2.xml's sensor block). What is motor_state[6]?

3. Besides the 12 actuated leg joints, how many more DOF does the robot's own free-floating body add?
