What Your Sensors Actually Tell You

No sensor hands you ground truth. Every number your code reads is already a best guess, built from something noisier underneath.

Already true in this robot, before any theory Look at the Go2's own MotorState_ struct: it has q and q_raw. Same for dq/dq_raw and ddq/ddq_raw. The firmware itself distinguishes "the raw encoder signal" from "the value we're actually giving you" — because even the simplest sensor on this robot, a joint encoder, doesn't hand your script a clean number straight off the wire. run_stand_mission.py reads motor_state[i].q — the processed value — to check against the stand pose. The real 0.181 rad error from Lesson 4 was a disagreement in that processed reading, not a raw wire glitch.

Three sensors, three different questions answered

The Go2's LowState_ message carries several distinct sensor families. Each answers a narrow question — and only that one:

SensorDirectly measuresCannot tell you
Joint encoderThis one joint's angle (q) — and angle-over-time gives velocity (dq)Anything about the robot's position or tilt in the world
IMU — gyroscopeHow fast the body is currently rotating (angular velocity, rad/s)Absolute orientation directly — you'd have to integrate it, and that drifts (below)
IMU — accelerometerLinear acceleration, including gravity's pullOrientation cleanly by itself — it's jittery, and can't distinguish "tilted" from "accelerating sideways"
Foot-force sensorGround-reaction force at one foot — is it bearing weight right now?Anything about joints or orientation — it only knows contact vs. no contact

Notice none of these, alone, tells you "is the robot standing up correctly?" That's exactly why LowState_ reports both foot_force (raw) and foot_force_est (estimated) — the same raw/estimate split as the joint encoders, this time for contact sensing. Foot-force is what tells a leg controller "this foot is on the ground, bear weight" vs. "this foot is mid-swing" — the exact distinction the next lesson (locomotion & balance) depends on.

Why you can't just trust the gyroscope

The gyroscope is precise moment-to-moment, so it's tempting to get orientation by just adding up ("integrating") its readings over time. The problem: every real gyroscope has a tiny constant bias — a small reading even when it's perfectly still. Add that same small error up every timestep, and the error doesn't stay small — it grows without bound, the way a barely-off compass heading, followed for miles, lands you far from where you meant to go. Analog Devices' "The Case of the Misguided Gyro" walks through exactly this failure mode.

The accelerometer doesn't drift that way — but on its own it's noisy, and it can't tell "tilted 10°" apart from "accelerating sideways," since gravity and acceleration both just show up as a force. Neither sensor alone is trustworthy for orientation. Combining them — sensor fusion — is standard practice specifically because each one covers the other's weak spot: gyroscope for the short term, accelerometer to keep it honest over the long term. The simplest version of this idea is called a complementary filter; more capable (and more complex) versions use a Kalman filter. Neither is covered in depth here — see the primary source below if you want to go further.

Primary source "Estimate Orientation with a Complementary Filter and IMU Data" — MathWorks walks through exactly this gyroscope + accelerometer fusion, at a beginner-appropriate level, from the same trusted documentation family as Lesson 4's PID video.

State estimation, in one sentence

State estimation is the general name for "combine imperfect sensor readings into the best available guess of what's actually true right now." The Go2's fused quaternion orientation (from Lesson 1) and its processed q joint angles are both products of this — neither is raw sensor data, both are estimates. This reframes something important: when the stand-up mission's IMU looked "nearly upright" while the joint check still failed, that wasn't a contradiction — orientation estimation and joint-angle sensing are two separate estimates, each with their own residual error, and they don't have to agree.

Try it: noise vs. delay, the fundamental trade

Below, a "sensor" reads a signal that steps to a new value at t=2s and holds — like a joint suddenly commanded to a new angle. The raw trace is noisy every frame. The filtered trace runs a simple smoothing filter (an exponential moving average): each new estimate nudges partway toward the latest raw reading, controlled by alpha.

  1. Set alpha near 1 (barely any smoothing) and run it. Filtered ≈ raw: fast, but jittery.
  2. Set alpha near 0.05 (heavy smoothing) and run it. Much smoother — but watch how long it takes to catch up after the step.
  3. Try something in between and compare the readout's two numbers.
The trade-off this demo is built to show There's no free setting that gives you both zero noise and zero delay. More smoothing (lower alpha) rejects more noise but reacts slower to genuine changes — a real, well-known limitation of this class of filter (EMA low-pass filtering). Choosing a filter is choosing which failure you'd rather have: a jumpy reading, or a sluggish one.

Practice before next lesson

Open a captured telemetry sample — stand-mission-log-tuned.jsonl — and find one raw/estimated pair yourself (e.g. compare a joint's q across two consecutive samples to its commanded target). Ask: is the gap you see consistent with sensor noise, or with a real tracking lag? That distinction is the whole skill this lesson is building.
