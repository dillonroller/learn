Why Standing Up Is Already Hard

Twelve joints each hitting their target angle is not the same claim as "the robot is stably standing." This lesson is about the gap between those two things — using a real bug this project already hit.

Real data: looked upright, still failed The first captured stand_up run aborted: the rear-right hip settled 0.181 rad short of target (Lesson 4). But its captured IMU quaternion (Lesson 1) told a different story: 
imu_quat = [0.99985, -0.0000213, -0.01756, -0.0000564]  # [w, x, y, z]
w ≈ 0.9998 is nearly the identity quaternion — the torso was only a couple of degrees off level, nearly upright. Two different claims: "the torso orientation looks fine" and "every joint reached its commanded target" can disagree, because run_stand_mission.py's controller only ever checks the second one, per joint, in isolation.

What "stable" actually means

For a robot standing still, the classical test is simple to state: take the robot's center of mass (roughly, the average position of all its mass, torso plus legs) and drop a line straight down to the ground. Draw the support polygon — the shape formed by connecting the feet currently touching the ground. If that dropped point lands inside the support polygon, gravity's pull is balanced by the ground pushing back through the feet, and the robot stays up. If it lands outside, gravity produces a net torque that nothing is countering, and the robot tips.

This is the standard definition used across the legged-robotics literature — see the primary source below for where to read it more rigorously. For a robot standing on four feet, the support polygon is (roughly) the quadrilateral connecting all four foot positions, which is why standing on four legs is comfortably stable and standing on two is not: the polygon is bigger, and it's much harder to push the center of mass outside it by accident.

Why hitting 12 target angles doesn't guarantee this

run_stand_mission.py's controller (Lesson 4) is 12 independent PD-plus-integral loops, one per joint. Each loop only ever asks "is my angle close to my target?" None of them ever compute where the center of mass actually is, or check it against the support polygon. It's entirely possible for every joint to be exactly on target and for the resulting whole-body pose to still be off-balance — or, as the real log shows, for the joints to be off target while the torso still happens to look level. Joint tracking and whole-body balance are different quantities; a controller that only watches one of them can't promise the other.

A real instance of this, from this project's own tuning log When the script's author first added an integral term to fix the steady-state droop, they tried KI=20. Every joint's own PID math was still individually correct — but the result was worse than no integral term at all: error grew to ~0.29 rad and the robot oscillated. Why: the four legs aren't 12 independent systems: they're 12 loops sharing one rigid body standing on the ground. A small correction from one leg shifts how much weight the other three feet carry, which shifts what their error looks like, which they then "correct," which shifts the first leg's load again. Every individual loop was tuned fine in isolation; the emergent, whole-robot behavior still went unstable. That's balance coupling, showing up as a real bug, not a hypothetical. From the script's own tuning-history docstring: 
KI=20, integral active during the ramp too: WORSE than no integral at all
  (error grew to ~0.29 rad) and oscillated. Two bugs compounded: the ramp's
  transient tracking-lag error got integrated as if it were a real
  disturbance ... and 20 is just too high a gain for this coupled,
  four-legs-on-the-ground system.
KI=6, 35s hold (final): verified reproducible over 3 consecutive fresh-sim
  runs: max joint error 0.0280, 0.0281, 0.0281 rad.
The eventual fix (KI=6, resetting the integral when the ramp ends) got the hold phase to converge cleanly — 0.028 rad, reproducible over multiple runs. Worth being precise about what that does and doesn't prove: it shows 12 well-tuned per-joint loops can hold this one specific, symmetric, already-balanced pose. It's still not a balance controller — nothing in it tracks the center of mass or the support polygon. Push the robot sideways, or ask it to reach a pose where the support polygon actually matters, and there's still nothing watching for that.

Why walking is the same problem, continuously

Standing still is a static balance problem: one fixed support polygon (four feet down), hold the center of mass inside it, forever. Walking is the same underlying rule applied while the support polygon itself keeps changing shape — every time a foot lifts off to swing forward, the polygon shrinks to whatever feet are still down, and the center of mass has to already be positioned (or actively moving) to stay inside that smaller, temporary shape before the foot lands again. This is exactly the sequencing this project's own Unitree learning-path notes treat as a separate, later milestone from "get a robot standing" — not a matter of degree, a qualitatively different control problem: static balance at one pose vs. continuous balance across a moving sequence of poses.

A common first model for that continuous version treats the whole robot as a single point mass on a massless pendulum at constant height — the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM) — because it makes the center-of-mass trajectory solvable in closed form, which is exactly what you need to plan where the CoM must be, moment to moment, as the support polygon keeps changing under it. See the primary source below if you want to see it used in real gait-planning research; this lesson only needs you to know it exists and why.

Primary resource

"Ch. 5 — Highly-Articulated Legged Robots," MIT's Underactuated Robotics (Russ Tedrake, freely available) is the rigorous version of the section above — it frames the same "keep something inside the convex hull of ground contacts" rule in terms of the center of pressure/zero-moment point rather than plain center of mass, which is the more general form real controllers use once the robot is moving (not just standing still). For a standing, motionless robot the two framings agree. For LIPM specifically: Li, Xie, Luo & Li, "Trajectory Planning of Flexible Walking for Biped Robots Using LIPM and LPM," Sensors 2021 (open access).
