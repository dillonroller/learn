The Sim-to-Real Gap

Why everything simulated so far in this topic — PD gains, joint frames, sensor readings, balance — is a rehearsal, not a performance. And why the source robotics project this whole curriculum is grounded in exists specifically to answer the question that leaves open.

This project's stack already hit this gap — before any robot physics were involved The source project's Unitree setup notes record that the official Unitree stack "targets Ubuntu 20.04/22.04" and needed real patches to run on macOS: the SDK's RecurrentThread called the Linux timerfd_create syscall directly, which "doesn't exist on macOS/BSD — this is a hard crash, not a warning," and had to be patched to fall back to a time.sleep-based loop. Separately, macOS's loopback interface is lo0, not Linux's lo — another silent assumption baked into the stack. Nothing about robot dynamics was involved yet, and the environment still didn't match what the software assumed. That's the sim-to-real gap in miniature: software built and tested against one set of assumptions, run against a different reality.

1. What a simulator actually is

unitree_mujoco runs on MuJoCo, a physics engine: a numerical approximation of rigid-body motion, contact, and friction. "Approximation" is doing real work in that sentence — MuJoCo doesn't simulate the actual atoms of a real Go2's rubber feet gripping actual concrete. It simulates a contact model someone wrote down: a function that's cheap to compute and close enough for its purpose, not a perfect replica of any one physical object. The same goes for its actuator model (how commanded torque becomes motion) and its sensor model (how "true" simulated state becomes a noisy-ish reading). Every one of those is a simplification, and every simplification is a place where a real Go2 can behave differently than its simulated twin.

The gap between "works when simulated" and "works on the physical robot" has a name: the sim-to-real gap. It's studied most rigorously in robot learning, where a controller is trained entirely in simulation and then has to survive contact with an actual robot:

Primary source Zhao, Peña Queralta & Westerlund, "Sim-to-Real Transfer in Deep Reinforcement Learning for Robotics: a Survey" (arXiv:2009.13303) — opens by naming exactly this problem: "the gap between the simulated and real worlds degrades the performance of the policies once the models are transferred into real robots." The stand_up mission's controller is hand-tuned PID (PD plus an added integral feedforward term — see Lesson 4), not a learned policy, so most of the survey's transfer techniques (domain randomization, meta-learning) don't directly apply — but the underlying gap it's naming is the exact same one, and its framing of why simulators diverge from reality is worth reading even for a classical controller.

2. Concrete ways a real Go2 can diverge from its MuJoCo twin

CategoryWhat differs in reality
Friction & contactReal foot/ground friction depends on surface, wear, moisture — MuJoCo uses one contact model with parameters someone chose
Actuator dynamicsReal motors have torque/rate limits, response delay, and heat buildup under sustained load; MuJoCo's actuator model is idealized
Sensor noiseReal IMU/encoder noise has its own statistics (bias drift, vibration coupling) that a simulated sensor may not reproduce — see Lesson 5
LatencyReal network/DDS and control-loop timing jitter that a local sim doesn't have to contend with
PowerBattery voltage sags under load in ways that change available torque over a mission — not modeled at all here
Mechanical wearCable drag, gearbox backlash, joint play — accumulate on a real robot and never exist in sim

None of these are exotic. They're the ordinary difference between a model of a thing and the thing. Every earlier lesson in this topic has quietly assumed simulation is close enough — this lesson is where that assumption gets named and questioned.

3. Every earlier lesson has a sim-to-real angle

  • Lesson 4 (PD control) — Kp=60, Kd=5 converging in MuJoCo doesn't guarantee the same gains won't ring, overheat, or feel dangerously stiff on a real Go2's actual actuators and load.
  • Lesson 1 (frames/orientation) — the IMU's scalar-first quaternion convention is a software/firmware fact, not a physics one, but it's exactly the kind of assumption that silently breaks when a real sensor driver differs from the simulated bridge's.
  • Lesson 2 (DDS/ROS2) — a loopback DDS domain on one machine has none of the latency, packet loss, or multi-machine clock drift a real deployed robot's network will.
  • Lesson 3 (kinematics) — MuJoCo's joint limits and link masses come from the model file, which may not match the specific real unit's manufacturing tolerances.
  • Lesson 5 (sensors) — simulated sensor noise is a choice made by whoever wrote the MuJoCo model, not a measurement of the real IMU/encoders/foot-force sensors.
  • Lesson 6 (locomotion/balance) — a balance margin that looks comfortable against MuJoCo's flat-ground friction model may not exist at all on a real, uneven, or slippery site.

4. This project's actual answer: verification, not vibes

The source robotics project isn't trying to make simulation perfect — it's trying to make the gap visible and evidenced. That's Layer 5 and Layer 6 of its architecture doc, word for word:

Layer 5 — Simulation and test planning: "For each mission, define what can be validated in simulation vs. what requires hardware, a real site, or a controlled field trial... How do we prove readiness before trusting the robot in production?"
Layer 6 — Feasibility report: feasibility ratings range from "Ready with existing robot" down to "Requires significant R&D," and the report always includes a "verification plan."

And its telemetry-contract doc names exactly why this matters, again word for word:

"This is the layer that lets a feasibility report cite evidence instead of assertion: not 'the robot can probably do this' but 'in N simulated runs and M field trials, these specific events fired in these specific sequences, and here's what failed and how it recovered.'"

"Verification" in this project doesn't mean "ran once in MuJoCo and it looked fine." It means a staged evidence trail — simulation → controlled real-hardware trial → field trial — where each stage answers a narrower, harder question than the last, and a feasibility rating is only as strong as the weakest stage it's actually been run through. The project's concept doc frames the whole system's job as answering "can this robot credibly do this job, and what would prove it?" — the "what would prove it" is the part simulation alone can never fully answer.

This project already lived a smaller version of this lesson run_stand_mission.py's own docstring records the actual tuning history: an integral gain of KI=9 "converged cleanly on the first run (0.042 rad) — but a second live run under the identical config oscillated and never converged (0.11 rad)." One successful run wasn't evidence the controller worked — it was sitting at the edge of stability, and ordinary run-to-run noise (timing jitter, non-deterministic contact physics) tipped it either way. The fix that shipped, KI=6, was verified "reproducible over 3 consecutive fresh-sim runs" before being trusted. That's the same principle this lesson is teaching, one level down: even within simulation, a single clean run is not proof — reproducibility across repeated runs is the minimum bar, and it's still a lower bar than a real robot.

Sim-verifiable, or needs real-world testing?

2. The real Go2's battery lasts a full 8-hour patrol shift.

3. Retuned Kp/Kd gains won't overheat or damage the real actuators under sustained standing load.

4. The robot can greet attendees without blocking an exit (a human-interaction example from the source project's own worked example).
